ICU and You  ·  The Jukebox  ·  Number 2

The reminiscence bump

Five songs for the people now between thirty and forty-five — and why the ones that arrived at eighteen never left

I mentioned the Beatles in the unit the other day and got a polite blank. Not disagreement. Not even curiosity. Just the particular silence of a room in which a reference has failed to land.

I am old enough for that to be funny rather than wounding. But it did set me wondering why I still have them and my registrars do not, and it turns out there is a name for it, and the name is a good one.

The reminiscence bump.

The music you meet between about fourteen and twenty-four — peaking somewhere around eighteen — is the music that stays. Not the best music. Not the music you would choose now, with the benefit of taste. The music that happened to be playing while you were becoming whoever you turned out to be. It fixes, and it does not come unfixed.

Which is why I can still find my way through a Beatles record I have not heard in a decade, and why a thirty-eight-year-old registrar will do exactly the same thing with something I have never knowingly heard in my life.

About this column

The Jukebox takes songs that wander into this territory — illness, a hospital, a resuscitation, a transplant, a death, a recovery — and tells you what is actually behind them. No lyrics are reproduced here. What gets reproduced is the story, and the story is usually stranger than the song.

The first of these was for the young adult ward. This one belongs to the age band we have just spent a fortnight on.

Whose songs these are

Anybody who is thirty to forty-five in November 2026 was born between late 1981 and late 1996. That is the Millennial band, almost to the month. Not Generation Z — Z is the one underneath, eighteen to twenty-nine now, and their turn comes when the ladder swings back round to the young adult. It will, and I will do the Z version then. That is one of the better things about a site arranged this way. It goes around, and it comes around.

So for this group, eighteen falls somewhere between 1999 and 2014. Three of the five below sit inside that window, and the two that do not have earned their place another way.

Reflect, and deflect

Two sorts of song end up mattering in a hospital, and we tend only to talk about one of them.

There are the songs that reflect the thing — that say back to you what is happening, and let you hear it from somewhere outside your own head, which is the only place it is bearable from. Those are the funeral songs, the ones on the ward playlist, the one somebody puts on in the car on the way home.

And there are the songs that deflect it. No help at all with the facts. Entirely the wrong mood. And exactly what is needed, because for three and a half minutes you are somewhere else. That second kind is badly underrated by people who have never had to sit in a relatives' room. There is one on this list on purpose, and it is last.

Listen along. All five, in order, plus the three that nearly made it, are on one playlist. About half an hour.

One · What we do

Fix You

Coldplay · X&Y, 6 June 2005; single 5 September 2005 · Berryman, Buckland, Champion and Martin

Fix you is what we do. It is the verb written on the door, whether or not it is the verb we can deliver on any given night.

Chris Martin wrote it for Gwyneth Paltrow after her father died; he had met her a few weeks after the death. Here is the part I did not know. Bruce Paltrow had bought a large keyboard shortly before he died and nobody had ever plugged it in. Martin did. The sound that opens the song — the one everybody waits through the quiet for — came out of a dead man's instrument, the first time it was ever switched on.

Martin has said it is the Coldplay song he is proudest of, and that it almost single-handedly got the band through two very difficult years. It has since been sung at more funerals than almost anything else written this century, which is an odd fate for a song whose title is a promise.

Lights will guide you home. We cannot promise that either. We say it anyway.

Two · The light

Light Surrounding You

Evermore · single, 14 October 2006, from Real Life · written by Dann Hume · ARIA number 1, platinum

A patient of mine died in her early thirties. They played this at her funeral, and it seemed to say what everyone in that room needed to hear.

Here is what I did not know until I went looking. Dann Hume, the band's drummer, wrote it at a piano, and he has said it is about somebody with great potential who did not believe in themselves. It is not about death. It is not about loss. It is a song about belief, written for the living, by a young man in his twenties.

It became Australia's goodbye song more or less by accident. Home and Away used it to farewell Sally Fletcher in the 2006 finale, the country reached for it, and has gone on reaching ever since. Number one on the ARIA chart, platinum, and the first New Zealand act to top the Australian chart in a decade.

So the people at that funeral heard something the writer never put in it. I do not think that is a flaw in them, or in him. I think it is the whole mechanism. A song stops belonging to the person who wrote it at about the second time somebody else needs it.

And I will say the unscientific part out loud, since this is my column. I do sometimes feel there is a light surrounding us in that room. Because there needs to be.

Three · The heart that kept going

Someone You Loved

Lewis Capaldi · single, 8 November 2018, from Divinely Uninspired to a Hellish Extent · video released February 2019

We cannot ignore donation on a page about this age band, and we should not want to.

Lewis Capaldi wanted the video to say something about organ donation, and wondered aloud whether his second cousin once removed might be in it. His manager sent an email. Peter Capaldi replied the next day and said yes.

He plays a widower. His wife is dying, in flashback, and he is nursing her. At the end he visits the family of the woman who received her heart, and he listens to it. The video was made with Live Life Give Life and Save9Lives. A million views in two days, and roughly a hundred thousand a day after that.

I have been in that conversation a great many times, from the other side of it. What the video gets right — and almost nothing else about donation gets right — is that the two families are strangers who share precisely one thing and nothing else at all, and that the meeting, in the rare cases where it happens, is not a resolution. It is two people in a room, and a sound.

The power to make any of it possible sits almost entirely with people who will never set foot in an intensive care unit. It takes a few minutes. It needs a Medicare card. It does not need you to be healthy, or young, or certain. It happens at the Australian Organ Donor Register, and anyone in Australia aged sixteen or over can do it.

Four · The try

I Try

Macy Gray · 1999, from On How Life Is · Gray, Ruzumna, Lim and Wilder · ARIA number 1 · Grammy, Best Female Pop Vocal, 2001

We came into this millennium on an upstroke. Hope, enthusiasm, a general sense that a good many things were about to get better. Some of that stayed. A good deal of it did not.

But look at what was sitting on top of the Australian chart as the century turned. A song called I Try.

The voice was the problem. Everybody told her so, at length, for years. It is the reason she nearly stopped, and it is also the only reason anybody alive can identify the record inside half a bar. The thing she was told to fix was the thing.

My late father's favourite saying to me, growing up, was that all doors are open to those who try. I have never forgotten it. I have also never entirely proved it, and I suspect he knew that perfectly well when he said it to me.

But it is what we are left holding. At the end of a long night, when the arithmetic has not worked and the family is standing in the corridor, the only thing you can honestly put down on the other side of the ledger is that you tried. Twenty-seven years ago Macy Gray opened all this with the sound of a woman trying, in a voice the whole industry had told her to get rid of. We are all still at it.

Five · The best thing you can do

Put Your Records On

Corinne Bailey Rae · single, February 2006, from Corinne Bailey Rae · Bailey Rae, Chrisette Michele and John Beck

I loved the tune and the beat for years before I knew the first thing about the rest of it. And I have to correct something I believed until very recently, because the correction turns out to matter more than the error did.

The song is not about an overdose.

Corinne Bailey Rae released it in February 2006, from her first album. It is an uncomplicated piece of advice to a young woman: let your hair down, put on the music you love, you will be all right. She has described it as coming out of feeling on the margins as a mixed-race child in Leeds, and out of learning to accept her own voice — which, you will notice, is the second time that has come up on this page.

Two years later, in March 2008, her husband Jason Rae was found dead in a flat in Leeds. He was thirty-one. A saxophonist who had played with Amy Winehouse and Mark Ronson and toured with the Haggis Horns. The coroner recorded death by misadventure: an accidental overdose of methadone and alcohol.

The grief did not go into this song. It could not have. It went into the next record, The Sea, which took her two years to make and sounds nothing like this one.

So the order runs the other way from the way I had it in my head, and the song is cleaner than I thought it was. Which leaves the advice standing, unqualified, and I think it is good advice.

There are moments in this work where there is genuinely nothing useful left to do. Not for the patient, not for the family, and not for you. The correct action at that point is to go home and put your records on. Not to process it. Not to debrief it. To be somewhere else for forty minutes, with something you love, in a room where nothing is beeping.

That is a deflecting song, and it is on this list on purpose.

And three that nearly made it

Lose Yourself — Eminem, 2002. Written in snatches on the 8 Mile set between takes, and the first hip-hop record to win the Academy Award for Best Original Song, which he did not attend. If you want the reminiscence bump for this band in its purest form, this is it. One shot, one opportunity — which is a fair description of an arrest.

Keep Breathing — Ingrid Michaelson, 2007. Two chords and one repeated line, which is precisely the point. It is what you do when there is nothing else left to do.

Unwell — Matchbox Twenty, 2003. Not being able to say out loud that you are coming apart, while everybody around you appears to be fine. More the relatives' song than the patient's, and none the worse for it.

The list is not closed

This one stays open. Send me what belongs on it and I will add to it periodically — the page and the playlist both. The column only works if other people fill it. It exists at all because a reader wrote in to ask whether there was a song called When ICU.

My registrars do not know the Beatles, and I have stopped minding. They have their own bump, sitting somewhere around 2007, and it is every bit as fixed as mine. In twenty years one of them will mention Macy Gray to a resident and get the polite blank, and will finally understand what the expression on my face was.

Which brings me to the thing I actually want from you.

Over to you

If you are between thirty and forty-five — what still plays in your head?

Not your favourite song. Not the one you would defend in an argument. The one that arrives whenever you think about being eighteen, nineteen, twenty-one. The one attached to a bedroom, or a car, or a share house, or somebody you are no longer in touch with. Everybody has one, and almost nobody chose it.

Tell me what it is. I am genuinely interested, and I will say why: if the reminiscence bump is real, then the songs this age group names are the songs that will be in the room when they are the patient, or the daughter, or the one making the decision. That is worth knowing.

And send me songs for the column generally — the story behind it if you know one, because the story is the part I want. Anything touching illness, a hospital, a resuscitation, a transplant, a death or a recovery, in any genre and any language. Particularly the ones where the received explanation turns out to be wrong. I have just had to correct one of my own.
