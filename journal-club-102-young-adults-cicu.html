ICU and You  ·  Journal Club  ·  Number 102

Thirty-two, in a coronary care unit

Twenty-nine thousand admissions, and the discovery that the young heart in trouble is usually not a heart attack

Isath A, Sharma T, Mahmood U, Smith D, Berg DD, Bohula EA, et al. Young adults in the cardiac intensive care unit: insights from the Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network registry. European Heart Journal: Acute Cardiovascular Care 2026;15:403–407.

Type  Brief report — observational multicentre registry Where  41 advanced cardiac ICUs, North America (CCCTN) When  Annual two-month collection periods, 2018 to 2023 Size  29,035 admissions; 1,959 aged 18 to 39
Read the paper

Not open access — all rights reserved, so you will need your hospital library. Five pages, one table and two figures, which for once is the right length for what it has to say. Figure 1 is the paper: seven bars, and the two that cross over tell you most of it.

Why bother with the journals at all

Because they are the news. Not the formal, ceremonial version of reading — the daily kind. The journals are our news pages, our opinion columns, our form guide and our sports results. People who would not dream of going a week without knowing what happened in the world will go a year without knowing what happened in their own specialty, and then wonder why the registrar knows something they do not.

It does not have to be a session. You do not need an hour, a highlighter or a plan. Read the abstracts. Skim the contents page of one journal while the kettle boils. Open the one paper in ten that looks interesting and abandon it at paragraph three if it is not. Nobody is marking this.

What matters is staying curious rather than staying current — nobody is current, and the people who claim to be are reading less than they say. What you are actually maintaining is the habit of noticing: that a question is open, that a practice has shifted, that somebody has published the thing you were arguing about last month.

And then do the part that makes it stick. Mention it on a ward round. Argue about it with somebody who disagrees. Ask what the study could not have shown. A paper read alone is half a paper.

Publish or perish is the line we all know. The quieter one is this: absorb it, or lose your edge.

The question

Everything written about the coronary care unit for twenty years has been about it getting older. The median age in this registry is sixty-seven. So what is the other end of the distribution doing — and is the thirty-two-year-old in bed four simply a younger version of the sixty-eight-year-old in bed five?

She is not. That is the paper.

What they did

The Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network is a registry of advanced cardiac intensive care units in North America, co-ordinated by the TIMI Study Group in Boston. Each participating unit submits every consecutive admission during an annual collection period of at least two months. The authors took six years of that, split it at forty, and compared.

One housekeeping note before anything else, because it matters for this fortnight. The paper’s “young adults” are 18 to 39, which straddles two of our own bands. But the median is thirty-two, with an interquartile range of 27 to 36 — so in practice this cohort sits almost exactly on early middle age. The comparator is everyone aged forty and over, which is a problem we will come back to.

Who they are

Aged 18–39  ·  1,959

Hypertension in 23.5%, diabetes 11.1%, coronary artery disease 5.6%. But congenital heart disease in 11.4% and a previous heart transplant in 3.8%. Female 40.0%, non-White 55.4%.

Aged 40 and over  ·  27,075

Hypertension 66.3%, diabetes 36.1%, coronary artery disease 37.6%. Congenital heart disease 1.2%, prior transplant 1.2%. Female 36.4%, non-White 43.5%.

The same in both

Lactate on arrival — a median of 1.8 in each group. SOFA score — 3.0 in each. By the two measures we use to say how sick somebody is, these patients arrive equally sick.

Read the first column again. Almost none of the furniture of cardiovascular risk, and a tenfold excess of congenital heart disease. A young adult in a coronary care unit is not an old patient who arrived early. Mostly they are somebody with a heart that was different from the beginning, or that failed for a reason that has nothing to do with plaque.

Two quieter findings in the same table are worth lifting out. Of the young adults with chronic kidney disease, 43.2% already had end-stage renal disease, against 20.8% of the older ones — so young kidney disease here is further advanced, not milder. And of the young adults with a history of heart failure, 73.2% had an ejection fraction under 40%, against 60.1%. Fewer of them have heart failure. The ones who do have worse ventricles.

Two bars cross over and the rest barely move. Heart failure is the leading admission diagnosis in young adults — one in four — and acute coronary syndrome is less than half as common as it is in everybody else. Valvular disease is identical in the two groups, which is quietly interesting in itself, and arrhythmia nearly so.

If your mental image of a young person in a coronary care unit is a heart attack, the data say you have the wrong picture about seven times in eight. The authors point to the rising burden of heart failure in young adults, and to a heart failure phenotype in this group that is substantively different from the older one — myocarditis and the genetic cardiomyopathies rather than ischaemic damage.

The same story this fortnight is telling from the other end

This sits squarely beside Podcast 61, where a cardiologist argues that the young heart attack is a different disease with opposite genetics. Here is the registry version: the young are in the unit far less often for a heart attack at all.

And a detail that completes it — when young adults do have an acute coronary syndrome, they get angiography or PCI at the same rate as anybody else (88.7% against 87.1%, with a p-value of 0.47). The gap is not in how we treat their infarcts. It is that they are not mostly having infarcts.

And they are sicker when they get there

Despite the thin risk-factor profile, the young arrive in more trouble. Cardiogenic shock in 22.4% against 18.7%. Cardiac arrest in 12.7% against 10.6%. The commonest single indication for admission is the same in both groups and is not cardiac at all — respiratory insufficiency, about a quarter of each.

What is done for them differs more than that. Mechanical ventilation 26% against 23%. Right heart catheterisation 21% against 16%, and among those admitted with shock or heart failure, 50.1% against 38.3%. Mechanical circulatory support 13% against 11%. Coronary angiography, going the other way, 19% against 38%.

But the striking one is which support:

Nearly a third of supported young adults go on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, against one in ten of the older. That is the single most operationally useful number in the paper. It tells you what a young adult in cardiogenic shock is likely to need, and therefore which conversation needs to start early, and with which hospital.

The headline, and the trap underneath it

Intensive care mortality was 6.5% in the young against 10.5%. Hospital mortality 9.5% against 14.4%. Both differences are highly significant. Length of stay was longer in the young — a median of 2.7 days against 2.2.

So: sicker on arrival, treated harder, stayed longer, died substantially less.

Before you repeat the mortality figures

These comparisons are unadjusted. The paper reports crude proportions. A thirty-two-year-old survives critical illness better than a sixty-eight-year-old, and no reader needed a registry to learn that. Taken at face value the finding is close to a tautology.

What rescues it is the third column of the table above. SOFA and lactate were identical in the two groups — so by the usual measures these patients arrived equally deranged, and the young still died at two thirds the rate. That is a real observation. It is also resting on a single median SOFA score of 3 in each group, which is a blunt instrument that hides a great deal.

And the comparator is everybody aged forty and over. A forty-one-year-old and a ninety-five-year-old sit in the same column. The authors concede the group is heterogeneous. Any mortality difference across a boundary like that is partly just the shape of the age distribution on the other side of it.

The uncomfortable reading the authors raise themselves

Two explanations, and only one of them is cheerful

Young patients received more of everything. The authors offer the obvious reading — an appropriately more aggressive approach in people with more to gain — and then, to their credit, the other one. This may be a risk–treatment paradox: older adults, at greater risk of a poor outcome and therefore with more absolute benefit to gain, being treated less aggressively precisely because they are old.

If that is what is happening, then part of the mortality gap in this paper is not biology. It is us. The paper cannot distinguish the two, and it does not pretend to.

The finding nobody should skip

The young cohort was more female (40.0% against 36.4%) and considerably more likely to be non-White (55.4% against 43.5%). The authors do not let that sit as a demographic curiosity. They name what they think it reflects: earlier onset of cardiovascular risk factors, and structural inequity — limited access to specialty care — compounding vulnerability in those populations.

In other words, the people arriving in a coronary care unit decades before they should be are disproportionately women and people of colour. Whatever is driving the young end of this distribution is not distributed evenly, and a registry of forty-one advanced North American units has just said so in print.

What to take to the bedside

  • Expect heart failure, not infarctionOne in four young admissions is heart failure and barely one in nine is an acute coronary syndrome. In a young adult with a failing heart, think myocarditis, genetic cardiomyopathy and congenital disease before you think plaque.
  • Ask about the childhood heartCongenital heart disease in 11.4%, prior transplant in 3.8% — roughly tenfold and threefold the older rates. Somebody has been following this patient for twenty years and holds the operative history you need.
  • Think ECMO early, and think about transferNearly a third of young adults needing mechanical support get ECMO, against a tenth of older ones. In a unit without it, that is a phone call that should happen sooner than instinct suggests.
  • Low risk-factor burden is not reassuranceThese patients had less hypertension, less diabetes and a seventh of the coronary disease — and arrived with more shock and more cardiac arrest. A clean risk profile in a young person tells you about the mechanism, not the severity.
  • The commonest reason to be there is breathingRespiratory insufficiency was the leading indication for admission in both groups, about a quarter of each. The coronary care unit is a respiratory ward with a cardiology badge, at every age.

What to read sceptically

  • Unadjusted mortality across a forty-year age gapThe central comparison has no multivariable adjustment reported. Identical SOFA and lactate make it more interesting than a tautology, but not much more than that.
  • “Forty and over” is not a comparatorIt is five decades of patients in one column, and the authors say so. Nothing here tells you how a thirty-eight-year-old differs from a forty-five-year-old, which for our purposes is the interesting boundary.
  • Two months a year, not six yearsCollection periods are annual snapshots of at least two months, not continuous enrolment. Whatever is seasonal is sampled unevenly.
  • Forty-one advanced North American unitsPredominantly tertiary and academic centres with ECMO on site — which is precisely the thing being measured. The ECMO finding in particular cannot be read straight across to a regional unit, or to Australia.
  • Readmissions are invisibleThe registry captures anonymised admissions, so a young patient readmitted three times appears three times. The authors note readmission may well be commoner in the young, which would inflate their apparent numbers and dilute their apparent mortality.
  • And it stops at the hospital doorNo quality of life, no long-term outcome — the limitation the authors themselves call out as mattering most “in a younger population with extended post-critical illness survival”. A thirty-two-year-old who survives has fifty years of whatever this did to her, and this paper ends on day five.

That last gap is the one I would spend the journal club on. The paper’s conclusion is that young adults do better, and within its own four walls that is correct. But survival to discharge is the outcome that suits a registry, not the outcome that suits the patient. We have built a unit that is good at getting a thirty-two-year-old out of the building alive and we have almost no idea what we hand back to her. The authors say as much, in a sentence near the end that is easy to read past — care models designed for this population, and survivorship programmes tailored to it, are what the data are actually asking for.

Which makes this a good paper to argue with rather than a good paper to quote. It describes something real and under-described, it is honest about what it cannot see, and the most important thing in it is the question it leaves open.

Challenge for you

What happens to your thirty-five-year-old in cardiogenic shock tonight?

Not the guideline answer. The real one. Does the conversation about extracorporeal support happen at the bedside, on the phone, or not at all — and how far away is the nearest unit that could take her?

I am interested in the timing above everything. How many hours from arrival to somebody saying the word ECMO out loud? And if your unit does not retrieve for it, do you know that before you need to know it, or do you find out on the night? Replies from regional and rural units particularly welcome; this is a paper written entirely in tertiary academic centres with the machine down the corridor, and most of us do not work there.
