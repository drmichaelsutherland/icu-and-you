ICU and You  ·  Medical Students' Place  ·  Number 6

The young adult

Eighteen to thirty — which, for most of you reading this, is your own age band

Find your level: Early years  ·  Clinical years  ·  Final years  ·  And one on you

Hey guys — this one is most of you. Every other set in this series has been about somebody younger than the person reading it. This is the first where the patient and the reader are the same age, and that turns out to change what is difficult about it.

The tiers map roughly onto the three phases of a six-year medical programme — the science, then the clinical reasoning, then what you will actually be expected to do with your own hands. Read the one that fits, and glance at the one above it.

What is difficult here is not the physiology. Airway, doses and circulation have been adult for years, and capacity is presumed rather than negotiated. The difficulty is that this age group holds its numbers long after the loss is serious, and that almost everything bringing them in is something that happened rather than something that grew. Both of those are traps for a student, and for the rest of us.

There is a fourth section, at the end, which is not about patients at all. The mortality data in tier two is your own, and nothing about being a medical student takes you out of it. It is short, and you can skip it.

Tier one

The most reserve you will ever treat

Phase 1 — the foundational years, and anything on shock, compensation and the physiology of pregnancy

  • Where the reserve actually sitsNot mainly in the heart. A young adult defends arterial pressure by constricting the splanchnic and cutaneous beds and recruiting the blood parked in them, so the circulation is topped up from inside before anybody gives anything. That is why the blood pressure in front of you describes the vasoconstriction rather than the volume.
  • Why the curve has a cliff in itCompensation is a flat line that ends, not a slope that warns you. Each further loss is met by the same mechanisms until those are maximal, and then there is nothing left to recruit. The shape matters more than the numbers: there is no gentle middle segment in which to notice.
  • Narrowing comes before fallingResistance rises before output fails, so the diastolic climbs while the systolic is still held and the pulse pressure quietly narrows. A pressure of 125/95 in a twenty-two-year-old is a more frightening set of numbers than 100/60 in someone of seventy, and no monitor will flag either.
  • Tachycardia, and the six good reasons you will dismiss itOften the only variable that has moved, and in this age group the easiest to explain away: pain, the drive in, the salbutamol, the three coffees, the fact that they are frightened of you, and the fact that they are twenty-two. Every explanation is plausible. Together they will cost you an hour.
  • You cannot read a rate without a baselineResting rates in the forties are normal in trained young people, so a rate of 90 can be a tachycardia of fifty beats. The only sources for the baseline are the patient and whoever came in with them, and neither will be there later.
  • A second normal: pregnancyCardiac output rises by a third to a half and plasma volume by about half. Functional residual capacity falls by a fifth while oxygen consumption rises by a fifth, which is why she desaturates fast. And uteroplacental flow is sacrificed first, so the fetus declares the shock before the mother does.
  • Which makes forty the wrong sort of normalHer arterial gas starts somewhere else: a PaCO2 around 30–32 mmHg (roughly 4 kPa) and a bicarbonate of 18–21 mmol/L. So a PaCO2 of 40 in a breathless pregnant woman is not reassurance — it is a woman who has stopped being able to hyperventilate — and it is the one figure every system you use will report as normal.
  • Creatinine falls in pregnancy tooWhich is the same trap wearing different clothes. A postpartum creatinine sitting inside the quoted reference range can represent a substantial loss of function, and the laboratory will not mark it. The estimating equations are not validated in pregnancy either, so the eGFR beside it is decoration.
  • They may not be new patientsMedian survival in cystic fibrosis is now around fifty-four years, median life expectancy in Duchenne is past twenty-four and rising, and five-year survival for cancer at fifteen to twenty-four is about ninety per cent. A generation that used to be paediatric now arrives in adult units carrying twenty years of history that somebody else is holding.
  • The Fontan circulation, grown upFlow through the lungs is passive, so it is preload-dependent and it hates positive pressure. Anything that raises pulmonary impedance — PEEP, hypoxia, acidosis, an embolus — produces shock rather than hypoxia. Find out the baseline saturation before you set a target for it.

If you take one thing: in this age group the blood pressure is the last number to move and the first one you will be shown. Everything in tier one exists to stop you believing it.

Likely to be examined as: a short answer on the cardiovascular and respiratory changes of pregnancy and how they alter the arterial blood gas, or on the physiological basis of compensated shock. A good viva opener is a fit twenty-two-year-old off a motorbike, heart rate 120, blood pressure 128/96 — what is that blood pressure telling you?

Tier two

What actually brings them in, and the history you take alone

Phase 2 — the clinical years, integrated clinical studies, and the ICE

  • Something that happened, not something that grewExternal causes lead every Australian age band up to forty-four. Road, work, water, assault, sport. For a clinical student the consequence is structural: the history is an event rather than a progression, and the people who can describe the event are frequently not the patient.
  • Look for what you cannot seeA young chest wall transmits force without breaking, so the ribs are intact over the contused lung and the pelvis springs back over the torn vessel. Mechanism outranks examination here more than at any other age, and the mechanism is a history question.
  • Alcohol is in the history more often than the chartIt changes the examination, the consent and the airway, and it is the most reliably under-recorded item in this age group. Ask it the way you would ask about a medication — plainly, with a number, and without making a face.
  • What they tookDeliberate and recreational, and often both at once. Suicide accounts for more than one in five deaths between twenty-five and forty-four, and the highest female rate in Australia is in women aged twenty-five to twenty-nine. The psychiatric assessment belongs to the admission rather than to the discharge: the person who can do it properly is rarely free at four in the morning, and the window does not stay open.
  • Well on Friday, septic on SundayMeningococcal disease, invasive group A streptococcus, influenza. Toxic shock syndrome has a median age in the low twenties. There is no comorbidity to blame it on and no slow decline to have noticed, so the index of suspicion has to come from you rather than from the chart.
  • The infarct that is not atherosclerosis — except that mostly it isRoughly two thirds of infarcts under sixty-five are still atherothrombotic, and in pooled Australian registry data half of all acute coronary syndrome patients aged twenty to fifty-four were current smokers. Then the rest: inflammation, pregnancy and spontaneous coronary artery dissection, drugs and radiation, supply–demand and structural causes. Dissection accounts for about eleven per cent of infarcts in women under sixty-five, and over half of those were initially called something else.
  • Pregnant, or recentlyNot something a general adult unit sees often, which is most of the problem. In Australia between 2014 and 2023 the leading causes of maternal death were cardiovascular disease, sepsis, suicide and thromboembolism — not the quartet usually taught — and forty-five per cent of the deaths were postpartum against eighteen per cent intrapartum. Recently pregnant is a question you have to ask out loud, because nothing on the admission paperwork will answer it for you.
  • Who else is in the roomThis is the first age at which the patient is unambiguously the historian, and the first at which you must notice who is listening. Partner, housemate, parent, employer, the friend who drove. Pregnancy, substances, sexual health, driving, what actually happened, and whether anybody at home is frightening them are all answered differently with an audience. Ask who they would like to stay, and ask it as routine rather than as a signal.
  • The paediatric history somebody else is holdingIf they were a child with something, the useful document is twenty years old and in another hospital. Congenital heart disease, childhood cancer, neuromuscular disease: you want the baseline saturation, the last echocardiogram, the anthracycline total, the airway history. None of it will be volunteered.
  • The young heart that stoppedIn a prospective Australian and New Zealand study of 490 sudden cardiac deaths at ages one to thirty-five, forty per cent were still unexplained after a full autopsy, with coronary disease twenty-four per cent and the inherited cardiomyopathies sixteen. Among the unexplained deaths that went on to genetic testing, a clinically relevant variant was found in about twenty-seven per cent. And most of these arrests happen at rest rather than on the field, which is the opposite of the story everybody carries.

If you take one thing: at this age the examination gets easier and the history gets quietly harder. What you most need to know is usually the thing that will not be said with the person who drove them in sitting beside the bed.

Likely to be examined as: a long case or an ICE station where the marks sit in the structure of the history rather than in the findings — whether you establish who the patient wants present, whether you ask about pregnancy and about substances without apologising for it, whether you take a mechanism. A favourite viva: a twenty-six-year-old woman with abdominal pain and a brief faint. What is your first test?

Tier three

What you will be expected to do

Phase 3 — the final clinical years, the pre-internship term, and your first nights on call

  • The pregnancy test, every timeNot because women are unreliable — the two best studies on this found that what women said about their own dates and history was right essentially every time — but because the cost of the miss is nothing like the cost of the test. Before the imaging, before the drug, before theatre.
  • If she arrestsDisplace the uterus to the left with your hands, from about twenty weeks or whenever the fundus is at or above the umbilicus. Do not tilt the bed; it degrades your compressions. Hysterotomy is considered in the second half of pregnancy, it is done where she is, a scalpel is enough, and compressions continue throughout. ANZCOR Guideline 11.10 sets no time threshold for it — the four-minute rule you have probably been taught is not Australian guidance and was never a rule.
  • Tranexamic acid, and the clock on itAfter significant trauma, and within three hours of the onset of postpartum haemorrhage when it is indicated. Both windows are measured in hours, both are routinely missed, and the drug is in the fridge of every unit you will ever work in.
  • Thromboprophylaxis, because nobody remembersThe omission is close to specific to this age group: a young patient looks like the opposite of a thrombotic risk. Meanwhile immobility, injury, sepsis, pregnancy and the oral contraceptive are all sitting there in the notes you just wrote.
  • If you cannulate the artery by mistakeLeave it in. Do not pull the catheter out and hold pressure on a subclavian or carotid puncture you cannot compress — tell somebody senior and get vascular advice with the catheter still in place. The prevention is upstream: see the wire in the vein on ultrasound, in two planes, before you dilate anything.
  • Capacity, practicallyPresumed in an adult and specific to the decision in front of you. Injury, hypoxia, pain, fear and intoxication remove it temporarily, so a patient who cannot consent at eleven can often consent at two. Record the basis rather than the verdict. And find out who they actually want told: in this band the partner is frequently not the next of kin, and the two are often different people.
  • Ask the question about the unitAmerican claims data on young adults with severe sepsis found higher mortality in paediatric than adult intensive care — on small numbers, with no severity adjustment, and with the comorbidity burden almost certainly explaining it. The decision is not yours at your stage. But is this person in the right place is a reasonable thing for a student to ask out loud, and it is better asked on day one than on day four.
  • Say what happened, in wordsThey will carry this admission for another fifty years. Around sixty per cent of intensive care survivors are back at work at a year, and new physical, cognitive or psychological problems are the rule rather than the exception; there is no young-adult-specific evidence here, so do not assume they bounce. The sentence they still have in ten years will be one somebody said in the first week, and it may as well be an accurate one.
  • When it goes the other wayYoung adults are a small share of deceased organ donors overall and a large share of donation after suicide. There were 557 deceased donors in Australia in 2025. Asking properly, separately, and not as an afterthought is a skill, and at some point you will watch it done badly.

If you take one thing: the commonest error at your stage in this age group is believing the numbers. The second is forgetting that she might be pregnant. The third is assuming that because they are young they will be fine afterwards.

Likely to be examined as: an OSCE or viva on trauma, on the collapsed young adult, or on maternal resuscitation. The examiner is listening for whether you ask about pregnancy unprompted, whether you can justify manual uterine displacement over tilting the bed, and whether you say out loud at what point you would call for help.

And one on you

You have just read your own age band’s mortality data

Not examinable. Skippable. Here because it would be strange to leave it out.

Everything in tier two is a list of things that happen to people exactly your age, and medical students are not a protected class within it. There is no version of this section that is not slightly awkward to write, so here is the short version.

The exposures are mostly ordinary and mostly structural. You will drive home from places you should not be driving home from, at hours when the roads are worst and you are at your least sharp. Alcohol is woven through the social life of a medical course and is almost never discussed in it as an exposure of any kind. Shift work arrives early and sleep debt compounds quietly. Sport and water and the things that make the years worth having carry their own arithmetic. And a good number of you do not have a general practitioner of your own, which is an odd position to be in while learning to be one.

Who is your own doctor, and when did you last see them as a patient rather than as a colleague?

How do you get home after a late shift or a night out — actually, not in principle?

What would have to happen before you told somebody you were not coping, and who would it be?

That last one is worth sitting with, because what stops people is usually a belief about consequences rather than an absence of services. So here are the two things worth knowing before you need them.

It is not rare. The most recent national survey of Australian medical students’ mental health was run in 2013. It is thirteen years old and there has been no repeat, which tells you something in itself. It reached more than eighteen hundred students: more than four in ten screened as likely to have a psychiatric disorder, and around one in five reported having thought about suicide in the preceding year. Four in ten believed that a doctor with a mental health condition is seen by colleagues as less competent. The first two figures are what make the third one so expensive.

And asking for help does not put your registration at risk — which is close to the opposite of what most students believe, and is the one item on this page I would most like you to take away. Mandatory notification about a student has not been abolished, whatever you may have read; what changed in 2020 is that the threshold was raised. A treating practitioner is now required to notify only where an impairment places the public at substantial risk of harm, which Ahpra itself describes as “a very high threshold” and says was set there in order to “give practitioners the confidence to seek help without the fear of a mandatory notification.” Its guidelines for students are blunter still: a student may undertake clinical training with a mental health condition, and that “is not enough to trigger a mandatory notification.” In Western Australia, treating practitioners are exempt from the requirement altogether. Having a condition and being impaired are not the same thing — and being treated for one is among the things that stops it becoming the other.

If you would rather talk to somebody who is not your university

Drs4Drs — 1300 374 377. Twenty-four hours, every day, anywhere in Australia, for doctors and medical students. Free and confidential, and it includes up to three funded counselling sessions. The state and territory services are listed on the same site.

Doctors’ Health NSW — 02 9437 6552. Twenty-four hours. Covers NSW doctors, dentists, veterinarians and students in all three, and family or colleagues worried about one. It states that it is independent of the regulators and that it does not report callers to Ahpra, to employers, to training providers or to colleges. doctorshealth.org.au — and note the name has changed, so you may still find it listed as the Doctors’ Health Advisory Service.

Lifeline — 13 11 14, or text 0477 13 11 14, twenty-four hours, for anybody. And Beyond Blue — 1300 22 4636, also around the clock. Neither has an upper age limit, which several of the youth services do.

If you are at UNSW, the faculty wellbeing team is at mh.wellbeing@unsw.edu.au, and the Medicine and Health wellbeing page lists the university services alongside these. Ahpra, for its part, has a page called simply Seek help, which is worth two minutes if the paragraph above has not convinced you.

Over to you

Which tier did you read — and what were you taught that this page contradicts?

Tell me your year, which tier fitted, and what was missing, too basic or too advanced. That part is the same every time, and it is how these get better.

This one has a second question. The four-minute rule for hysterotomy is still taught in perfectly good medical schools and is not in Australian guidance; the tilted bed is still taught and degrades compressions; the normal-looking postpartum creatinine is still read as normal. Which of those three were you taught, and which way round? No names and no universities. A few dozen replies would tell me something I cannot find out any other way.
