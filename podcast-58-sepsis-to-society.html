From sepsis to society

The disease is the easy part. Tex Kissoon on everything around it

Show  PCICS Podcast, episode 161 Guest  Prof Niranjan “Tex” Kissoon Hosts  Saidie Rodriguez  ·  Deanna Todd Tzanetos Published  2 September 2026 Length  53 minutes
Listen to the episode

A joint WFPICCS and PCICS production, free to listen to. One long argument rather than a medley, so it wants an uninterrupted drive or a long walk rather than the gaps between cases.

Why it is worth your time

Almost everything we write about sepsis is about the first hour. Recognise it, take cultures, give antibiotics, give fluid, escalate. That is the part intensive care is good at, and the part our guidelines cover.

This episode is about the other ninety-nine per cent. Tex Kissoon has spent a career arguing that the clinical problem is comparatively tractable and the rest of it is not — and that a child's chance of surviving sepsis is determined long before anyone reaches for a cannula, by where they were born, whether they were vaccinated, how far the clinic is, whether the family can pay, and whether anyone at that clinic has been taught what sepsis looks like.

The scale, for context

Roughly 49 million cases of sepsis a year and something over 11 million deaths — about one death in five worldwide. Children under five account for around 40 per cent of cases, with approximately three million paediatric and neonatal deaths annually. Around 85 per cent of the global burden falls on low- and middle-income countries.

The World Health Assembly adopted a resolution on sepsis in 2017, proposed by the Global Sepsis Alliance, calling on all member states to build national action plans. Uptake has been slow. Kissoon is a co-author of the Global Burden of Disease analysis those figures come from, and now presides over the organisation that drafted the resolution.

The six things he wants changed

The episode is organised around six domains, and the interesting move is that only one of them is clinical.

Education

Not of intensivists — of everybody else. The person who first sees a septic child is almost never a specialist, and in much of the world is not a doctor. The recognition problem is a teaching problem.

Advocacy

Sepsis kills more people than several cancers combined and almost nobody can define it. Public ignorance is not incidental to the mortality; it is part of the causal chain, because it determines how late people present.

Health systems

Oxygen, a functioning laboratory, a reliable antibiotic supply, a referral pathway that works at night. Most sepsis deaths are not treatment failures but system failures.

Poverty reduction

The most uncomfortable of the six for a clinical audience, and the one he does not soften. Crowding, malnutrition, unsafe water and missed vaccination determine incidence. You cannot treat your way out of an incidence problem.

Access

Distance, cost and permission. A child who reaches care late has usually not been ignored at home; they have been unable to get there, or the family has been deciding what it will cost.

Post-sepsis support

The one most relevant to your own unit. Survivors carry new morbidity — cognitive, physical, psychological — sometimes for decades, and almost no health system has anywhere to send them. We count survival and then stop counting.

What it has to do with a regional Australian unit

It would be easy to file this as a global health episode about somewhere else. Two reasons not to.

The post-sepsis gap is ours too. A child who leaves your unit having survived meningococcal disease goes back to a school that has not been told anything, with parents who are frightened of every fever for a year, and no structured follow-up for the things sepsis actually leaves behind. Research into parents' experience describes it consistently: a rupture between life before and after, a sense of navigating in the dark, and guilt about warning signs they think they missed. None of that is exotic and none of it is expensive to address.

And the access argument applies on the mid north coast. Not in the same degree, but in the same direction: distance to a tertiary centre, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children carrying a heavier burden of infection, and families for whom presenting early has a real cost in petrol, time and lost pay. The six domains do not stop at a national border; they change scale.

Worth knowing about the guest

Kissoon is professor of paediatrics at the University of British Columbia, past president of the World Federation of Pediatric Intensive and Critical Care Societies, president of the Global Sepsis Alliance, co-chair of the paediatric arm of the Surviving Sepsis Campaign, and chair of World Sepsis Day — which fell on 13 September, the fortnight this went up.

Challenge for you

What happens to your sepsis survivors after they leave?

Not the mortality figure — the follow-up. Is there any? Who sees them, what is looked for, and who tells the school? If the honest answer is that they go back to the GP and nobody looks for anything in particular, say that; it is the commonest answer and it is the one worth publishing.
