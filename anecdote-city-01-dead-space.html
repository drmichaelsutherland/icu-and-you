No. 1

Dead space

“Her lungs are like lumps of rock,” he said. He was right about the rock and wrong about the lungs

It was just another busy Monday morning.

I was the senior Registrar on call for ICU, and they called me down to the Emergency Department first thing. It was a crisis. Acute severe asthma in a 30-something female. She was already tubed, and they had been working on her for a couple of hours. She was struggling. In fact, the ED physician said to me on the phone, “I think she is going to arrest.” He quickly summarised that she had been tubed the night before, but now they could neither oxygenate nor ventilate her. The lungs were super tight, and he could not inflate them easily, and they were using manual compression to try to empty the lungs with each expiratory phase of the cycle. The sats were at rock bottom, and so were the blood pressure and perfusion.

“Her lungs are like lumps of rock”, he said, adding, “It is just impossible”.

A lot goes through your mind as you rush down to attend a call like this. None of them are good things. Deaths from asthma, even in young people, were not unknown, but were becoming rarer and rarer. Usually, the various follow-ups ended up finding something or other that could have been done better, and that “something or other” was the important thing to know to prevent the death in the first place.

The Emergency Department of this particular hospital was absolutely top-notch, and the clinicians down there were super. That didn’t necessarily make me feel better, because they were absolutely fantastic at treating desperate emergencies, and … now they were calling for help… from us?

I didn’t at all like the sound of “manual compressions”, neither in the domain of “manual” nor in the domain of “compressions”. He quoted a tidal volume of about 150 ml, which is pretty much what is known as the anatomical dead space, and the “dead space” appeared to be where this was heading.

* * *

Fortunately, I was standing next to the ICU Consultant when I took the call, and he offered to come down to the ED with me. This was a guy who went on to become one of Australia’s leading Intensivists, and he was a handy guy to have with me.

The scene was Gothic, without the mood music. I had no idea what we could possibly do to assist. Ventilation was impossible, and ECMO wasn’t available.

My boss called for the bronchoscope. Oxygen saturation was 50%, and the patient was blue. I was pale as a sheet. I thought even trying a bronchoscopy would be fatal for the patient.

“Look at this,” said the Intensivist. I had a peek. At the end of the bronchoscope was a glue-like collection of gludgy, dry, clay-like goo. Dried secretions, with a tiny hole at the centre. He pushed the scope through the goo. And suddenly the patient could be ventilated. And she survived and thrived.

* * *

There was no asthma. It turned out the patient had been intubated the day before at a small, isolated facility, and the circuit had had no humidification.

And you need humidification. Dry gases in dry airways create obstructing, brick-like mucus that obstructs the passages.

In biological terms, you need a patent tube and plenty of lube.

Just another manic Monday.
