ICU and You  ·  Superquiz  ·  Number 67

Late middle age

Five questions — companion to Mind Map #38, Late middle age

Open book. No time limit, and as many attempts as you like. Look things up — that is the point. Three or more correct earns a certificate you can print or save.

  1. 1A 58-year-old man, body mass index 38, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and hypertension, has been ventilated for nine days with pneumonia. He has now failed three spontaneous breathing trials; each time he becomes tachypnoeic and tachycardic at about twenty minutes, with a rising blood pressure. The chest radiograph is unchanged. Echocardiography shows a normal ejection fraction with grade 2 diastolic dysfunction. Cumulative fluid balance is +6.4 L. The most likely reason he is failing, and the most useful next step, is:

    Weaning-induced pulmonary oedema is not a curiosity. Across 283 spontaneous breathing trials it accounted for 59 per cent of all weaning failures — the single commonest reason a trial fails. Positive pressure had been quietly unloading a left ventricle that cannot manage alone; the moment it comes off, venous return rises and afterload rises with it.

    This patient is the phenotype, three times over. The independent risk factors are chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (odds ratio 8.7), structural heart disease (4.5) and obesity (3.6). He has all three, and six litres of positive balance on top. A normal ejection fraction does not exclude it — diastolic dysfunction is the substrate, and the failure happens in diastole.

    Why the timing is the clue. Twenty minutes in, with a rising rather than falling blood pressure, is the signature. A weakness problem declares itself earlier and with a falling tidal volume; a sedation problem does not produce hypertension.

    Why D is the one that costs you. Extubating “regardless” after three failures, without addressing why, is how you get a fourth. Reintubation carries a hospital mortality of 18 per cent against 3 per cent in those never reintubated.

    Liu J et al, Cardiac dysfunction induced by weaning from mechanical ventilation, Critical Care 2016;20:369. Reintubation mortality from the cohort in Respiratory Care 2012;57:1555.

  2. 2A 61-year-old woman taking apixaban for atrial fibrillation arrives three hours after the onset of a left basal ganglia haemorrhage of 22 mL, with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 12. Her last dose was this morning. Blood pressure is 192/104. Your hospital stocks four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate; andexanet alfa is not held and would take hours to obtain. The most appropriate immediate step is to:

    ANNEXA-I is the trial behind this, and it cuts both ways. Andexanet was compared with usual care, and 85.5 per cent of the usual-care arm received prothrombin complex concentrate at a median dose of 3,000 IU. Haemostatic efficacy was achieved in 67.0 per cent against 53.1, an adjusted difference of 13.4 percentage points, and the trial was stopped early for efficacy.

    But thrombotic events ran at 10.3 per cent against 5.6, ischaemic stroke at 6.5 against 1.5, and thirty-day mortality was 27.8 per cent against 25.5 — no difference at all. Better control of the haematoma, four times the stroke rate, and no survival benefit demonstrated. That is a real choice, not a formality, and it is a reasonable position that the drug has not yet earned routine first-line use.

    Which is why C is right in a hospital that does not stock it. Waiting hours for a drug of unproven patient-level benefit, while a haematoma expands, is the worse of the two errors.

    A and B are not options. Fresh frozen plasma does not reverse a direct factor Xa inhibitor at any volume you could safely give, and vitamin K is for warfarin. D delays everything for a result that will not arrive in time and that, if normal, still would not exclude a clinically meaningful effect.

    Connolly SJ et al, Andexanet for factor Xa inhibitor-associated acute intracerebral haemorrhage (ANNEXA-I), New England Journal of Medicine 2024;390:1745.

  3. 3A 54-year-old man who drinks about fifteen standard drinks of beer a day, and eats very little, is brought in confused. Sodium 108 mmol/L, potassium 3.1, urea 1.8, serum osmolality 228, urine osmolality 92 mOsm/kg, urine sodium 12 mmol/L. He is given thiamine, started on sodium chloride 0.9 per cent, and offered a meal. Six hours later his sodium is 119 mmol/L. The most important action now is to:

    He has risen 11 mmol/L in six hours, which is already more than the whole twenty-four-hour allowance. Guidelines cap correction at 8 to 10 mmol/L in the first day and about 8 thereafter, and slower still when the starting sodium is extreme.

    The mechanism is what makes this band of patients dangerous. In beer potomania — and the tea-and-toast pattern that sits beside it — the kidney has been unable to excrete water for want of solute. The urine osmolality of 92 says the kidney is already maximally dilute and trying. The moment solute arrives, as saline or as food, a brisk water diuresis begins and the sodium climbs on its own. That is autocorrection, not treatment, and nobody prescribed it.

    Every risk factor for osmotic demyelination is present: sodium below 105 to 110, malnutrition, hypokalaemia, alcohol, and a rapid rise. Malnourished cells are least able to recover the solute they shed.

    So the task is now to slow it down, not speed it up. Free water, and desmopressin to close off the water diuresis, is an accepted rescue. A is the answer that does the harm, because “correcting appropriately” is precisely how this gets missed on a ward round.

    European and American guidance on hyponatraemia, as reviewed in Journal of Nephrology 2021;34:1157, which also describes the proactive desmopressin clamp.

  4. 4A 52-year-old Aboriginal man from a remote community in the Northern Territory is admitted with a severe asthma exacerbation and given high-dose corticosteroids. On day four he develops an ileus, Gram-negative bacteraemia and diffuse bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. His eosinophil count is normal. The diagnosis that most urgently needs excluding is:

    Ileus, Gram-negative sepsis and pulmonary infiltrates appearing a few days after corticosteroids, in a patient from an endemic area, is strongyloides hyperinfection until proved otherwise. The larvae migrate through the bowel wall and carry gut organisms with them, which is why the blood cultures grow enteric flora and why the abdomen goes quiet.

    This is an Australian problem, not an imported one. Prevalence has been reported at up to 60 per cent in some remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. And corticosteroids have precipitated death in more than 60 per cent of disseminated cases.

    The normal eosinophil count is a trap, not a reassurance. Steroids suppress eosinophilia, and its absence during hyperinfection is a poor prognostic sign rather than evidence against the diagnosis.

    Melioidosis is the near miss, and deserves its credit — also endemic in the Top End, also unmasked by immunosuppression, and it belongs on the same list. It tends to give pneumonia, abscesses and bacteraemia with Burkholderia pseudomallei rather than an ileus with enteric organisms. Cover both while you find out.

    The real lesson is upstream. Serology — strongyloides IgG by ELISA, since faecal microscopy is often negative in chronic infection — or empirical ivermectin 200 µg/kg, before the steroids, in anyone from an endemic area.

    Chronic strongyloidiasis — don’t look and you won’t find, Australian Family Physician 2016;45(1):40.

  5. 5A 57-year-old man with metastatic lung cancer is intubated for septic shock and has no capacity. There is no advance care directive and no appointed guardian. At the bedside are his de facto partner of twelve years, who has been his full-time unpaid carer, and his adult son, who has flown in from overseas and is insisting that everything be done. They disagree with each other. Under New South Wales law, the most appropriate next step is to:

    The hierarchy is set out in statute and it is not the one most people assume. The person responsible is the first available of: an appointed or enduring guardian; then a spouse, de facto spouse or partner in a close continuing relationship; then an unpaid carer; then a close relative or friend with frequent contact and a personal interest in the patient’s welfare. The partner here qualifies twice, at the second level and the third.

    Next of kin has no automatic standing at all. The Tribunal’s own fact sheet says the person responsible “is not always the patient’s next of kin”. An adult child sits at the fourth level, and only then if the contact and the interest are there.

    Why C is wrong: the Tribunal is for when there is no person responsible, when they cannot be found, or when they will not or cannot act — and for special treatment. It is not the route for resolving a family disagreement when a person responsible is standing in the room.

    Why D is half right and still wrong. Treatment urgently necessary to save life or prevent serious damage to health does not require consent, and you should give it. But that provision justifies the next hour. It does not substitute for establishing what this man was told about his illness, what he said about it, and who speaks for him now.

    One caveat that matters. This is New South Wales. Every state and territory has its own hierarchy and they do not agree — Victoria’s medical treatment decision maker and Queensland’s statutory health attorney sit in a different order. Know the one you work under.

    NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal, Guardianship Division, Person responsible fact sheet, under the Guardianship Act 1987 (NSW).
