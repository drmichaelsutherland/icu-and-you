ICU and You  ·  Myths and Misconceptions  ·  Number 2

Five things everybody knows

Late middle age — where each belief came from, and whether it survives

How this one differs from the last

The first edition took five things we were taught. These five are things our patients believe — and four of the five they were told by a doctor, which is the part that should keep us honest. Several checkable claims, each traced to where it started, each given a verdict, and the verdict graded rather than scored for effect.

Every one of these governs a decision somebody in their fifties is making this week: what to drink, whether to take the tablet, whether to start the patch, what the normal test means, and whether to have the blood test at all. Two of them collapse completely. Two survive in a smaller and more honest form. One is not a myth at all, although every word of it is wrong.

The first

“A glass of red wine a day is good for your heart.”

Where it came from

The French paradox, and then thirty years of cohort studies that agreed with it. Study after study found a J-shaped curve: light drinkers had less coronary disease than people who drank nothing at all. Millions of participants, remarkable consistency, and a conclusion so welcome that it became one of the most repeated health messages of the late twentieth century.

The curve was real. The question nobody pressed hard enough was who, exactly, was standing in the group at the bottom left.

What the evidence shows

  • The sick quitterPeople stop drinking because they become unwell. Counted as non-drinkers, they make the comparison group sicker than the population it is supposed to represent — so the drinkers look healthier without being healthier. Most reviews never removed former drinkers from that group at all.
  • Zhao and colleagues, 2017Younger cohorts, and a strict definition of lifetime abstinence. The protection vanished: no significant benefit up to two drinks a day, relative risk 0.95, confidence interval 0.75 to 1.21.
  • Liang and Chikritzhs, 2013Put the former drinkers back where they belonged, in the drinking group. The apparent heart protection disappeared.
  • Mendelian randomisationGenetic variants affecting alcohol metabolism are allocated at conception, so they cannot be confounded by lifestyle. Carr and colleagues pooled four such studies of ischaemic heart disease in 2024: no significant association in either direction, including when they went looking specifically for a J-shape. Millwood and colleagues found none with myocardial infarction. For stroke no study found protection, and two found increased risk.
The nuance worth keeping

The collapse of the J-curve does not make a glass of wine dangerous in the way that a bottle of it is. The Australian guidelines are written as risk rather than prohibition: within ten standard drinks a week, a healthy adult has less than a one in a hundred chance of dying of an alcohol-related condition.

What died is not the glass of wine. It is the idea that drinking it was better for you than not drinking it.

Busted

There is no protective dose in the 2020 NHMRC guidelines, and no protective dose in the designs that cannot be fooled by who is in the abstainer group. The guideline sentence is the less you drink, the lower your risk of harm from alcohol, with no floor under it.

Worth saying in this age band particularly. Australians between forty-five and sixty-four account for about fifty-five per cent of the country’s alcohol-caused deaths — the demographic most attached to this belief is the one it costs most.

The second

“Statins cause muscle pain.”

Where it came from

From the clinic, which is the most persuasive place a belief can come from. Statin myopathy is real, rhabdomyolysis is real, and every prescriber has had the conversation. Observational series put muscle symptoms in ten to twenty-five per cent of people taking statins, while the randomised trials found a difference of about one per cent against placebo. Two numbers that far apart usually mean somebody has mismeasured something.

What the evidence shows

  • SAMSON, 60 adults, one yearImperial College London, funded by the British Heart Foundation, in people who had already stopped statins because of side effects. Twelve bottles, one a month, allocated at random and blinded: four of atorvastatin 20 mg, four of placebo, and four empty. Daily symptom score out of a hundred on a phone. Forty-nine finished.
  • The three numbersMean symptom intensity: 16.3 on the statin. 15.4 on the placebo. 8.0 with no tablet at all. About ninety per cent of the symptom burden experienced on a statin was also experienced on a dummy. Participants abandoned the placebo for intolerable side effects as often as they abandoned the drug.
  • StatinWISE, 200 patientsThe same n-of-1 design, in primary care, in people who had stopped or were about to stop because of muscle symptoms. No difference in the frequency or the severity of symptoms between statin months and placebo months.
  • What they did nextShown their own data, about half the SAMSON participants went back on a statin and four more intended to. Most of StatinWISE’s did the same. Nobody argued them into it.
The empty bottles are the whole point

Eight against fifteen. Taking a tablet — any tablet — roughly doubled the symptoms. That is not a trick of the data and it is not a character failing. It is what expectation does to the perception of a body you have been invited to pay close attention to.

Which means the symptoms are not imaginary, and “it is in your head” is both unkind and inaccurate. True statin myopathy exists and a raised creatine kinase in somebody with weakness is not a nocebo and must never be treated as one.

Busted, but handle gently

The attribution is usually wrong. The suffering is not. The useful response is not an argument about the literature but the same experiment the trials ran: stop, restart, change the drug or the dose, and where you can, blind it. People believe their own data in a way they do not believe yours.

The third

“Hormone therapy causes breast cancer.”

Where it came from

From a single day. In July 2002 the Women’s Health Initiative stopped its oestrogen-plus-progestogen arm early and announced an increase in invasive breast cancer. The finding travelled as a relative risk, without the absolute beside it, and prescriptions collapsed across the developed world within months.

Two things about that trial were not in the headline. The average participant was sixty-three years old and most were more than a decade past menopause. And the preparations were not the ones in use now.

What the evidence shows

  • The absolute numbersFrom the Australasian Menopause Society, for use of under five years starting near menopause: oestrogen alone, about 3 extra cases per 10,000 woman-years. Oestrogen with a progestogen, about 9.
  • It climbs with age and with durationCombined therapy for five years or more: 15 extra per 10,000 woman-years at 50 to 59, 29 at 60 to 69, 36 at 70 to 79. The excess falls again after stopping.
  • The other arm went the other wayWHI also randomised women who had had a hysterectomy to oestrogen alone. In long-term follow-up that arm showed a statistically significant reduction in breast cancer incidence. It was not the arm that made the news.
  • And the heartIn follow-up there was no evidence of cardiovascular harm in women started close to menopause, with a trend the other way; Cochrane found reduced mortality or coronary protection in those starting within ten years. It is still not a reason to prescribe it — MHT is not recommended as primary cardiovascular prevention.
The opposite error is just as wrong

“Hormone therapy does not cause breast cancer” is not the corrected version of this myth. Combined therapy does raise the risk. It raises it modestly, more with longer use and more with later starting, and the progestogen matters — micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appear safer than most of the older synthetics.

This is a verdict about proportion, not about absolution.

Busted as stated — and the harm was done by a missing denominator

The risk is real and it is small. What was wrong in 2002 was a relative risk, drawn from a trial whose average woman was sixty-three, reported without the absolute, and then applied to women of fifty-two deciding whether to treat symptoms that were wrecking their sleep and their work.

A generation went untreated on the strength of a number that was true and useless in the same breath. If there is a lesson here for the rest of us, it is about how a finding is published, not about oestrogen.

The fourth

“A normal stress test means your arteries are fine.”

Where it came from

From a model that is entirely reasonable. A narrowing limits flow; limited flow causes ischaemia when you load the heart; a test that loads the heart will therefore find the problem. Every step of that is true. It is simply about the wrong lesion.

What the evidence shows

  • Little and colleagues, 1988Forty-two consecutive patients who happened to have had coronary angiography both before and within a month after an acute myocardial infarction. Twenty-nine of them had a newly occluded artery.
  • Where that artery had beenIn 19 of the 29, sixty-six per cent, the artery that went on to close had been less than 50 per cent narrowed on the earlier film. In 28 of 29, ninety-seven per cent, it was under 70 per cent.
  • Which is worth reading twiceThe vessel that killed the muscle was, on the angiogram taken beforehand, one of the ones nobody was worried about.
  • Because rupture is not narrowingWhat ruptures is a lipid core under a thin fibrous cap. That is a question about the composition of a plaque, and a test that measures flow cannot answer it — a plaque can be dangerous and roomy at the same time.
A normal test is not a worthless test

It is good evidence against flow-limiting disease, which is a real question and often the one you need answered before an operation. The error is not in doing the test. It is in letting it answer a question it was never asked — which, for the patient, is always am I going to have a heart attack.

Busted

And notice what this does to the ranking of everything else. If imaging cannot identify the plaque that is going to rupture, then the things that change how plaques behave — the lipids, the blood pressure, the cigarettes — are not the consolation prize you offer when the scan is normal. They are the intervention.

The fifth

“Every man over fifty should have a PSA test.”

Where it came from

From an inference that looks airtight. Prostate cancer is the commonest cancer in Australian men and the second leading cause of cancer death among them, with about 29,000 diagnosed in 2025. There is a cheap blood test. Therefore everyone should have it.

That sentence is wrong in three separate places, and it has also just moved.

What the evidence shows

The 2026 Guidelines for the Early Detection of Prostate Cancer in Australia, built on a review of more than 28,000 research articles.

  • Not “should”Testing two-yearly from 50 to 69 is offered after an informed discussion of the benefits and harms. A man may test, opt out, or opt back in later. It is a decision, not an instruction.
  • Not “every man”Routine testing is not recommended from 45 to 49 at average risk. It starts at 45 for men at higher risk: a brother with prostate cancer, a father diagnosed before 65, two or more second-degree relatives who died of it, Black sub-Saharan ancestry, or a BRCA2 mutation.
  • Not simply “over fifty”Past 70 it turns on clinical assessment, and two-yearly testing is offered only where life expectancy is greater than seven years.
  • And the number that counts changesAction thresholds are 1.0 µg/L at 45 to 49, 3.0 at 50 to 69 on the standard pathway (2.0 for higher risk), and 5.5 at 70 and over. Above threshold, repeat at one to three months. An MRI of the prostate is recommended before any biopsy, which is the change that most alters the harm side of the ledger.
The direction of travel surprised people

After two decades in which the advice moved steadily away from testing, this revision moves back toward it — because MRI before biopsy, and active surveillance of low-risk disease, have reduced the cost of finding something. The harms of screening are not fixed properties of the test. They depend on what happens next, and what happens next has changed.

Not a myth — but not what it says either

For a man of fifty-five at average risk, a PSA every two years after a proper conversation is precisely what Australian guidance now recommends. So the instinct behind the sentence is sound.

Every word of the sentence is still wrong. Not every man, not simply over fifty, and not should. What needs retiring is the version that makes it automatic, because an automatic test is a test nobody discussed, and the discussion is where most of the benefit lives.

Challenge for you

Which one of these did you still half-believe?

I will go first. I have said the thing about the glass of red wine to patients, out loud, in a professional capacity, and I believed it when I said it. It was in the air for thirty years and it had the cohort studies behind it, and none of that makes it true.

So: which of the five did you still half-hold, and where did you pick it up — a lecture, a consultant, a newspaper, a patient? I am as interested in where a belief came from as in whether it survives, because the route in tells you something about what else might have come the same way.

And the opposite case, which is rarer and more useful: something you were told to disbelieve that turned out to be right.
