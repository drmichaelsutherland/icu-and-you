Early middle age

Thirty to forty-five — the band where what happened and what grew cross over, and the physiology is the easy part

This is the age band where the two lines cross. Most of what brings a young adult in is something that happened to them. By the far end of this decade and a half, more of it is something that grew.

Neither has stopped. Injury still leads every age band up to forty-four, and the trauma is the same trauma — road, occupational, farm, surf. What has changed is what is sitting underneath it. There is a first chronic disease now, often undiagnosed; twenty years of smoking or drinking; a body mass index that has been climbing since the second child. The reserve is still a young adult’s, so they compensate late and then lose it in one step. The difference is that this time something gives way underneath.

Three things deserve saying plainly before the map. Suicide is the leading cause of death for Australians aged 15 to 44, which in this unit arrives as the overdose, the hypoxic brain injury, and the donation conversation. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients in this band carry the chronic disease burden of a much older patient — renal failure, rheumatic heart disease, diabetes and ischaemic heart disease a decade or two early — so the birthday is a poor guide to the physiology. And the drugs we now give have their own critical illness: checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis, cytokine release, euglycaemic ketoacidosis on an SGLT2 inhibitor. Most of us were not taught any of those.

What is not difficult here is the physiology. Doses are adult, the airway is unremarkable, capacity is presumed. What is difficult is everything around it: mid-career, mid-mortgage, children at school, and a household that comes apart while the patient is unconscious.

Four stations: where the two lines cross, what actually brings them in, what grows and what we give them, and what outlasts the admission.

How to use a mind map

Do not read it like a chapter. Scan it. Let your eye go where it wants, read three boxes that catch it, and stop. You are not trying to finish it and you are certainly not trying to memorise it.

Then come back. Before a shift on which this subject might come up, or after a case that went a particular way, or while waiting for a retrieval team. Thirty seconds at a time, often, beats an hour once.

It works the way a painting in a corridor works. Nobody sits down and studies the painting they walk past every morning, and yet after a year they could tell you what is in it, where the light falls, and exactly what is on the left-hand side — and they would notice at once if something had changed. That noticing is the point. These maps are not built to be recalled on demand; they are built so that when the thing in front of you does not fit, something quietly objects.

On a phone: turn it sideways, or scroll the map across. Pinch to zoom on any section.

Challenge for you

Who is looking after the children tonight?

It is the question this age band turns on, and the one least likely to be asked. A thirty-eight-year-old is admitted at two in the morning and somewhere there are school-age children, a partner who has not slept, an employer who has not been told, and a household running on nobody.

Does your unit ask it — routinely, on admission, written down? Is there anyone whose job it is to act on the answer, or does it depend on which nurse is on? I am interested in what actually happens rather than what the policy says, and particularly in regional units where the family may be several hours away and the social work office is open on Tuesdays.

And the other half, if you have it: a time when nobody asked, and what that cost.
