ICU and You  ·  The Jukebox  ·  Number 1

Five songs from the young adult ward

A coma, a cancer, a mind with no way out, an organ donor, and one young man flatly refusing to die

A new column

The Jukebox is for popular songs that wander, deliberately or otherwise, into the territory this site covers. Some of them know exactly what they are about. Most of them do not, and those are usually the better stories.

Two ground rules. I do not reproduce lyrics here — other people's words are other people's property, and a description does the job. And I check the backstory, because the received version of why a song exists is wrong more often than not, which is half the fun.

This started with somebody asking me whether there is a song called When ICU. There is not. I looked properly. What there is instead is better.

In May 2020, Phoebe Bridgers released a song from her album Punisher that she had written and recorded under the title ICU. It is not about a hospital at all — it is about the end of a long relationship with her former drummer, who plays on it. By the time it came out, the pandemic had made the pun unbearable, and she renamed it I See You.

Which is to say the only song in popular music whose title was changed out of deference to intensive care units is one that had nothing to do with them.

So here are five that do, with the stories behind them checked rather than repeated. They happen to arrange themselves into something like an admission.

Listen along. All of these, in order, are on one playlist — including the two in the tea room at the end, and the Phoebe Bridgers track that started the argument. Eight songs, about half an hour.

One · The coma

Girlfriend in a Coma

The Smiths · single, 10 August 1987, from Strangeways, Here We Come · Morrissey and Marr · UK number 13

Two minutes and two seconds, jangling along pleasantly, about a young woman who is not going to wake up and a narrator who is not entirely sure how sorry he is. The gap between the tune and the subject is the whole point, and it is the reason the song still unsettles people forty years on.

Here is what I did not know. Johnny Marr has said the song came out of his and Morrissey's shared affection for a 1970 reggae record — Bob and Marcia's version of Nina Simone's To Be Young, Gifted and Black — and that he was trying to catch something of its spirit. The band first attempted it as rocksteady. Morrissey had told Melody Maker three years earlier that all reggae was vile.

So the most famous coma song in English pop began as an attempt at a reggae tune by a man on record as despising the genre, and arrived as the first single from the last album, released a fortnight or so before Marr walked out. The widely repeated claim that the BBC banned it does not hold up; it simply was not played much, which Morrissey appeared to regard as the correct response.

Two · The diagnosis

Cancer

My Chemical Romance · The Black Parade, 23 October 2006 · credited to all five members · never a single

The Black Parade is a rock opera about a man the band simply call The Patient, and Cancer is the point at which the record stops being metaphorical. It is sung from inside the illness, and it is mostly about the body — the indignities, the things that have stopped working, the business of looking at somebody you are leaving.

Gerard Way said at the time that it is not a poetic track, that it is direct and brutal, that the disease is partly a metaphor but he also meant it literally, and that he set out to write the darkest song he could and thought he had managed it. Rob Cavallo, who produced the record, says Way called him over to the piano, sang while Cavallo found the chords, and the song was written in about eight minutes. The engineer turned up half an hour later and they recorded it.

Twenty One Pilots covered it in 2016 for a magazine tribute marking the album's tenth anniversary, and took it into the American charts, which the original never was. It is on more hospital playlists than any song on this page, and adolescent and young adult oncology units in several countries have had to think about what to do when a patient asks for it.

Three · The one with no way out

One

Metallica · …And Justice for All, 1988; single 10 January 1989 · Hetfield and Ulrich · Australia number 38 · Grammy, Best Metal Performance, 1990

Taken from Dalton Trumbo's 1939 novel Johnny Got His Gun, which Trumbo also filmed in 1971, and whose footage the band used in the video. The protagonist, Joe Bonham, is a soldier caught by an artillery shell. He has lost both arms and both legs. His face is gone — eyes, ears, nose, mouth, jaw. He is blind, deaf and mute, and he cannot move.

And his mind is entirely intact. In the novel he eventually works out that he can communicate by banging his head against the pillow in Morse code, and spends a long time doing it before anybody realises what the noise is.

A correction, before anybody writes in

This is not locked-in syndrome, and I was about to say it was. Locked-in syndrome is a lesion of the ventral pons: quadriplegia and lower cranial nerve palsy with the cortex intact and, classically, vertical gaze and blinking left as the way out. Joe Bonham has a normal brainstem. He has been deafferented and de-efferented mechanically, by having the apparatus removed.

Nor is he ventilated. It is 1918; there is nothing to ventilate him with, his diaphragm works, and in the novel he is fed by tube. What Trumbo did, in 1939, was imagine the exact ethical problem that intensive care would go on to create for real — a preserved mind with no route out of the body — about a decade before the equipment existed to produce it. That seems to me more impressive than if he had simply described one.

One footnote the band have never quite lived down. They played One at the Grammys in February 1989 and then lost the inaugural award for hard rock and metal to Jethro Tull, a flute band, for an album called Crest of a Knave. They won the following year, when the category was split.

Four · The donor

Lightning Crashes

Live · Throwing Copper, 24 April 1994; single September 1994 · Ed Kowalczyk · number one on the American alternative and rock charts

Ed Kowalczyk wrote it at twenty-one at his mother's house in Pennsylvania. He has always described it as being about a death and a birth happening in the same building at the same hour — a transference, he called it — and has said plainly that people who read the video as a woman dying in childbirth have misunderstood him. He meant it as a happy ending.

The record is dedicated to Barbara Lewis, a friend of the band from their home town, killed at nineteen by a drunk driver fleeing a robbery, around the time they were recording. That much is well attested and Kowalczyk has spoken about it.

And Barbara Lewis was a registered organ donor. Her family have talked since about the people who received her organs; the accounts differ on which organs and which recipients, so I will not pretend to a precision that is not there. But a song about a life passing from one person to another, written by a young man who did not know it, dedicated to a nineteen-year-old who had quietly arranged for exactly that to happen, is the sort of coincidence that would be rejected by an editor as too convenient.

Somebody in your unit this year will be the age she was.

Five · The one who refuses

Live Forever

Oasis · single, 8 August 1994, from Definitely Maybe · Noel Gallagher · UK number 10, their first top ten · platinum in Australia

He started it in 1991, before he had joined his younger brother's band. He was working for a builder's merchant in Manchester, a pipe fell on his foot, and he was put on light duties in a storeroom, where there was time to write. He brought the finished song to rehearsals in 1993, and it is generally credited with winning him the job of writing the songs.

Noel has said, more than once, that he wrote it against the mood of the time — that he remembered Nirvana having a song whose title announced that its author hated himself and wanted to die, and that he was simply not having it; that Cobain seemed to have everything and be wretched, while Oasis had nothing and he still thought getting out of bed was the best offer going. He has also, on other occasions, denied that the song was aimed at Nirvana at all, and he was a genuine admirer. Both things are on the record. People are allowed to be inconsistent about where their best song came from.

It belongs here because it is the most accurate song on this page. Not medically — medically it is nonsense. But the conviction of a healthy twenty-four-year-old that he is structurally exempt is the exact physiology this fortnight has been about: the most reserve a human body will ever have, and a person who has never once had cause to doubt it.

And two for the tea room

Stayin' Alive runs at 103 beats per minute, which sits almost exactly on the recommended rate for chest compressions. That is why it is on every life support course in the country. The evidence is one small teaching study from 2008 — fifteen participants, presented at a conference — which is a thin foundation for a global teaching convention, and I offer that as a fact rather than a complaint. A disco song about surviving a week in Brooklyn turned out to be the best metronome in the trolley.

Ventilator Blues, on the Rolling Stones' Exile on Main St., is not about a ventilator. It is reportedly named for the feeble fan in the stifling basement at Villa Nellcôte where they recorded it — though even that explanation is poorly sourced, so the honest position is that nobody is quite sure. It is also the only song Mick Taylor was credited on during his years in the band, which he has since mentioned being a bit peeved about.

This is a column that only works if other people fill it. Tell me the song — and if you know it, the story behind it, because the story is the part I want. Anything that touches illness, a hospital, a resuscitation, a transplant, a death or a recovery, in any genre and any language. The quirkier the better.

I would particularly like the ones where the received explanation turns out to be wrong. And if there is a song you cannot hear any more because of a shift you once did, you can tell me that too. Several people have mentioned they have one.
