Pantoprazole

The drug we start without deciding to, and stop without meaning to

About this series

Drugstore is for drug profiles. Each one runs the same way: the few things that matter most, then the practical business of doses, routes and the errors that recur, and then the pharmacology proper — kinetics, dynamics, indications and adverse effects.

The intention is to serve two readers at once. The clinician who needs the drug at three in the morning, and the First Part candidate who has to explain its metabolism. Those are different needs, and most references choose one.

This one is pantoprazole, with the weight on the decision rather than the molecule — because the molecule is not where the difficulty is. It sits in a fortnight about early middle age because the person who bleeds from an ulcer at forty-two usually has the reason written on their own medication chart, and because a pantoprazole started this week may still be being dispensed in 2036.

Almost every patient on your unit tonight is on pantoprazole. Rather fewer of them have a reason. It is a good drug at a sensible dose for a question that, once answered, is never asked again.

The ten that matter

  1. It prevents bleeding, and the effect is large. REVISE randomised 4,900 ventilated adults to pantoprazole 40 mg daily or placebo. Clinically important upper gastrointestinal bleeding occurred in 1.0% against 3.5%, a hazard ratio of 0.30 (95% CI 0.19–0.47) and an absolute difference of 2.5 percentage points — about forty patients treated to prevent one bleed.
  2. It has not been shown to change mortality. Ninety-day mortality was 29.1% against 30.9%, hazard ratio 0.94 (95% CI 0.85–1.04). Stress ulcer bleeding is seldom what kills people, which is the honest explanation and also the uncomfortable one.
  3. The unresolved question is the sickest patients. In REVISE the mortality hazard ratio was 0.85 below an APACHE II of 25 and 1.04 at or above it; in SUP-ICU the relative risks were 0.92 and 1.13. Neither interaction reached significance, and both point the same way. Nobody has shown harm. Nobody has excluded it either.
  4. The prophylactic dose is low. Forty milligrams once daily, by either route. The 2024 SCCM and ASHP guideline recommends low-dose over high-dose prophylaxis and defines low dose as up to 40 mg daily. High dose belongs to the bleeding ulcer, not to the prevention of one.
  5. Risk means coagulopathy, shock or chronic liver disease. Those are the factors the same guideline identifies, and it found no firm evidence that mechanical ventilation alone is one. REVISE nonetheless enrolled by ventilation and found a benefit. Both statements are true, and the gap between them is where the judgement lives.
  6. The job you will actually be judged on is stopping it. In five Western Australian intensive care units, 184 patients were started on a proton pump inhibitor for prophylaxis; 90 of them — 48.9% — were still taking it when they left hospital, and nine of those ninety had a documented indication.
  7. For a bleeding ulcer, the high dose comes after the endoscope, not before it. Eighty milligrams then 8 mg/h for three days, or 80 mg then 40 mg two to four times daily. Before endoscopy, the American College of Gastroenterology could not reach a recommendation either way.
  8. Neither of those uses is registered in Australia. The Australian Product Information for the injection lists ulcer, reflux oesophagitis, refractory lesions and Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, at 40 mg a day. Stress ulcer prophylaxis is not there, and neither is 8 mg an hour. Both are defensible. Both are off-label, and it is better to know that than to find out in a coroner's court.
  9. In the bleeding cirrhotic, pantoprazole is not the treatment. What changes the outcome is a vasoactive drug, antibiotics and an endoscopy. And the Australian label contraindicates pantoprazole in cirrhosis or severe liver disease. We give it anyway — see below for why that is reasonable and why you should still know the label says otherwise.
  10. Plasma half-life about one hour; acid suppression beyond twenty-four. The bond to the pump is covalent, so the drug has long gone while the enzyme it inactivated has not. This is the single most examinable fact about the class, and the most useful one at the bedside.

Prophylaxis: who, how much, and when to stop

Three large trials have now asked the question in ventilated patients, and they agree about the bleeding and disagree about nothing very much else. REVISE is the biggest and the most recent, and its result is unambiguous: pantoprazole prevents clinically important upper gastrointestinal bleeding, with a number needed to treat of about forty.

A 2026 meta-analysis of nine randomised trials in 8,388 invasively ventilated adults found the same thing — clinically important bleeding, odds ratio 0.36 (95% CI 0.19–0.69); all-cause mortality, odds ratio 0.98 (0.89–1.07); ventilator-associated pneumonia, 0.99 (0.87–1.12); Clostridioides difficile infection, 1.63 (0.97–2.73), with event rates too low to settle it.

The thing that is not settled

Both of the large placebo-controlled trials show a mortality signal running the wrong way in the most severely ill, and neither reaches significance. PEPTIC, comparing proton pump inhibitors with H2 receptor antagonists, showed a similar pattern. The mechanism, if there is one, is entirely speculative. The practical consequence is small but real: in the patient who is already dying, the prophylaxis is doing least good and is the likeliest place for an unmeasured harm to sit. It is a reason to review it daily, not a reason to withhold it.

Which leaves the part nobody writes a trial about. Prophylaxis is started on day one by somebody who is busy, and after that it is nobody's job. The Western Australian audit is the cleanest Australian number on this: roughly half of the patients started on it in intensive care were still on it at hospital discharge, and nine in ten of those had no recorded reason. The authors put the direct cost to their state health system at about $250,800 a year, which is the least interesting part of it.

The guideline's good practice statements are blunt and worth memorising: stop it when the risk factor has gone, and stop it before the patient leaves the intensive care unit, because after that nobody will.

The bleeding ulcer

This is where the high dose belongs, and the evidence behind it is much older and much stronger than the prophylaxis evidence. After successful endoscopic haemostasis of a bleeding ulcer, the American College of Gastroenterology makes a strong recommendation for high-dose proton pump inhibitor therapy for three days, on moderate to high quality evidence: further bleeding relative risk 0.43 (95% CI 0.33–0.56), mortality 0.41 (0.22–0.79).

SituationRegimenNote
Stress ulcer prophylaxis 40 mg once daily, intravenous or enteral Low dose, deliberately. Not a registered Australian indication. Review daily and stop before the patient leaves the unit.
Bleeding ulcer, after endoscopic haemostasis 80 mg intravenously, then 8 mg/h for 72 hours
or 80 mg, then 40 mg two to four times daily		 Strong recommendation. Choose between them on cost and ease — and note that in the guideline's own comparison the continuous infusion trended very slightly worse.
Days four to fourteen, high-risk ulcer 40 mg twice daily, orally Conditional, low quality, one trial: further bleeding at 28 days 10.8% against 28.7% for once daily.
Registered Australian intravenous use 40 mg daily (20–40 mg for reflux oesophagitis) Where the oral route is not appropriate. Switch to oral as soon as practicable.
Helicobacter eradication 40 mg twice daily with antibiotics, 7 days, orally Not an intensive care decision, but worth knowing you have just made the breath test and the biopsy unreliable for a fortnight.

Before endoscopy the position is honestly unresolved. The guideline panel could not reach a recommendation for or against; the trial it rests on found further bleeding of 3.5% against 2.5% and mortality of 2.5% against 2.2%, both differences within noise. Starting it while you wait for the gastroenterologist is reasonable and is not treatment. The treatment is the endoscopy.

The cirrhotic with melaena

Pantoprazole is not the drug that matters here. A vasoactive drug and antibiotics and an urgent endoscopy are. Where proton pump inhibitors have been tested after band ligation of oesophageal varices — four randomised trials, 445 patients — there was no reduction in bleeding at eight weeks (relative risk 0.71, 95% CI 0.39–1.30) and none in mortality (0.75, 0.23–2.53). The confidence intervals are wide and this is not proof of no effect. It is, though, a reason not to let a pantoprazole infusion feel like treatment while the terlipressin has not been written up.

Giving it

  • ReconstitutionInject 10 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride into the vial. It may then be given as it is, or added to 100 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride or 5% or 10% glucose, over two to fifteen minutes.
  • Nothing else in the lineThe Product Information says plainly not to mix it with any medicinal product other than those diluents. In a patient with two lumens and nine infusions this is the sentence that gets ignored. Flush, or give it somewhere else.
  • If you must keep itTwo to eight degrees, no more than twelve hours, and discard it if it is cloudy or has thrown a precipitate.
  • Tablets do not go down a tubeThey are enteric coated and the Product Information says they must not be chewed or crushed. Crushing one does not deliver a smaller dose; it delivers a dose that acid destroys before it is absorbed.
  • The granules doThe 40 mg granule preparation exists for exactly this, and the Australian label describes giving it through a nasogastric or gastric tube. Worth knowing the hospital stocks it before the night you need it.
  • Before food, for a reason that is not absorptionPantoprazole's absorption is not meaningfully affected by food — a meal may delay absorption by a couple of hours, but neither the peak concentration nor the total exposure changes, and the label says it may be taken without regard to meals. The pre-meal advice is a pharmacodynamic one: the drug only inactivates pumps that are actually secreting, and a meal is what recruits them. Which raises a question nobody has answered well — what continuous enteral feeding, or complete fasting, does to that logic in a ventilated patient.
  • Renal failure changes nothingNo dose adjustment, and essentially none of the drug leaves unchanged in the urine.
  • Liver failure doesHalf-life rises to seven to nine hours and exposure rises five- to sevenfold. The Australian label reduces the initial dose in mild disease and contraindicates the drug outright in cirrhosis or severe liver disease. Practice does not follow it, and the honest reconstruction is that the contraindication is about accumulation in a drug that is already given once daily and has a wide margin, not about hepatotoxicity. If you give it in that setting — and most of us do — reduce the dose as the label directs for milder disease, know that you are outside it, and say so in the notes.

What else it does

  • HypomagnesaemiaA class effect, listed in the Australian Product Information, and the reason a potassium that will not come up should prompt a magnesium level.
  • Clostridioides difficileIn REVISE, 1.2% against 0.7%, hazard ratio 1.78 (95% CI 0.96–3.29). Not significant, not reassuring either. The event rate is simply too low for a trial of five thousand to settle.
  • PneumoniaThe old worry, and the trials do not support it: ventilator-associated pneumonia 23.3% against 23.8% in REVISE.
  • Acute interstitial nephritisRare, idiosyncratic, listed, and worth a thought in the patient whose creatinine climbs with no other explanation and an eosinophilia.
  • ThrombophlebitisThe commonest adverse effect of the injection in its own label, which is a quietly useful thing to know about a drug that goes into a peripheral cannula.
  • ClopidogrelPantoprazole is the proton pump inhibitor of choice here, because it inhibits CYP2C19 least. The randomised evidence, from COGENT, used omeprazole and still found a large reduction in gastrointestinal bleeding (hazard ratio 0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.56) with no excess of cardiovascular events (0.99, 0.68–1.44). So the interaction is real in the laboratory and has never been convincingly real in a patient — and pantoprazole is how you avoid having the argument.
  • The urine drug screenThe belief that proton pump inhibitors cause false-positive cannabinoid screens is widespread and, as far as anyone can show, wrong. It appears to have come from an early manufacturer's insert that cited no data; direct laboratory work found no cross-reactivity at 10,000 ng/mL and none in thirty-two patient samples from people taking five different proton pump inhibitors. If you are about to explain a positive result that way, do not.

The errors that recur

  1. Prophylaxis at the treatment dose. Eight milligrams an hour is for a patient who has bled. It is not a more careful way of preventing bleeding, and the guideline specifically recommends against high-dose prophylaxis.
  2. The infusion nobody stopped on day four. Three days is the recommendation after endoscopic haemostasis. Day nine of an 8 mg/h infusion is not caution; it is an unreviewed order.
  3. The pantoprazole that followed the patient home. It is started for a reason that lasted thirty-six hours and dispensed for a decade. Write the stop on the ward transfer, not in the discharge summary.
  4. Crushing the tablet. A destroyed enteric coat, a dose that mostly never arrives, and a chart that says it was given.
  5. Treating melaena in a cirrhotic as an ulcer. The pantoprazole is easy and the terlipressin and the antibiotics are the ones that change the outcome.
  6. Reading the one-hour half-life as a short duration of action. It is the most common wrong answer in the viva and the reason people ask for a second dose at eight hours.
  7. Letting it count as an answer. A patient who bleeds on pantoprazole has a diagnosis you have not made yet. The drug is not the investigation.
For the First Part

Class and structure. A substituted benzimidazole, lipophilic weak base, given as an inactive prodrug. That last clause is the whole answer in miniature: everything interesting about this drug follows from the fact that it has to be activated by acid, and the only acid of that strength in the body is on the far side of the membrane it is trying to reach.

Dynamics. The drug is a weak base, so it crosses membranes freely while un-ionised and is trapped where it is protonated. The parietal cell secretory canaliculus sits at about pH 1, and the drug concentrates there roughly a thousandfold. Once protonated it rearranges to a reactive sulphenamide, which forms a covalent disulfide bond with cysteine residues on the α subunit of the gastric H+/K+ ATPase — Cys813 and Cys822 for pantoprazole. The pump is inactivated for good. Recovery requires synthesis of new pump protein, which is why the duration of effect is a property of the parietal cell and not of the drug.

And the point that earns the mark. Plasma half-life about one hour; acid suppression beyond twenty-four. If you can state that, say why, and then say what it implies — that steady-state inhibition takes several days of once-daily dosing to build and several days to wear off, and that measuring a plasma concentration would tell you nothing — you have answered the question three different examiners were going to ask.

Kinetics. Oral bioavailability about 77%. Volume of distribution roughly 11 to 24 L, essentially extracellular. Protein binding about 98%, mostly albumin. Clearance 7.6 to 14 L/h. Terminal half-life about one hour. Hepatic metabolism by O-demethylation through CYP2C19 and then sulfation, with a minor CYP3A4 contribution; metabolites inactive; about 71% recovered in urine and 18% in faeces, with no unchanged drug appearing renally.

The CYP2C19 question, which they like. Poor metabolisers — a minority of Europeans and a substantially larger minority of East Asian populations — have a half-life of three and a half to ten hours, and still need no dose adjustment. Ask why, and the answer is the dynamics again: the bond is irreversible, the dosing interval is already far longer than the half-life, and accumulation of a drug whose effect does not depend on its plasma concentration does not matter. The same reasoning explains why hepatic impairment, which raises exposure five- to sevenfold, is a labelling problem rather than a toxicity one.

Compare and contrast. Against the H2 receptor antagonists: competitive and reversible against one of three secretagogue pathways, so acid suppression is partial and tolerance develops within days; proton pump inhibitors act at the final common step, which is why they are better and why nothing is gained by combining them. Against potassium-competitive acid blockers: reversible, no acid activation needed, faster onset, no prodrug — a good one-line contrast to have ready if the examiner wants to know what is coming next.

One caution for candidates. The Australian Product Information is a registration document, not a pharmacology text, and the numbers above are drawn partly from it and partly from the manufacturer's clinical pharmacology data. Where a figure on this page is not in the Australian label, the verification record says where it came from.

Challenge for you

Go and count. How many patients on your unit right now are on pantoprazole, and how many of them have a reason you could write down?

It takes about four minutes with the drug chart. I would like to know the two numbers, and your unit size, and whether there is anything in your local policy that says when it stops.

And a second one: has anyone in your hospital ever audited how many patients leave on a proton pump inhibitor they arrived without? If so, I would like to hear what the number was — including if it was reassuring.
