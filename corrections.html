Corrections

What has been changed on this site, when, and why

Everything here is written by a fallible person, checked as carefully as I can manage, and published anyway. Some of it will turn out to be wrong.

This page exists so that when something is wrong, the fixing of it is visible rather than quiet. A site that never records a correction is not a site that has never made a mistake — it is a site that edits its errors away and hopes nobody noticed. I would rather you could see the repairs.

  • Anything that could affect care is fixed the same day — a dose, a threshold, an algorithm the wrong way round. If a proper correction will take longer than that, the page comes down until it is ready.
  • Corrections are dated and described here, with what the page used to say, so that anyone who read the earlier version can see what has changed.
  • If it went out in the newsletter, the correction goes in the next one. The people who saw the error are the people who need to see the fix.
  • Whoever found it gets the credit, by name if they are happy to be named. Finding an error in someone else's teaching material and saying so is a generous act, and it should not be anonymous unless the finder wants it to be.
  • Where something is genuinely contested rather than wrong, the page says so instead of picking a side quietly.

The record

  1. 28 October 2026

    The child who dies at ten at night: advice that only worked in office hours

    In Medicolegal No. 1 — The school-age child

    It said: for any child death, ask whether there is a child protection history, and ask the social work team to check, before assuming it is not coronial.

    It says now: the same sentence without the social work clause, followed by a new section on what to do when the child dies overnight — that there is nothing you have to decide at ten at night, only three things you must not do; that the report goes to police or a coroner rather than to social work; and that the Child Protection Helpline on 13 21 11 runs around the clock, as do the tertiary child protection consultants the page already listed.

    Why: nothing in the original was untrue, but it named the one route that is shut at the hour most of these deaths actually happen, and it could be read as wait for social work before deciding — which inverts the safe default. The page already carried the 24-hour numbers further up; it simply failed to connect them at the point of need.

    Found by Dr John Alexander, Intensive Care Registrar at Coffs Harbour Health Campus, who read the page and asked the obvious question nobody else had. It is the first correction on this site prompted by a reader, and it is exactly what this page is for. If something here does not survive contact with a night shift, please say so — a question like that makes the page better for everybody who reads it afterwards. The exchange is also in You Asked.

  2. 26 October 2026

    Neonatal resuscitation: the oxygen threshold, and a delivery room too cold

    In Mind Map #31 — Essentials of Neonatal Intensive Care, published August 2026

    It said: start in air at term, 21–30% under 32 weeks; and, in the golden hour box, hat, warmer, plastic wrap under 32 weeks, room at 23–25 °C.

    It says now: 21–30% under 35 weeks; and room at 26 °C or more if preterm.

    Why: ANZCOR sets the blended-oxygen recommendation at under 35 weeks, not 32. The range itself was right; the gestation was three weeks too low, which would have started a 33-weeker in air. The likeliest explanation is the sentence two lines below, where 32 weeks genuinely is the threshold — for the polyethylene wrap. On the room, ANZCOR asks for an ambient temperature of at least 26 °C for the preterm newborn. 23–25 °C is a common figure for a general delivery room, but that sentence was explicitly about the preterm baby, and so described a room too cold for the infant in it.

    Found during the number verification project, checking twelve figures on that map against ANZCOR Guideline 13. Two further figures were qualified rather than corrected at the same time. The tube depth formula, weight plus six, is in the guideline but overestimates by a full centimetre at 500 g — the guideline's own table is more precise in the smallest infants, and the map now says so. And the umbilical venous catheter depth, which has no ANZCOR source at all, now names the landmark it is measured from.

  3. 23 October 2026

    Paracetamol: a modified-release rule stated as a general one

    In Drugstore No. 2 — Paracetamol

    It said: above 500 mg/kg or 30 g is a massive overdose, where absorption may continue for up to twenty-four hours and increased acetylcysteine doses are used.

    It says now: that threshold, and the prolonged absorption, belong to modified-release preparations. For immediate-release paracetamol the guideline sets no dose threshold at all — the trigger for an increased acetylcysteine dose is a concentration more than double the nomogram line, which doubles the second bag to 200 mg/kg.

    Why: the Australian and New Zealand guideline is explicit that the 30 g / 500 mg/kg rule applies to modified-release overdoses. Patients needing increased acetylcysteine after an immediate-release overdose have usually taken 30 g or more, but that is a description of them, not the thing that decides. Stating it as a dose threshold risks reaching for a history instead of a concentration.

    Found during the number verification project. The intravenous dose for infants under 10 kg was tidied at the same time — the product information says four to six hourly, not six — and that row now also records that the approved dose and the NSW TAG recommendation differ twofold, rather than quietly following one of them.

  4. 23 October 2026

    Adrenaline autoinjector weight bands

    In Drugstore No. 1 — Adrenaline, published September 2026

    It said: autoinjectors, 0.15 mg from about 15 kg, 0.3 mg from about 30 kg.

    It says now: 0.15 mg for 7.5–20 kg, 0.3 mg from 20 kg, 0.5 mg from 50 kg.

    Why: both thresholds were too high, and the 0.5 mg device was missing altogether. ASCIA’s device prescription guideline and the Royal Children’s Hospital agree on 7.5–20 kg, over 20 kg and over 50 kg. As written, a ten-kilogram child would have read as too small for a junior device when they qualify for one, and a child of twenty-two would have been left on 150 microgram when they should have 300.

    Found during the first pass of the number verification project, which is to say by going looking rather than by a reader finding it — the less creditable of the two routes.
