Late middle age

Forty-five to sixty — where the illness that will kill them introduces itself, and still nobody has written anything down

Forty-five to sixty is the decade and a half in which the illness that will eventually kill most of these patients turns up and introduces itself. Usually in our unit, usually at night, and usually without having been named before.

That is the whole character of the band. The young adult arrives with something that happened to them; early middle age is where what happened and what grew cross over. Here, what grew has won. Coronary heart disease is the leading cause of death in men aged 45 to 64 and lung cancer the second; for women in the same band it is breast cancer and lung cancer. None of that is a surprise. What surprises people is how often the admission is the first anyone has heard of it.

They also still look well, which is the trap. Reserve has genuinely started to go — diastolic function, renal reserve, muscle, the chest wall — but it goes quietly, and a fifty-four-year-old who cycles to work can hold a normal set of observations over a great deal of trouble. The history is the diagnostic test in this band, and most of it has never been written down: the drinking, the snoring, the twenty-five pack-years, the stonemason’s decade, the tablet somebody stopped.

And nobody has had the conversation. A fifty-five-year-old with advanced chronic disease generally has no ceiling of care recorded, no substitute decision-maker named, and a family who believe, reasonably, that this is a one-off. Three stations: what it is, what nobody wrote down, and what now.

How to use a mind map

Do not read it like a chapter. Scan it. Let your eye go where it wants, read three boxes that catch it, and stop. You are not trying to finish it and you are certainly not trying to memorise it.

Then come back. Before a shift on which this subject might come up, or after a case that went a particular way, or while waiting for a retrieval team. Thirty seconds at a time, often, beats an hour once.

It works the way a painting in a corridor works. Nobody sits down and studies the painting they walk past every morning, and yet after a year they could tell you what is in it, where the light falls, and exactly what is on the left-hand side — and they would notice at once if something had changed. That noticing is the point. These maps are not built to be recalled on demand; they are built so that when the thing in front of you does not fit, something quietly objects.

On a phone: turn it sideways, or scroll the map across. Pinch to zoom on any section.

Challenge for you

What did the history turn up that the notes did not?

This band rewards asking more than it rewards treating, and the question that pays best is almost always one that takes thirty seconds. The partner who says he stops breathing all night. The daughter who says it is more like a bottle, not a couple of glasses. The brother who mentions the stonemasonry.

So: a time in a patient between about forty-five and sixty when something you asked, or somebody volunteered, changed the diagnosis or the plan. What was the question, who answered it, and what did it change? And the other half if you have it — the one nobody asked, and what that cost.
