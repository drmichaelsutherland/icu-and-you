Rejoice, we conquer

The most famous last words in the history of running. Nobody said them.

Who this is for

Anyone at all. No medicine is assumed and none is required. This one is a story first, about something everybody half-knows and almost nobody has checked, and the useful part arrives at the end rather than the beginning.

I am an intensive care specialist. I have also, for reasons that will become clear, spent a certain amount of my life on this particular dead Greek.

You know the story. A Greek soldier runs from the battlefield at Marathon to Athens, bursts in on the assembly, gasps out that the Persians have been beaten, and drops dead on the floor. It is why there is a race called a marathon. It is on tea towels.

I have a particular interest in it. Some years ago I ran the Athens Marathon in period attire, a sentence I am obliged to follow immediately with the observation that period attire is a poor choice of thing to run forty-two kilometres in under a Greek sun, and that this had not occurred to me in advance. I went on to write a book about the man. So I have had rather more reason than most to go back and look at where the story actually comes from.

It comes from almost nowhere.

What the oldest source actually says

Herodotus is writing within living memory of the battle, close enough that the sons of the men who fought there were walking around Athens and able to argue with him. He is the only source we have anywhere near the event. And he does have a runner.

But the run is the wrong run.

His runner is a professional — a hemerodromos, a day-runner, a man who made covering ground his calling. And he is sent before the battle, not after it, and not to Athens. He is sent from Athens to Sparta to ask the Spartans to come and help, across the mountains of the Peloponnese, and he is in Sparta the day after he left.

Herodotus gives no distance, incidentally. He gives a time. The mileage everybody quotes comes from much later writers, and the best modern answer is simply that the race now run over that route is two hundred and forty-six kilometres long.

On the way, up on Mount Parthenion above Tegea, he meets the god Pan — or rather, and this is the sort of thing I enjoy, Herodotus is careful to say that the runner said he met the god Pan. Pan called him by name and asked why the Athenians paid him no attention, when he had always been well disposed towards them, had often been useful, and intended to be useful again. The runner delivered that message too, when he got home. The Athenians, being sensible people, built Pan a precinct under the Acropolis and honoured him every year with a sacrifice and a torch-race.

Notice what is not in any of that. There is no run from Marathon to Athens. There are no dying words. There is no dying. The one ancient writer in a position to know tells us about a longer and frankly more impressive run, and then lets the man live.

It is worth sitting with how strange that is. If a man had run in from the battlefield and died on the floor of the assembly in front of the entire Athenian government, Herodotus — who never knowingly left out a good story, and who cheerfully reports gods, dreams and giant gold-digging ants — would have told us. He had the space. He had the inclination. He did not have the story.

So when did the man start dying?

Later. And never the same way twice.

Plutarch, writing about six hundred years afterwards, gives it twice over and commits to neither version. One authority of his — Heracleides of Pontus, who is himself writing a century and a half after the battle, so the story is old even here — says the runner was a man called Thersippus. Most historians, Plutarch says, hold it was a man called Eucles, who ran in from the battle still in his armour, burst in on the leading men of the city, said one thing, and died on the spot.

Neither of them is Philippides. Neither of them is the man from Herodotus at all.

And what did he say? Here the story does something I find quietly delightful. The manuscripts of Plutarch have the runner say “Rejoice” — the ordinary Greek hello — and then “we rejoice”, which is the ordinary Greek reply to it. He gasps out a greeting, answers it himself, and dies. It is a pun, and a rather good one. The version you will find in most English translations, where he announces a victory, rests on editors changing that second word, and at least one modern scholar thinks they should not have.

The announcement belongs to Lucian, writing later still — six and a half centuries after the battle, which is further from Marathon than we are from Shakespeare. Lucian's runner says “Joy to you, we are winning”, and dies on the word. And Lucian is the one who finally welds Herodotus' name onto Plutarch's corpse: he calls the dying man Philippides. Everything everyone knows arrives, assembled, at that moment.

One more, because it is the most-repeated false quotation in the whole business. The Greek word you will see on race medals, running shirts and marathon websites — nenikēkamen, “we have conquered” — appears in no ancient source at all. Lucian's runner uses the present tense. He does not say we have won. He says we are winning, which is both better Greek and a far better line, because it is what a man who has just arrived actually knows.

And the English words everybody knows — the ones at the top of this page — are not ancient either. They are Robert Browning's. He published a poem in 1879 that took the long run to Sparta and the short run from Marathon, which he knew perfectly well were two different errands, and fused them into one glorious doomed afternoon for one man. He prints Lucian's Greek as his epigraph, so he is not hiding anything. He also lifts Pan out of Arcadia altogether and puts him on a mountain in Attica, a relocation one exasperated classicist compared to taking a short cut from Liverpool to London by way of Edinburgh.

It is a very good poem. It is also, in the English-speaking world, where very nearly everybody got the story — and fifteen years later a French philologist called Michel Bréal wrote to Baron de Coubertin suggesting that the new Olympics might run a race from Marathon to Athens, and here we all are. Whether Bréal had read Browning, nobody has ever been able to show.

Nobody said it. A Victorian poet said it, on his behalf, two and a half thousand years late.

Why we wanted it to be true

Because it is a marvellous shape for a story, and because it says something we half-believe about effort — that there is a price, that the body can be spent like money, that a man can give everything and have the giving be the thing that kills him. It is heroic. It is tidy. It arrives at the finish line.

And that, finally, is where I have a professional objection, because I have spent forty years watching who actually collapses, and it is almost never the person the story would have picked.

What actually happens to fit young people

Here is the part worth keeping.

When a young person dies suddenly of a cardiac cause in this country, the setting is usually not the sports field. It is a bed, a sofa, an ordinary Tuesday. In a careful Australian and New Zealand study of nearly five hundred such deaths in people aged one to thirty-five, most happened at rest. The playing field is where we notice these deaths, because there is a crowd and a camera and a terrible silence afterwards. It is not where most of them happen.

The same study found something else, which I think the public is rarely told and deserves to be. In about four cases in ten, a full post-mortem examination finds nothing. No blockage, no scarred muscle, no explanation at all. Families are sent away with the worst news of their lives and no reason attached to it. Some of those are genetic, and testing the family afterwards matters enormously — but a large number simply remain unexplained, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a tidier story than the evidence supports.

So the sentence but he was so fit is not the objection people think it is. Fitness is a wonderful thing, and I would not talk anybody out of it for a moment. It is not a certificate.

If you remember one thing from this page

Fainting during exertion and fainting after you stop are two completely different events, and they are treated completely differently.

Fainting after you stop — at the end of the run, standing at the finish, waiting for your breath — is usually the circulation catching up with you. Your legs have stopped pumping blood back up to your heart and the pressure drops. It is common and it is generally benign.

Fainting while you are still going is different, and cardiologists treat it as a warning sign, because for some of the conditions that cause sudden death in young people it is the only warning anybody ever gets. If it happens to you or to someone you love, it is worth a doctor's appointment and almost certainly a tracing of the heart. Not next year. This month.

And since we are on the subject of long races, the thing that most often hurts people in them is not the heart at all. It is heat, and it is water — specifically, too much water. Drinking steadily through a long event because somebody once told you to stay ahead of your thirst can dilute the salt in your blood far enough to cause fits and, occasionally, to kill. When Boston marathon runners were studied in 2002, something like one in eight finished with a low sodium level, and the clearest signal was having gained weight over the course of the race — which is to say having drunk more than you lost. Thirst is a remarkably good instrument. It has had a long time to get good at the job.

If somebody collapses in front of you
  • If they are not responding and not breathing normally, treat it as a cardiac arrest. Do not wait to be sure.
  • Call 000. Put the phone on speaker — the person on the other end will talk you through it, and they are very good at it.
  • Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, and keep going. Hands only is fine. Doing it imperfectly is enormously better than not doing it.
  • Send somebody else for the nearest defibrillator. They are in shopping centres, gyms, clubs and sporting grounds, and they are designed to be used by people who have never seen one. It will tell you what to do out loud.

Of everything that can still be changed once somebody has collapsed, the one that matters most is whether a bystander started before the ambulance arrived. That bystander is statistically likely to be somebody exactly like you, on an ordinary day, with no training and no warning.

And the costume

I finished, since you ask. Slowly, and with a new and lasting respect for the ancient Greek approach to textiles. The thing that nearly stopped me was not my heart. It was the long climb out of Marathon that goes on, and on, and does not care how you feel about it, and a growing sense that I had made a series of decisions nobody had asked me to make.

But I was running a route that a real man really did run, in some direction, for some reason, at some point in 490 BC. We have lost almost everything about him, starting with his name. The two main families of Herodotus manuscripts disagree — one says Philippides, the other Pheidippides — and the editions on a classicist's desk today mostly print Philippides, which is awkward, because Pheidippides is the one on the T-shirts. And if you prefer Plutarch's account you are not looking for either of them; you are looking for Thersippus, or for Eucles. Four names for one man, and possibly for no man.

What we have kept instead is a Victorian poem and a world-wide habit of getting up at five in the morning.

He probably did not die. I would rather he did not. And the useful thing he left us is not the lesson the story teaches — that effort is dangerous — but the one underneath it, which is that a body will go an extraordinarily long way, and that when one unexpectedly does not, it is usually for a reason that had nothing to do with how hard it was asked to work.

Ask me anything

Really — anything. There is no such thing as a question that is too basic.

This one invites two in particular. Is there a piece of medical lore you grew up with that you have never actually checked, and would like me to? People send me these and they are among my favourite things to chase down. And second — has anyone close to you ever collapsed, or fainted, in a way that was never properly explained? You do not have to tell me the details. I am interested in how often the explaining never happens.

I cannot give advice about a particular person or a particular illness, and I will say so plainly if that is what is being asked. Everything else is fair game.

The ancient material is all readable for nothing, and I would encourage it. The Sparta run, Pan on Mount Parthenion and the arrival “on the day after” are Herodotus, Histories 6.105–106; the man is not heard of again. Thersippus, Eucles and the greeting answered by a greeting are Plutarch, On the Fame of the Athenians 347C, where the Loeb’s “we are victorious” follows an editorial emendation of the manuscripts’ χαίρομεν. “Joy to you, we are winning” is Lucian, A Slip of the Tongue in Greeting 3. On the name, the development of the legend and the case against that emendation, the fullest modern treatment is Valeria Melis, Classica Cracoviensia 23 (2020) 25–54; current standard editions (Wilson’s Oxford text, 2015; Hornblower and Pelling, 2017) print Philippides. Browning’s “Pheidippides” is in Dramatic Idyls (1879), with Lucian’s Greek as its epigraph; the complaint about his geography is F. G. Allinson, Classical Weekly 24.19 (1931), and Owen Rees for Bad Ancient is a good short way in. Michel Bréal’s proposal was a letter to Coubertin of 15 September 1894, described in The Sport Journal; no documented link between Browning and Bréal exists, which is why I have not claimed one. The modern race over the Sparta route is the Spartathlon, 246 km. On the medicine: sudden cardiac death in the young is Bagnall and colleagues, a prospective study of 490 deaths at ages one to thirty-five in Australia and New Zealand, New England Journal of Medicine 2016. Syncope during against after exertion follows the American College of Cardiology’s review of the syncopal athlete. The Boston figures are Almond and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine 2005, on the 2002 field. And for completeness: there is no credible medical literature on what the runner died of, because there is no body, no witness and no agreed name. Anyone who tells you it was his heart is guessing, and so is anyone who tells you it was the heat.
