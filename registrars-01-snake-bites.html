Australian snake bites

Most people bitten by an Australian snake are never envenomed. The whole job is working out, quickly and without a reliable test, which ones are

A teaching presentation given at Coffs Harbour Health Campus by Dr Maheshani Wijenatha, Intensive Care Registrar. Moderator: Dr Azad. Set for the web by ICU AND YOU with her permission; the clinical content and its selection are hers.

A new group

About three thousand people a year are bitten by a snake in Australia. Roughly one in twenty receives antivenom. Between one and four die — most often from a brown snake, and most often early, before anybody has had the chance to do very much at all.

Those numbers carry the shape of the problem. The denominator is large, the treated fraction is small, and the deaths cluster at the very front of the timeline. This is not a disease where thoroughness compensates for delay.

What the venom does

It helps to separate what happens at the bite from what happens everywhere else, because the first is mostly irrelevant and the second is the entire clinical problem.

Locally, Australian elapid bites are unimpressive. There may be scratches, a pair of punctures, mild swelling, sometimes nothing visible at all. Tissue necrosis of the kind seen with vipers elsewhere in the world is not a feature here. A benign-looking bite site excludes nothing.

Systemically, four syndromes matter, in roughly descending order of frequency: venom-induced consumption coagulopathy, neurotoxicity, myotoxicity and acute kidney injury, with thrombotic microangiopathy and early cardiovascular collapse as the serious outliers.

The earliest symptoms are maddeningly nonspecific — headache, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, diaphoresis, blurred vision, collapse. In a well-looking patient these are easy to attribute to anxiety, and that is the trap. Headache and vomiting after a possible bite are systemic envenoming until the bloods say otherwise.

Before hospital

Pressure immobilisation is the only first aid that has ever been shown to help, and the detail matters more than the enthusiasm.

  • A broad elastic bandage, about 15 cm wide, applied as firmly as for a sprain — snug, not arterial.
  • Start over the bite and bandage the whole limb, then splint it.
  • Immobilise the patient, not just the limb. Walking defeats the point.
  • Apply it to anyone presenting within four hours of the bite, including those who arrive without one.
  • Do not wash the bite site, do not cut, do not suck, do not apply a tourniquet.
The bandage is not a parking space

A correctly applied pressure bandage buys time. A tight bandage left on for many hours during a long country transfer causes harm of its own. If you are receiving a patient who has been bandaged since a property two hours away, the bandage is now a clinical problem as well as a treatment.

At the door: three questions

Everything that follows in the first hour is an attempt to answer three things.

  1. Is this patient envenomed? Not “was this patient bitten” — the majority of bites deliver no significant venom load, and those patients need observation rather than treatment.
  2. If so, which syndrome? The coagulation screen usually answers this before the examination does.
  3. Does this patient need antivenom, and is it still early enough to matter?
The kit you no longer reach for

Venom detection kits are no longer recommended for the management of snake bite in Australia. The false-positive rate in patients who turn out not to be envenomed has been reported around 36%, and the result almost never changed what was given. Currently available point-of-care devices are not recommended either. Geography, syndrome and the bloods are what choose the antivenom now.

The bloods

Baseline, and repeated: coagulation screen with INR, APTT, fibrinogen and D-dimer; full blood count with film; creatine kinase; electrolytes, urea and creatinine; and a venous gas if the patient is unwell.

The thresholds worth carrying in your head:

  • INR above 1.2 in this context is abnormal and means coagulopathy until proven otherwise. This is not the threshold you use anywhere else in the hospital.
  • D-dimer above 2.5 mg/L suggests partial consumption coagulopathy; in complete VICC it runs a hundred to a thousand times the assay cut-off.
  • CK above 1,000 U/L points to myotoxicity.
  • The thrombotic microangiopathy triad — falling platelets, rising creatinine, red cell fragments on the film — is the one that declares itself late and does the long-term damage.

D-dimer is useful for making the diagnosis and useless for following it, because it stays high for days after the coagulopathy has resolved.

Reading the coagulopathy

Two quite different things get called coagulopathy, and conflating them leads to the wrong expectations about antivenom.

The last row is the one that changes behaviour. Persistent coagulopathy twelve hours after a brown snake bite is expected physiology, not a failure of treatment, and it is not an indication for more antivenom.

On the mid north coast the practical list is short: brown, red-bellied black, rough-scaled, and Hoplocephalus. Tiger snake sits at the southern edge of its range.

Neurotoxicity and myotoxicity

Neurotoxicity is descending and starts with the cranial nerves — ptosis first, then ophthalmoplegia, then bulbar weakness, then the diaphragm. Ptosis in someone who may have been bitten is not a cosmetic finding.

The practical distinction is between presynaptic and postsynaptic blockade. Postsynaptic toxins are competitive and may reverse with antivenom. Presynaptic toxins destroy the nerve terminal, and once established no amount of antivenom reverses them — recovery takes days to weeks and depends on regeneration. That is the clinical argument for giving antivenom early rather than confidently.

Myotoxicity presents as muscle pain and tenderness, a rising CK, and myoglobinuria, with hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury as the consequences to watch.

Antivenom: when

Antivenom is indicated for clinical or laboratory evidence of systemic envenoming. Timing drives effectiveness more than dose does.

  • Ideally within two to three hours of the bite for most snake types.
  • Between six and twelve hours it is unlikely to be effective, but should still be considered.
  • Beyond twelve hours it should not be given.

Early cardiovascular collapse is the most common cause of death from snake bite in Australia, which is why the window is as unforgiving as it is.

Antivenom: which, and how much

Where the snake is unidentified, one vial of brown snake antivenom and one vial of tiger snake antivenom cover every snake in most of Australia — 4,000 units in total. Polyvalent is the alternative where death adder, taipan or mulga are in the geographical frame.

Dose is about the snake, not the patient

There is no weight-based calculation. A child receives the same dose as an adult, because the dose neutralises the venom the snake injected, and the snake did not adjust for the patient. One vial neutralises the venom from one snake; extra vials do not hasten recovery, and there is no role for redosing.

Administration: dilute the vial roughly 1:10 in 0.9% sodium chloride — around 1:5 where fluid overload is a genuine concern — and give it intravenously over about fifteen minutes. In cardiac arrest attributed to envenoming, it is given undiluted by rapid push.

Be ready to treat anaphylaxis before you start the infusion, every time. Adrenaline drawn up, not merely available.

When it goes wrong

Antivenom is horse-derived immunoglobulin and behaves accordingly.

  • Hypersensitivity reactions in around 25%, most of them mild.
  • Severe anaphylaxis in 2–3%, which is why the adrenaline is drawn up.
  • Routine premedication is not recommended. It does not prevent the reactions and carries its own risk.
  • Serum sickness at four to fourteen days — fever, rash, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy. Treated with prednisolone 25–50 mg daily. Every patient who receives antivenom goes home knowing to expect it and where to go, and their GP should be told in writing.

For major bleeding in VICC, fresh frozen plasma can be given to replace consumed factors — but not within six hours of the bite, while circulating prothrombin activators are still present to consume whatever you give.

Observation and discharge

Anyone with a suspected snake bite is observed for at least twelve hours, with coagulation studies and a neurological examination repeated at six and twelve hours after the bite.

The pressure bandage comes off once the patient is somewhere with antivenom and a laboratory. If envenomed, it comes off after antivenom has been given; then bloods and neurological examination are repeated an hour later, because removing the bandage can release a further bolus of venom.

Discharge in daylight hours, when envenoming is resolving and the patient is well — a point that sounds like bureaucracy and is not. It means the patient is not driving home to an empty house in the dark at the hour when deterioration would be least likely to be noticed.

Eight things to take away

  1. Most bites are not envenomings, and most patients need observation rather than antivenom.
  2. A normal-looking bite site excludes nothing.
  3. Headache and vomiting are systemic envenoming until the bloods disagree.
  4. Pressure immobilisation of the whole limb, within four hours, and immobilise the patient too.
  5. Venom detection kits are no longer part of the pathway.
  6. INR above 1.2 is abnormal here.
  7. Antivenom early or not at all — and the paediatric dose is the adult dose.
  8. Twelve hours of observation, bloods at six and twelve, and warn every treated patient about serum sickness.
A note added in setting this for the web

Two small refinements against the most recent published reviews, neither of which changes the management. Current sources give the ideal antivenom window as three hours rather than two for most snake types; and the six-to-twelve-hour period is now described as unlikely to be effective but still worth considering, which is a little softer than a flat cut-off. The twelve-hour limit stands.
