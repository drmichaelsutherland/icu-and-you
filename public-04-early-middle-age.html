ICU and You  ·  For the general public  ·  Number 4

It was always going to be a Tuesday

What actually brings people between thirty and forty-five into intensive care. It is almost never the thing you are afraid of.

Who this is for

Anyone at all. No medicine is assumed and none is required. This one is for the age group the site is spending a fortnight on — roughly thirty to forty-five — and for the people who love them, which is a considerably larger group.

I am an intensive care specialist in a regional unit. Some of what follows is the part everybody already knows and nobody acts on. Some of it is the part almost nobody knows. And the last section is the one I would most like you to have read before you need it.

The statistics and the telephone numbers here are Australian. If you are reading this somewhere else, the shape of it will be much the same; those will not.

People ask me what I see. They expect the answer to be dramatic, and I think they are usually a little disappointed.

Not the shark. Not the snake, which Australians living overseas are asked about constantly and which has almost never been the reason. Not the light aircraft, not the cliff, not the tropical fever carried home in a suitcase. Those exist. I have seen them. I could tell you about them for an hour and you would be entertained, and you would learn nothing you could use.

What I see, in people between thirty and forty-five, is a Tuesday.

An ordinary Tuesday, in an ordinary week, in a life that was going along perfectly well. And almost always, when you go back through it, what happened on the Tuesday was settled some years earlier by a series of things so small that nobody involved can remember deciding any of them.

The fifteen years nobody writes down

Here is the odd thing about this age group, and it is why I am giving it a fortnight of its own.

At thirty-five you feel exactly as you did at twenty-five. Not approximately. Exactly. You can still do the run, carry the toddler, work the long day, and recover from the late night with nothing worse than a complaint. Nothing in your body is telling you anything has changed.

Meanwhile, quietly, things are changing.

Blood pressure drifts up a little each year. The weight arrives so gradually that no single week is the week it happened. The drinking that was a university habit becomes a Wednesday habit. The sleep gets worse, and is blamed on the children, who are genuinely responsible for some of it. None of this hurts. That is the entire difficulty with it: there is no symptom, because none of these things has one.

Two in five Australian adults have high blood pressure. Of those, almost two in three do not know they have it. And among the eighteen to thirty-four-year-olds who do have it, fewer than one in five has it treated and controlled.

Stop on the middle number. It is not that people are managing it badly. It is that most of them have never been told there is anything to manage.

High blood pressure does not feel like anything. It does not give you headaches, or a red face, or a sense that something is wrong. People are certain it does, and they are thinking of the afternoon they were furious about something. The only way to find out before it has done any damage is to put a cuff on an arm, which takes about a minute, and which plenty of adults in this age group have not had done since they were last pregnant or last needed a medical for work.

So that is the reminder. You already knew it, and you have very likely not had it measured.

What actually brings people in

If I set aside the units that only take one kind of patient, and think about the people who come through an ordinary general unit at this age, the list is short and it is not mysterious.

The car. Injury and the other external causes — crashes, falls, poisonings — are together the leading cause of death in every Australian age group up to forty-four. The common thread at this age is not recklessness. It is being tired, being late, and being four minutes from home on a road you have driven nine hundred times.

The drink. Not usually as a dramatic event. Usually as a quantity, applied steadily over fifteen years, to a liver, a pancreas, a heart and a set of reflexes. The Australian guideline is no more than ten standard drinks a week, and no more than four on any one day. I have yet to meet a person outside medicine who could tell me that number. It is not a wall with safe on one side. It is a slope, and all the number does is tell you roughly where on it you are standing.

The infection you worked through. This is the one that catches the competent. A chest infection, a skin infection, a tooth, a urinary infection, in a person with a deadline and a reputation for not making a fuss. The ones who arrive very late are nearly always the ones who were very reliable.

The first heart event. In Australian men, coronary heart disease becomes the leading cause of death from forty-five. It does not arrive at forty-five. It arrives having been built, piece by piece, during the fifteen years before it.

And then there is the one that leads the whole list, and that hardly anyone puts on it.

Those external causes are a group. Taken singly, the most common cause of death in Australia between fifteen and forty-four is suicide, and between twenty-five and forty-four it accounts for about one death in five.

I am not going to dress that up, and I am not going to leave it as a statistic either. People in this band are at the point of maximum load — work, money, young children, ageing parents, a relationship under strain — and at the point of minimum willingness to say that they are not coping, because everybody is depending on them and it does not feel like an option. That combination is the risk. Not weakness. Load, plus silence.

If that is you, the thing to know is that this responds to treatment, that people come through it every day who were certain they would not, and that the first conversation is far less difficult than you are imagining. Lifeline is 13 11 14 or text 0477 13 11 14, and Beyond Blue is 1300 22 4636. Both are twenty-four hours, both will talk to anyone, and neither requires you to be in a crisis to be allowed to ring.

And if it is someone you love: ask them directly. The fear that asking will plant the idea is one of the most durable pieces of folklore there is, and it is wrong — asking does not raise the risk. The question people regret is the one they decided would be awkward, and never asked.

Four things you may not know, and probably should

  • Your medicine list is medical informationAt three in the morning nobody can look up what you take. Your pharmacy is shut, your general practitioner is asleep, and the hospital record, if there is one, is years out of date. A photograph of your boxes, or a typed list in your phone, is the most useful thing you can carry into a hospital — and in this age band it matters more than it did ten years ago, because more people are on long-term medicines by this age, and the medicine is sometimes part of the problem rather than part of the background.
  • A normal blood sugar does not always mean what you thinkIf you take one of the newer tablets for diabetes, heart failure or kidney disease — the ones whose names end in -gliflozin — you can become seriously ill with ketoacidosis while your blood sugar reading looks perfectly fine. Two practical consequences. If you are vomiting, not eating, or unwell, say out loud that you take it. And if you are having an operation, ask well in advance when to stop it, because there is a rule about that and it is easy to miss.
  • Chest pain in a woman in her thirties is not nothingA heart attack at that age is often not a blocked artery at all. It is a tear in the wall of one, it happens overwhelmingly to women, it has little to do with cholesterol or lifestyle, and some of the usual treatments for a heart attack can make it worse. We call it anxiety, or reflux, or a panic attack, more often than we should. If you are told it is probably nothing and you are not reassured, say so, in those words.
  • “Previously fit and well” is a sentence to be careful withIt appears at the top of almost every referral in this age group, and what it usually means is that nobody has checked. It is also how a decision gets made without anybody making one — he’s young, he’ll be fine sounds like a clinical judgement, and often stands in for one.
If you remember one thing from this page

Four numbers, and you can have all of them inside a month: your blood pressure, your weight, your cholesterol and your blood sugar. One appointment. No preparation, no drama, no particular courage required.

Everything above is a long way of saying that in this fifteen-year stretch your body stops reporting to you, and those four numbers are what it has instead of symptoms. Most people in this band have not had them measured since they last needed a form signed.

If someone you love ends up in intensive care

This is the part I would most like you to have read in advance, because nobody takes anything in on the first day.

The room will be worse than you expect and the news will usually be better than the room. There will be more equipment than seems reasonable, and a great deal of it is monitoring rather than treatment. The alarms mostly mean that a number has stepped outside a range somebody set, which is how they are designed to behave. If something were urgent, you would see people walking quickly, and you would not have to work it out from the noise.

A few things that are worth knowing before you are standing there.

  • Sedation is not sleepIt is a drugged state, and people drift up through it without looking any different. Assume you are heard. Say the ordinary things — who is at school, who fed the dog, who sends their love. Familiar voices genuinely help, and the ordinary is more use than the profound.
  • Confusion is expectedA great many patients become muddled, and some are frightened and convinced of things that are not happening. It is common, it is usually temporary, and it is not madness and not a sign of brain damage. Your calm presence and a window with daylight in it do more than you would think.
  • Appoint one personOne family member who rings, and who then tells everyone else. It is kinder to the family and it is much safer for the patient, because the story stops drifting as it is relayed. Ask for a time of day when you can expect a call, and get the name of the person to ask for.
  • Bring the medicine listYes, this again. The family who walk in with a photograph of the boxes change what happens in the first hour.
  • Write your questions downYou will not remember them. You will also not remember the answers, so take notes, or bring somebody whose job is to take them.
  • You may be asked what they would have wantedNot because anybody is giving up, but because in this age group it has usually never been discussed — so when I ask, I am asking you to guess, at the worst possible moment. Knowing the answer is a kindness you can only give in advance.
  • Eat something, and go home and sleepYou are no use to anyone on day nine if you have not slept since day one, and day nine is a real possibility. Nothing will be decided in the hours you are away, and the staff will ring you.

And one thing about afterwards, because it is routinely a shock. People do not leave intensive care and resume. They come out weak, out of breath on a flight of stairs, with a memory that is not working properly and a mood that is not their own, and that can run for months. It is so usual that it has a name: post-intensive care syndrome. It is not a sign that something went wrong, and it is not weakness in somebody who has always been strong. Knowing it is coming helps more than anything else I can tell a family on the way out the door.

One more thing worth saying out loud in this age group, and it is the one most often missed. There are usually children. Nobody asks about them, because it is not on any form, and the ward will not know unless somebody says. Say it. Say who is at home, and who is looking after them, and whether that arrangement will last the week. It is the question whose answer most often turns out to need somebody to act on it.

Take home
  1. Get the four numbers. Blood pressure, weight, cholesterol, blood sugar. One appointment, this month. If you have no general practitioner, that is the first thing to fix.
  2. Know the alcohol number. Ten standard drinks a week, four on any one day. Work out roughly where you are. Nobody needs to be told the answer but you.
  3. Carry your medicine list. A photograph of the boxes, on your phone, today.
  4. If you are ill and not getting better, get seen — and stop working while you do.
  5. Do not let “probably nothing” be the end of it if you are not reassured. Say that you are not reassured. It is a legitimate sentence and it changes consultations.
  6. Ask the direct question of anyone you are worried about. It does not plant the idea. Lifeline 13 11 14, text 0477 13 11 14, Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636.
  7. Tell the people close to you what you would want if you could not speak for yourself. Once, badly, over dinner, is infinitely better than never.
  8. If someone you love is admitted: one spokesperson, a written list of questions, the medicine list, and say who is at home and who is looking after the children. Then go home and sleep.

And the Tuesday

None of this is a warning about how fragile you are. The opposite, really. People in this age band have more physiological reserve than they will ever have again, which is exactly why they can carry an untreated blood pressure for a decade without noticing, and exactly why they arrive looking well when they are not.

The reserve is real. It is just not infinite, and it is quiet about where it has got to.

So, no, it was never going to be the shark. It was going to be an ordinary Tuesday. Some of what landed on it had been accumulating quietly for fifteen years. Some had been building for months, in somebody carrying more than they had told anyone. And some of it was four minutes from home on a familiar road at the end of a long day.

None of the three is as fixed as it looks from inside it. One takes an appointment. One takes a sentence, said out loud, to somebody. And one takes stopping the car twenty minutes short of home, which is the hardest of the three and the only one that costs nothing.

Four numbers. One appointment. Not next year.

Ask me anything

Really — anything. There is no such thing as a question that is too basic.

This one invites two in particular. If you have ever sat beside someone in an intensive care unit, what did nobody tell you that you wish somebody had? I would like to put the answers into a future page, and the ones that come from families are better than the ones that come from us. And second — if you are in this age band and you have had none of the four numbers measured, I would quite like to know what has got in the way. I am not asking in order to tell you off. I am asking because the answer is probably fixable and I do not know what it is.

I cannot give advice about a particular person or a particular illness, and I will say so plainly if that is what is being asked. Everything else is fair game.
