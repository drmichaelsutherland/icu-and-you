Color Atlas of Physiology

Silbernagl and Despopoulos — mind maps before anybody called them that, and a preface you do not forget

About this series

A new column, for books that have made a difference to how we teach, train or practise in intensive care. Not textbooks, or not only textbooks — anything that changed how somebody thinks about the work. Some will be old. Some will not be medicine at all.

I am starting with mine, but the point of the series is what other people have to offer, and there is an invitation at the foot of the page. Send me the book that did it for you.

Stefan Silbernagl and Agamemnon Despopoulos. Color Atlas of Physiology. Thieme, Stuttgart and New York. Seventh English edition, 2015. First published in German in 1979.

I found it the way you used to find books: browsing a medical bookshop as a resident, with no particular plan, and pulling something off a shelf because the spine looked interesting. I have bought a great many books more systematically since and remember almost none of them as well.

It is a small book, and the format is the whole idea. One concept, one double page. Text on the left, and on the right a full-colour plate that carries the argument rather than decorating it. Turn to countercurrent multiplication and there it is, entire, in front of you. Turn to the cardiac cycle and the same. Nothing is continued overleaf.

These are mind maps, drawn thirty years before anybody thought to call them that.

That is not a loose comparison. The plates do exactly what a mind map does — a central idea, its branches laid out spatially, the relationships carried by position and colour rather than by prose — and they were doing it in 1979, long before the technique had a name or a following. When I build the mind maps for this site I am, consciously, borrowing from a book I bought as a resident.

And the illustrations are a genuine delight. Not merely clear: handsome. Somebody cared what they looked like, in a field where the usual ambition is to be unambiguous and stop there.

Who it is for

Nearly everybody, which is an unusual thing to be able to say about a physiology text.

It suits structured thinkers, and those of us who would like to be. It suits people who are short of time, which is all of us, because a double page is a complete unit you can finish. It suits visual thinkers particularly well. And it works for a medical student meeting the material, a fellowship candidate revising it under pressure, and an established clinician who wants a refresher without committing to six hundred pages of Guyton.

It also suits the reader who simply likes physiology, which I make no apology for being.

The preface

Now the part I did not expect, and the reason I still think about this book.

The name on the cover is Agamemnon Despopoulos, which is not a name you meet often — I can think of one other, and he is three thousand years old. Despopoulos was born in New York in 1924, was professor of physiology at the University of New Mexico until 1971, and then went to Basel as a scientific adviser to CIBA-GEIGY.

The preface to the third edition explains, quietly and in a few sentences, that the first German edition was already at the printer when, on 2 November 1979, Despopoulos and his wife Sarah Jones-Despopoulos sailed from Bizerta in Tunisia, intending to cross the Atlantic. Nothing was ever heard from them again. Silbernagl writes that hope had to be given up, thanks his co-author for the creative enthusiasm without which the book would not exist, says plainly that continuing it alone was not easy — and signs off from Würzburg.

Beside that paragraph is a photograph of Despopoulos at the wheel of the boat in an orange foul-weather jacket, grinning broadly. It is the least funereal image imaginable, which is what makes it land.

A physiologist sailed into the Atlantic and did not come back, and the book he was halfway through went on without him for seven editions.

I do not want to make more of that than it will bear. But there is something in it about what we leave and how little control we have over which part of us lasts. He was fifty-five. The book has now taught physiology to two generations of doctors who have no idea what happened to the man whose name is on it.

Worth knowing before you buy it

The seventh English edition is from 2015, so the newest material is a decade old and the recent editions are Silbernagl's work. For core physiology that matters very little — countercurrent multiplication has not changed — but it is not where you go for anything current.

It is also not a reference and not a textbook. It will not teach you a subject from nothing and it has no room for argument or uncertainty; the format forbids both. Use it for structure, for revision, and for the pleasure of seeing something complicated made to fit on one page.

Which it does better than anything else I own.

Challenge for you

Which book did you find by accident, and keep?

Not the one you were told to buy — the one you pulled off a shelf, or were handed by somebody, or found in a hospital library at two in the morning, and have never got rid of. Tell me what it was and why it stuck.
