ICU and You  ·  Procedures  ·  Number 5

PICC lines

Choosing the patient, the vein and the size — and several things you were taught that will not survive contact with the evidence

A PICC is the easiest central line to put in and the hardest one to justify. Almost nothing goes wrong at insertion. Most of what goes wrong happens over the following fortnight, to a patient somebody else is looking after, for reasons that were determined before the needle went anywhere near the arm.

Which makes this a procedure where the thinking beforehand matters more than the hands. So this piece spends its first half on decisions and its second half on technique, and that proportion is deliberate.

The distinction that gets people into trouble

A midline is not a central line. It goes in the same way, it looks much the same coming out of the arm, and it confers none of a PICC's tolerance for difficult infusates. Vasopressors, concentrated potassium and parenteral nutrition do not belong in one.

The naming around these devices is genuinely inconsistent between services — “extended dwell”, “long peripheral” and “midline” are used interchangeably and wrongly. Do not trust the label on the handover sheet. Ask the only question that matters: where is the tip?

Should this patient have one at all?

There is a structured answer to this, and it is worth knowing by name because it will be quoted at you. MAGIC — the Michigan Appropriateness Guide for Intravenous Catheters — used a formal consensus method with a fifteen-member multispecialty panel to rate device choice against indication and duration.

Expected durationAppropriate device
5 days or fewerPeripheral cannula. PICC is rated inappropriate.
6–14 daysMidline preferred over PICC
15 days or morePICC appropriate
31 days or moreTunnelled catheter or port

That table assumes the infusate could go peripherally. If it could not, the calculus changes immediately — that is the one thing that justifies a PICC at short notice.

Two ratings that should change what you ask for

Multi-lumen PICCs placed “so we have a spare” are rated inappropriate. So is a second lumen to separate sampling from infusion. Single lumen is the default, and lumen count is an independent risk factor for thrombosis. Intensive care asks for triple lumens by reflex, and most of the time the reflex is wrong.

And be honest about the weak link. MAGIC's own panel conceded that clinicians predict expected duration of access badly — which is the dominant input the whole algorithm runs on. In intensive care that prediction is worse than anywhere.

There is a paediatric version, miniMAGIC, partly Australian in authorship, and it is not simply the adult table with smaller numbers, and its ratings are banded by age rather than applied across childhood. In neonates a PICC becomes appropriate at eight days rather than fifteen, and implanted ports are rated inappropriate at any duration — but that rating is specific to neonates. For children and adolescents ports are appropriate from thirty-one days, and in oncology they are appropriate at ten kilograms and above. Midlines are rated uncertain in infants — not because the adult evidence fails to transfer but because the panel found no robust paediatric evidence to rate — while remaining appropriate in neonates to seven days and in children to fourteen.

Two paediatric ratings are worth carrying on their own. For a child or adolescent on treatment for active cancer, the panel rated inserting a PICC rather than an implanted port as inappropriate, which is stronger than anything in the adult document. And for parenteral nutrition and long-term device dependency the panel did not reach equipoise but disagreed — a different outcome from “uncertain”, and a signal that the question is contested rather than merely unstudied. Tunnelled cuffed catheters were rated appropriate there across every age group.

The infusate question, and a rabbit

You have been taught that osmolarity above 600 mOsm/L, or a pH below 5 or above 9, mandates central access. Those numbers are quoted with great confidence. Here is where they come from.

Where 600 actually comes from

Not from a study. It is consensus — the figure used by the Infusion Nurses Society and the European ERPIUP consensus — and no primary experiment establishes it.

The experiment everybody gestures at is a 1998 study in rabbit ear veins, and it does not give 600. It gives a duration-dependent curve: about 820 mOsm/kg at eight hours, 690 at twelve, 550 at twenty-four. The finding that actually mattered — that the tolerated figure moves with how long you run the infusion — is dropped every time the number is repeated. So is the fact that the model was deliberately low-flow, which is the mechanistic point and also the main reason a human threshold cannot be read off it. Note too that the experiment measured osmolality, in mOsm/kg, and every clinical threshold is osmolarity, in mOsm/L. The paper's own conclusion was about infusion rate, not a cut-off: run hypertonic fluid faster and you shorten the exposure.

The guideline numbers do not agree either. The American nutrition societies say 900 for peripheral parenteral nutrition, on their own grading of weak evidence. The European guidelines say under 850, and elsewhere 800 to 1000. Thresholds actually in use across organisations run from about 500 to 1250. The current infusion standards — ninth edition, 2024 — set no definitive limit for either osmolarity or pH, which tells you something. And in lipid-containing mixtures the association with phlebitis has not been demonstrated at all, at admixture osmolarities well above any of these figures.

And the pH window, which points the other way

The 5-to-9 window is consensus too. ERPIUP sets it and says only that there is “wide consensus in the literature”, citing no primary study, and an independent review concludes that human tolerance of pH and osmolarity has never been properly studied.

But a primary experiment does exist, and it is the companion to the osmolality one — the same authors, the same rabbit ear veins, published the following year. It found endothelial loss and perivascular oedema at pH 4.5, little or no damage around 6.5, and concluded that the tolerance pH of the peripheral vein is about 6.5.

Which makes the conventional number not merely unsourced but permissive. The one piece of experimental evidence we have suggests a narrower window than the one being taught. That is the opposite of the direction you would expect an inherited convention to err in, and it is worth knowing before you reassure yourself that something at pH 5.2 is fine peripherally.

What is solid is vesicant status. Anthracyclines, vinca alkaloids, concentrated vasopressors and concentrated potassium cause tissue necrosis if they extravasate, and that is mechanism rather than arithmetic. MAGIC's approach of reducing the whole question to a binary — peripherally compatible or not — is more honest than a numeric threshold pretending to a precision nobody has.

The arm you must not use

This is the section most likely to save somebody something they cannot get back.

Chronic kidney disease: think about the fistula they do not have yet

Every arm vein you use is a fistula somebody may need in three years. Australian guidance advises vein preservation from CKD stage 3b — an eGFR under 45 — in anyone likely to require dialysis, and states that PICCs should be avoided in chronic kidney disease and considered only when there is no alternative.

MAGIC uses the same threshold, though not independently — both documents simply adopt the KDIGO stage 3b boundary, and MAGIC came first. Preserve the forearm veins of both arms, the dominant arm's as a last resort; use the dorsal veins of the dominant hand for peripheral access; and where prolonged access is genuinely needed, a small-bore internal jugular line is preferred to a PICC.

The evidence is observational and confounded — patients who get PICCs are sicker, and sicker patients have worse fistulas. But prior PICC is associated with roughly a threefold odds of not having a functioning fistula, and the alternative costs almost nothing. That asymmetry is what makes the recommendation reasonable despite the evidence being imperfect.

The conflict that looks real, and is not

Infection guidance prefers the subclavian site for non-tunnelled central lines. Renal vein preservation prefers the internal jugular and warns against subclavian, because of central venous stenosis. Set side by side those look irreconcilable, and that is how most people carry them.

They are not. The exception is written into the infection guidance itself, and at a higher grade than the preference it qualifies. The CDC recommends the subclavian site at Category IB — and, at Category IA, says to avoid the subclavian site in haemodialysis patients and patients with advanced kidney disease, to avoid subclavian vein stenosis. The 2022 SHEA / IDSA update carries the same carve-out, for anyone receiving or likely to require dialysis.

So there is nothing to resolve at the bedside. If the two still look irreconcilable, the resolution is in the infection document rather than in your judgement — read recommendations 2.2.3 and 2.2.5 together and the answer is already sitting there. SHEA goes further in the same direction, incidentally: do not choose a PICC as a strategy for reducing bloodstream infection.

Be careful with the numbers in this area, including the ones often quoted here. The figures for subclavian stenosis come from a single-centre prevalence survey rather than from either guideline, internal jugular catheters are reported in the same review at 25 to 40 per cent, and the best-known head-to-head comparison is 42 against 10. The PICC figures — about 4.8 per cent stenosis and 2.7 per cent occlusion — come from angiography in 150 patients. The direction is consistent across all of it; the arithmetic is not comparable, and a tidy ratio between these studies would be invented.

And one prohibition that turns out not to be evidence-based

“Never use the arm on the side of a mastectomy or axillary clearance” is taught everywhere. The best study — Naranjo and colleagues, nearly 7,900 cannulas in 3,724 patients — found two complications in 2,743 contralateral placements against two in 5,153 ipsilateral ones, and concluded that total avoidance of the ipsilateral arm is not needed. ANZCA says that in patients who have had axillary dissection and do not have lymphoedema there is no contraindication to peripheral cannulation or non-invasive blood pressure measurement in the affected arm — which is narrower than the “no contraindication to skin puncture” it is usually shortened to.

Hold even that loosely. Fewer than half of Naranjo's patients had any nodal surgery, complications were counted from diagnostic codes within fourteen days, and the authors name the obvious confounder themselves: a cannula was unlikely to be sited in an arm with lymphoedema, or in the arm of a patient who objected. Both lymphoedema events in fact followed contralateral placement. The systematic review that collected this literature puts it more cautiously than the headline does — the level of evidence for avoiding ipsilateral puncture is poor overall, which is not the same as evidence that it is safe.

But do not over-extrapolate. That evidence is about cannulas and venepuncture. A PICC is a large-bore, long-dwell device with a measurable thrombosis rate, and nobody has shown it is safe in a cleared axilla. The honest position: the blanket ban on ipsilateral needles is not supported; caution about ipsilateral PICCs remains sensible and is also unevidenced. Established lymphoedema is a reasonable relative contraindication on mechanical grounds. And ANZCA makes the point that cuts both ways: the risks of reaching for central access instead are likely to outweigh the benefit, so “use the other arm” is a better answer than “put in a central line”.

The remaining contraindications are more straightforward. Absolute: infection or burn at the site; known thrombosis or occlusion of the target or draining vein; a vein too small for the smallest acceptable catheter; superior vena cava obstruction; refusal. Relative: an ipsilateral pacemaker or defibrillator (lead-related venous narrowing is common, so use the other arm — sensible anatomy rather than evidence), prior ipsilateral central stenosis, and active bacteraemia, which is a question of timing rather than of site.

Coagulopathy, incidentally, is an argument for a PICC rather than against one — the insertion site is compressible, which is more than can be said for a subclavian.

The vein, and the size

Basilic — first choice

Largest calibre, most direct route to the axillary vein, straight course with fewer obstructing valves, and it sits medially and superficially — away from the brachial artery and median nerve.

Brachial — third

Paired, smaller, and sitting in the neurovascular bundle immediately beside the brachial artery and the median nerve. Identify both on short-axis ultrasound before you needle anything.

Cephalic — last

Smaller and more variable, and it joins the axillary vein at a sharp angle at the deltopectoral groove. If your catheter stops at the shoulder, this is usually why.

Aim for the middle third of the medial upper arm, roughly 12 to 15 cm above the medial epicondyle. Too low and the site sits in the antecubital region where arm circumference changes with elbow flexion and vessels converge; too high and you are near the axilla, where hair, moisture and movement defeat the dressing. There is a trademarked scheme that divides the arm into zones and names them by colour — the underlying anatomy is sound, the branded protocol rests on a forty-catheter personal series, so learn the reasoning rather than the trademark.

Catheter-to-vein ratio: use 45 per cent, and know what it rests on

The rule is that the catheter should occupy no more than 45 per cent of the vein's diameter. The number comes from an Australian prospective cohort of 136 patients that recorded four symptomatic thromboses. Above 45 per cent against at or below, the relative risk was 13 — with a confidence interval running from 1.45 to 122.8.

That is a signal, not an estimate. The authors themselves described the previously used 33 to 50 per cent range as arbitrary — and all four of their thrombosis cases were in patients with a haematological malignancy, which the first author's own later cohort was careful to point out while reporting a much weaker association. Say 45 per cent of the vein's diameter, because measuring area instead gives a completely different-looking number for the same catheter in the same vein. Use it, because it is the best figure available and the American standards have adopted it — but note that European consensus uses 33, and do not quote any of them as established.

The better-evidenced version of the same idea is simply absolute catheter diameter. A meta-analysis of forty studies found symptomatic DVT rising steadily with gauge: 0.89 per cent at 3 French, 3.26 at 4, 5.46 at 5, and 10.66 at 6. Heterogeneity was high, so hold the absolute values loosely — but the gradient is unambiguous and it is entirely within your control.

The four decisions that determine the thrombosis rate

Smallest diameter that will do the job. Fewest lumens. Basilic vein. Ultrasound for every step. Everything else is downstream of those.

The tip

Lower third of the superior vena cava, at the cavoatrial junction, or the upper right atrium. The principle underneath, which explains both failure modes at once, is that the tip should lie parallel to the vessel wall in high-flow blood.

  • Too highA tip in the brachiocephalic or upper SVC abuts the wall more or less perpendicular to flow. That is the set-up for erosion, perforation and thrombosis. Paediatric registry data put occlusion risk nearly threefold higher for SVC than right atrial tips.
  • Too deepMid or low right atrium, or worse the ventricle: arrhythmia, myocardial erosion, tamponade.
  • Remember the arm moves the tipTip depth changes with arm position. Confirmatory films should be taken with the arms beside the body, and a tip that looked perfect with the arm abducted may not be.
Does every PICC need a chest film? In adults, not necessarily.

NSW guidance states that if real-time tip confirmation can be verified during insertion, post-procedure chest radiographs are not required, and describes the intracavitary ECG method as accurate, precise and cost-effective.

Five qualifications. It is state-level guidance, not national — check your own service. The method needs an identifiable P wave, and where one cannot be found it does not apply. Atrial fibrillation used to be the stock objection and no longer is: a validated modification reads the fibrillatory waves in the TQ segment instead, and its proponents argue it should now be the preferred method in these patients rather than a reason to abandon the technique. A film is still needed if there is any concern about a mechanical complication. The ECG confirms depth, not course — a catheter in the internal jugular or the azygos vein can still produce a rising P wave. And paediatric practice is more conservative — the major Australian children's hospitals require radiographic confirmation before use, with narrow exceptions for midlines and for tips confirmed during an interventional procedure.

One paediatric detail worth carrying even if you mostly do adults: for a lower-limb PICC, the Royal Children's scheme puts the tip at or just above the diaphragm, above L5, which is the lower border of the inferior vena cava, and specifically avoids L1, where the renal veins enter. Attribute that rather than treating it as the rule — the Sydney Children's network uses a different scheme, siting the tip either above the renal vessels or well below them.

What actually goes wrong

Two pieces of standard teaching need correcting here, and they point in opposite directions.

Thrombosis: higher than a central line, but far lower than the famous number

The landmark meta-analysis — 64 studies, 29,503 patients — found PICCs carried about 2.5 times the odds of DVT compared with centrally inserted catheters. A later analysis of over 109,000 patients reproduced that odds ratio almost exactly.

Two things qualify it. The later analysis also looked at the randomised trials on their own, and there the difference was not statistically significant — though the point estimate barely moved, so what the randomised data show is that they are too sparse to confirm or exclude the effect, not that the effect is absent. The landmark meta-analysis could not do this comparison at all: it contained no randomised trials. And when analysis is restricted to studies using modern technique — ultrasound, documented tip verification, deliberate size selection — the pooled rate of symptomatic thrombosis is 2.4 per cent.

Resist the temptation to read that last figure against the frightening one. The famous critically-ill incidence of nearly 14 per cent comes from studies several of which screened asymptomatic patients; the 2.4 per cent is symptomatic thrombosis in largely non-intensive-care populations with no screening. Much of the apparent collapse is population and case definition rather than technique. The 109,000-patient analysis gives about 10.6 per cent in critical care and 3.7 overall, and those are the fairer numbers to carry.

So the useful message is not a frightening odds ratio. It is that the risk is substantially determined by how you place the line.

Infection: a PICC is not the clean option

The belief that PICCs are less infective than central lines is an artefact of where they are used. The meta-analysis that is usually cited in their favour found an overall relative risk of 0.62 — but that advantage was almost entirely in outpatients (RR 0.22). In hospitalised patients it was 0.73 — a real reduction, and much smaller. The figure that matters most, though, is the one that corrects for how long the devices stay in: per catheter-day the incidence rate ratio was 0.91, with a confidence interval from 0.46 to 1.79 — no difference at all. A PICC dwells longer, and counting per catheter rather than per day quietly rewards it for that.

Choose a PICC for infusate, duration or access. Do not choose one because you think it is cleaner.

The rest, with the honest caveat that several of these have no trustworthy denominator. Occlusion and dislodgement are the two that actually end devices, and which of them leads depends on whose data you read: the Australian pilot trials found dislodgement commonest, at around 6 per cent, while the much larger Australian randomised trial reported in 2025 — nearly 1,100 patients — found occlusion the commonest reason for failure. Take the pair seriously and the ranking lightly. That same trial gives prospective catheter fracture rates under one per cent, so fracture is no longer a case-report-only phenomenon, though fragment embolism still is. Insertion arrhythmia is well understood mechanistically but has never been reliably counted.

Children run the opposite way

A meta-analysis of 3,689 children (neonates excluded) found overall complications 6 per cent, infection 3 per cent, thromboembolism 1 per cent — roughly an order of magnitude below adult intensive care — but mechanical failure 8 per cent, which is higher.

That inversion is the paediatric signature. In an adult you are mostly worrying about the vein. In a child you are mostly worrying about the catheter.

When it will not go in, and other trouble

Almost all of what follows is practice-based consensus rather than trial evidence. Treat it as technique, and treat anyone who tells you otherwise with suspicion.

  • It will not advance. Abduct the arm to 45–90 degrees. Withdraw two or three centimetres and re-advance rather than pushing harder. Flush while advancing — hydraulic opening of valves is the single most useful manoeuvre. Apply gentle skin traction distal to the site to straighten the vein, and rotate the catheter slightly. If it arrests at the shoulder you are probably in the cephalic, and the answer is usually to resite. Never force against resistance.
  • It has gone up the internal jugular. Prevent it: turn the head towards the insertion side, chin down to the shoulder, as the catheter crosses the shoulder; have an assistant press over the ipsilateral internal jugular; and scan the neck with ultrasound before you send anyone for a film. Correct it: withdraw to below the junction, reposition the head, re-advance with a vigorous flush. If it is seated in the jugular and will not redirect, remove and resite — you cannot steer it round that corner.
  • You have lost blood return. Think fibrin sheath first — it infuses freely and will not aspirate. Also tip against the wall, migration, mural thrombus, a kink, or a clamp somebody left shut. Sit the patient up, raise the arm, ask for a deep breath or a cough, roll them to the other side. Flush 10 mL of saline with a 10 mL or larger syringe and try again. Check the external length against what was documented.
  • It is occluded. In an adult of 30 kg or more, alteplase 2 mg in 2 mL — the 2 mg catheter-clearance vial, reconstituted to 1 mg/mL, not a dilution from the 50 mg stroke vial. Instil into the lumen, and no more than the lumen's fill volume, which for most PICCs is well under 2 mL; the remainder of the dose goes nowhere useful and some of it goes systemically. For a multi-lumen device the dose is divided between the affected lumens rather than repeated in each. Assess at 30 minutes, reassess at 120, and a second dose may follow: maximum two doses per occlusion episode. Do not apply vigorous suction and do not push against resistance — you will either rupture the catheter or embolise the clot. The product information lists only hypersensitivity as a contraindication; active bleeding, surgery or deep-tissue biopsy within 48 hours, and suspected catheter infection — where the concern is releasing a local infection into the circulation — are precautions, and some local guidelines are stricter than the label. Follow yours.
  • It is occluded, and it is a child. Paediatric dosing and dwell differ from the adult label, and Australian paediatric practice has diverged from it. Over 10 kg: 1.5 mg in 1.5 mL, to a maximum of 3 mg across lumens. Under 10 kg the dose is not drawn from the reconstituted vial but diluted first — 0.5 mg (0.5 mL) into 1.5 mL of 0.9 per cent sodium chloride, then instil 1 mL of that solution, which is 0.25 mg. Writing it as “0.25 mg” without the dilution invites drawing up 0.25 mL of neat alteplase instead. Dwell two to four hours initially and, for later doses, up to seventy-two. The catheter-clearance presentation is approved from two weeks of age and is not recommended in preterm neonates. Follow your local protocol, know it will not match the adult product information, and note that the paediatric documents in circulation are themselves past their review dates.
  • It is blocked, but not with clot. Alteplase will do nothing for a lipid residue or a drug precipitate. Those need a different agent — and a pharmacist.
  • You think it has fractured. Stop the infusion. Clamp proximal to the suspected defect. Do not flush. Consider a tourniquet high on the arm if a fragment may have embolised, and get an urgent chest film. Retained fragments are an interventional radiology problem.
  • Arrhythmia during insertion. The tip is too deep. Withdraw one to two centimetres and it almost always stops. The corollary matters more: an arrhythmia appearing days later suggests inward migration — check the external length.
  • The arm has swollen. Catheter-related upper limb DVT until proven otherwise, and it needs an urgent compression ultrasound. Then the part that surprises people: a functioning, correctly positioned, uninfected, still-needed catheter does not automatically come out. All four conditions matter, and infection is the one most often skipped over. Anticoagulate and keep it. Remove it if it is infected, malpositioned, blocked, no longer needed, or if symptoms progress despite anticoagulation.

Taking it out

  • Never routinelyDo not replace PICCs on a schedule to prevent infection, and do not exchange them over a wire to treat suspected infection. Both are explicit recommendations and both are still done.
  • But promptly when doneRemoving a line that is no longer essential is the single most effective infection prevention measure there is, and the most neglected. Ask every day.
  • Not for fever aloneAn explicit recommendation, and one intensive care trainees reliably get wrong.
  • Never sitting upRemoval with the site above the heart risks air embolism. Supine or head-down; ask for a Valsalva or breath hold, or remove at end of inspiration; then an occlusive dressing left on for at least 24 hours, because the residual tract keeps letting air in. If you suspect an embolism: left lateral, head down, 100 per cent oxygen — and call the emergency response, which the guidance makes an explicit step rather than an implication.
  • If it will not comeStop pulling. Venospasm is the usual cause. Warm compresses, wait twenty or thirty minutes, reposition and relax the arm, then gentle sustained traction. Excessive force is the main reason catheters fracture on removal.
  • Measure itEvery time, against the documented insertion length, and write down that you did. A short catheter means a retained fragment until imaging says otherwise.

The bundle, briefly

Nothing here is novel and all of it is audited.

  • Skin2 per cent chlorhexidine in 70 per cent alcohol, applied back and forth for at least 30 seconds, then allowed to dry; povidone-iodine in alcohol where chlorhexidine cannot be used. The drying is not optional — the antisepsis depends on contact time, and wet alcohol is a fire risk. Chlorhexidine in infants under two months is an unresolved question rather than a recommendation, and chlorhexidine-impregnated dressings are specifically not for premature neonates.
  • BarriersCap, mask, sterile gown, sterile gloves, full-body drape — and, in the Australian wording, eye protection and a sterile ultrasound probe cover, both of which are missing from the older international list and both of which matter now that ultrasound is standard.
  • DressingA chlorhexidine-impregnated transparent dressing for adults — the NSW guidance specifies eighteen years and over, and paediatric services set their own weight and age floors. Change transparent dressings every five to seven days, gauze every one to two days, and either immediately if damp, soiled or lifting. Swap gauze for a transparent dressing as soon as the bleeding or exudate stops.
  • SecurementSutureless, of some kind — that much is agreed. Which kind is not. The Australian pilot trial in this area was not powered to separate its arms, dislodgement remained the dominant failure mode at around 6 per cent, and tissue adhesive is promising rather than proven. Some paediatric services now use subcutaneous anchoring.
If you remember six things

Fewer lumens than you think you need. The smallest catheter that will do the job. Basilic, middle third of the arm, under ultrasound. Tip at the cavoatrial junction and parallel to the wall. Ask about kidneys before you touch the arm. And take it out the day it stops being necessary.

Challenge for you

What do you do when the catheter stops at the shoulder?

Everybody who places enough of these has a sequence — something about the arm, something about the head, a flush at the right moment, a point at which they stop and resite rather than persisting. Very little of it is written down anywhere, which is exactly why it is worth collecting.

I would also like to hear about the PICC you wish had never been inserted — the one that went in easily, for a good enough reason at the time, and cost the patient something later. Those are the ones this piece exists for.

And the one I would most like to publish: what are the three things you would say to somebody starting out on PICC insertion? Tips, tricks, cautions, dos and don'ts — whatever three you would actually say if a registrar turned to you before their first one. The habit you have never had to think about. The thing you only learned by getting it wrong. The line you draw for yourself about when to stop and ask for help.

Three is deliberate. It is short enough that you will send it, and long enough that you have to choose — and what you leave out will be as interesting as what you keep.
