Because she overlaid it

Three thousand years, and the same sentence

This week's crossword had OVERLAYING in it, clued as a nineteenth-century verdict for cot death. That is true of the death certificates. The word is a great deal older than that.

It appears in the First Book of Kings, in the passage everyone knows for a different reason entirely. Two women come before Solomon, each claiming the same living child. It is remembered as a story about a sword and a wise king. It begins, though, as a story about a baby who died in the night.

18 And it came to pass the third day after that I was delivered, that this woman was delivered also: and we were together; there was no stranger with us in the house, save we two in the house.

19 And this woman's child died in the night; because she overlaid it.

21 And when I rose in the morning to give my child suck, behold, it was dead: but when I had considered it in the morning, behold, it was not my son, which I did bear.

1 Kings 3:18–21, King James Version

Three things in eleven words

One. That is very probably the oldest written description of what we would now call sudden unexpected death in infancy. Two women, two newborns three days apart, one shared sleeping surface, and a baby dead by morning with no other explanation offered. The Victorians did not invent the word or the verdict; they inherited it, put it on certificates, and passed it to us as a crossword answer.

Two. The whole case turns on a mother's certainty about her own child. Not on evidence, not on a witness — there was none, the text is careful to say so twice. On when I had considered it in the morning, behold, it was not my son. She looked at a dead baby in her arms and knew, against every appearance, that it was not hers.

This week's aphorism, three millennia early: in an infant, the parent is the monitor.

Three, and it is the one I keep returning to. The two women are described as harlots. They are the least credible witnesses in that society, with no standing, no property and no man to speak for them, and they walk in off the street and are heard by the king. The story does not pause to justify this. It simply records that their testimony was taken seriously enough to be tested.

We have observation charts, retrieval services and point-of-care testing that Solomon did not. What the passage has that we sometimes lack is the assumption that the woman holding the baby is worth listening to — including, and especially, when she has no status and her account is inconvenient.

And the part that should sit uncomfortably

Read it carefully

The passage assigns blame in four words. Because she overlaid it. No inquest, no examination, no consideration of anything else that might kill a three-day-old in the night — and the accusation is made by the other woman, in a dispute, with something to gain.

That is the oldest version of a habit we have not entirely lost. It is precisely why the modern approach to an infant death is a full resuscitation, meticulous documentation, the coroner in every case, and no speculation whatever about cause in the room. Shared sleep is a real risk factor, particularly alongside alcohol, sedatives, smoking or exhaustion, and safe sleeping advice saves lives. But advice given beforehand and blame apportioned afterwards are entirely different acts, and only one of them helps anybody.

Three thousand years on, a bereaved mother is still liable to be the first person suspected. The least we can do, having inherited the word, is to decline to inherit the reflex.
