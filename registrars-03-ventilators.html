Ventilators

Every mode is an answer to two questions: what is controlled, and who decides when the breath starts and stops

There is an old line that intensive care is easy: all you have to do is make air go in and out, and blood go round and round. The ventilator is the first half of that sentence, and it takes rather more than a sentence.

What follows is a registrar’s working account of the machine — what is in it, what you set on it, what the settings actually do, and how to read what it is telling you back. It is arranged the way it was taught: the anatomy first, then the goals, then the modes, then the waveforms, then getting the patient off it.

The anatomy of a ventilator

Underneath the screen, a ventilator is four things: something that supplies gas, something that controls how that gas is delivered, something that measures what happened, and something that stops it from doing harm.

What is inside the box
GroupComponents
Sources of power Gas supply, electrical power, and a pressure generator.
Control of gas delivery Gas blender, gas accumulator, inspiratory flow regulator, humidification, the patient circuit, and the expiratory pressure regulator — the PEEP valve.
Monitoring Sensors for gas concentration, flow, pressure and volume.
Safety features Filters — gas intake particle filters, pre-circuit bacterial filters, moisture traps and heat and moisture exchange systems, and an expired gas filter — and alarms.

Who needs one

Five indications, and the fifth is the one that is easiest to leave too late.

  1. Airway protection. A reduced level of consciousness, a threatened or actual airway obstruction, or to allow sedation that would otherwise be unsafe.
  2. Failure to oxygenate.
  3. Failure to ventilate.
  4. Excessive work of breathing, or impending respiratory fatigue.
  5. Severe physiological instability, such as severe shock.
The one to act on early

Do not let a shocked patient fatigue. A patient in severe shock is spending a large share of a failing cardiac output on the work of breathing, and when the respiratory muscles finally give out they do so suddenly. Providing the ventilatory support that lets them recover is a decision best made while there is still time to make it calmly.

The settings

FiO2
The fraction of oxygen delivered, from 21% to 100%.
PEEP
Positive end expiratory pressure — the pressure the lung is held at between breaths.
I:E ratio
Or the inspiratory time directly. Generally 1:2, and longer on the expiratory side if there is obstruction or gas trapping.
Rate
Respiratory rate, or the breath cycle time.
Size of breath
Tidal volume, or pressure support, or pressure control — depending on which of the two is being controlled.

The four variables that define a breath

Modes have proprietary names and the names differ between manufacturers. The variables underneath them do not. Any breath, on any machine, can be described by four of them — and almost every ventilator problem is a problem with one of the four.

Control
What the ventilator holds constant: either volume or pressure. It cannot be both.
Trigger
How and when the ventilator decides to start a breath. Time, flow, pressure or volume.
Cycle
What ends inspiration and begins expiration. Time, in volume control; or flow, in pressure support, where the breath ends once flow has decelerated to about a quarter of its peak.
Limit
The ceiling a variable may not exceed during inspiration. If pressure support is set to 20 above a PEEP of 10, the inspiratory pressure will not exceed 30 cmH2O.

What we are trying to achieve

Four goals, and they pull against one another. Most of the difficulty in ventilating a sick lung is in the trade.

  • Oxygenation.
  • Ventilation — clearing carbon dioxide.
  • Preventing iatrogenic lung injury, which the ventilator is entirely capable of causing.
  • Maintaining comfort and avoiding dyssynchrony.

Oxygenation

Mechanical ventilation improves oxygenation in three ways: it reduces the work of breathing and so the metabolic demand of the respiratory muscles; it reduces intrapulmonary shunt by opening atelectatic lung; and it raises mean airway pressure.

Which gives three levers.

  • Raise the FiO2.
  • Raise the mean airway pressure by raising PEEP.
  • Raise the mean airway pressure by lengthening the inspiratory time, so that a greater proportion of each cycle is spent in inspiration.

Ventilation

Alveolar minute ventilation is (VT − VD) × RR, and it is what clears carbon dioxide. So there are three levers again: increase the tidal volume, increase the rate, or reduce the dead space if there is any dead space you can actually do something about.

Not injuring the lung

Four named injuries, each with a mechanism and each with a number attached to its prevention. These are the numbers worth carrying.

Ventilator-induced lung injury
InjuryMechanismPrevention
Barotrauma Excessive alveolar pressure causing alveolar rupture. Plateau pressure under 30 cmH2O and driving pressure under 15 cmH2O.
Volutrauma Excessive tidal volume causing alveolar overdistension, with epithelial and endothelial injury and inflammation. Tidal volumes of 6 to 8 mL/kg predicted body weight, and 4 to 8 in ARDS. Accept permissive hypercapnia if that is what it takes.
Atelectrauma Repetitive opening and closing of alveoli, causing local mechanical stress and inflammation. Apply PEEP — generally starting at 5 cmH2O and titrated up as needed.
Oxygen toxicity Pulmonary and systemic injury from prolonged high oxygen concentrations, mediated mainly by oxidative stress. Aim for normoxia, around 88 to 96%, by titrating the FiO2. Avoid prolonged hyperoxia.

Dyssynchrony, and wasted effort

A patient who is fighting the ventilator is usually not being difficult. They are being mistriggered, or starved of flow, or cycled off at the wrong moment. Five patterns cover most of it.

Ineffective trigger
The patient makes an effort and the ventilator does not recognise it, so no breath is delivered.
Auto-triggering
A breath is initiated without any real patient effort, giving more ventilation than intended and a good deal of discomfort.
Flow starvation
The patient wants more flow than is being delivered. In severe cases this produces double triggering.
Double triggering
Two ventilator breaths delivered on one prolonged inspiratory effort.
Reverse triggering
A mandatory breath provokes diaphragmatic activation, which then triggers a further breath.

Modes

Three questions sort every mode on every machine.

  • The control variable — pressure or volume.
  • The breath sequence — spontaneous or mandatory.
  • The targeting scheme — a set point, as in plain volume or pressure control, or adaptive, as in pressure regulated volume control.

Pressure control

The pressure is set. Flow decelerates through the breath, because as alveolar pressure rises the pressure gradient driving gas into the lung falls away. The tidal volume that results is determined by the driving pressure and the compliance — which means it is not guaranteed, and will change when the lung changes.

Volume control

The tidal volume is set. Flow is constant, and the volume delivered follows from the inspiratory flow rate and the inspiratory time — a flow of 500 mL/s for one second is 500 mL. Pressure is now the variable, and it rises as volume accumulates against the compliance of the lung.

Adaptive modes

Pressure regulated volume control is a pressure control mode wearing a volume target. The tidal volume is set; the first breath is delivered as a volume control breath; the system calculates the compliance from the pressure that breath required; and subsequent breaths are pressure control breaths aimed at producing the volume you asked for.

It gives you the square waveform of pressure control with the guaranteed minute volume of volume control. The cost is that the support falls as the patient's own effort rises, which is the opposite of what a tiring patient needs.

Pressure support

A purely spontaneous mode, with a backup rate for when there are no spontaneous breaths. The patient triggers it, by flow or by pressure depending on what is set. The ventilator then supports the breath with a set pressure above PEEP — pressure support of 10 over a PEEP of 5 gives an inspiratory pressure of 15. The breath ends when flow decelerates to about a quarter of its peak.

SIMV

Designed as a weaning mode, to carry a patient from mandatory breathing to spontaneous. It guarantees a set respiratory rate, and outside the trigger window the patient may take additional supported breaths of their own.

Spontaneous or mandatory

What each costs and what each buys
ModeAdvantageDisadvantage
Spontaneous
  • Allows sedation to be weaned
  • Required before extubation
  • Better synchrony
  • Better ventilation of dependent lung, through active diaphragmatic contraction
  • No control over rate
  • Minimal control over tidal volume
  • Carbon dioxide clearance depends on the patient's own respiratory drive
Mandatory
  • Allows deep sedation and paralysis
  • Guaranteed rate and minute volume
  • Decreased patient effort
  • Slower weaning
  • Less comfortable
  • Deconditioning and diaphragmatic atrophy
  • More dyssynchrony
And the three control modes against one another
ModeAdvantagesDisadvantages
Pressure control Higher mean airway pressure, protective against barotrauma, better recruitment. Tidal volume may vary, so minute ventilation varies; possible volutrauma; the higher initial flow meets greater resistance.
Volume control Guaranteed tidal volume and stable minute ventilation; the lower initial flow avoids an early high resistance peak. Poorer recruitment, lower mean airway pressure, and insufficient flow can cause dyssynchrony.
PRVC Guaranteed tidal volume and minute ventilation with the square waveform of pressure control. Variable mean airway pressure, and increased patient effort results in decreased support.

Reading the waveform

The pressure trace carries more information than any number on the screen, and most of it is in the difference between two pressures rather than in either one alone.

Resistance

A rising peak pressure with an unchanged plateau means the resistance has changed and the lung has not. The list of causes is short and most of it is mechanical.

Circuit
Tube kinking, biting, secretions, mucous plugging, filter obstruction.
Airways
Bronchospasm, mucous plugging, blood or clot.

Compliance

A rising plateau pressure, with the peak-to-plateau gap unchanged, means the lung or the chest wall has stiffened. Here the list is longer and almost all of it is important.

Alveolar and interstitial
ARDS, pulmonary oedema, pneumonia.
Loss of aerated lung
Atelectasis, mainstem intubation, severe mucous plugging, lobar collapse.
Pleural and chest wall
Pneumothorax, effusion, obesity, abdominal compartment syndrome.

Dynamic hyperinflation

Caused by a high respiratory rate, large tidal volumes, inadequate expiratory time, or airway obstruction and bronchospasm — usually some combination. The flow trace gives it away before the blood pressure does.

  • Increased work of breathing.
  • Difficulty triggering, because a more negative pressure is needed to overcome the intrinsic PEEP.
  • Dyssynchrony.
  • Reduced venous return, then hypotension, then cardiovascular collapse.
Worth looking for deliberately

Dynamic hyperinflation is an insidious thing, and it is one of the few ventilator problems that kills by way of the circulation rather than the lung. In a patient who is obstructed, hypotensive and hard to trigger, it is worth disconnecting the circuit and watching what happens to the blood pressure.

Weaning and liberation

Two approaches. Gradual, by reducing the pressure support or the number of mandatory breaths; or abrupt, by a spontaneous breathing trial on minimal support. The evidence generally favours the abrupt approach, though it is not always possible.

When weaning fails, it is almost always because the load on the respiratory system exceeds what the patient can generate. Both halves of that are worth going through deliberately.

Why weaning fails
DomainWhat to look for
Airway safety Secretion burden and clearance, airway oedema, cranial nerve palsies, and the diminished reflexes that follow prolonged ventilation.
Respiratory load Inappropriate ventilator settings, reduced compliance, increased resistance, dynamic hyperinflation, endotracheal tube diameter, secretions.
Cardiac load Heart failure, and the increased cardiac workload that comes with the metabolic demand of breathing. Reduced oxygen-carrying capacity from anaemia or sepsis.
Neurological Depressed central drive, delirium, ICU-acquired weakness, peripheral neurological dysfunction, and pain.
Musculoskeletal Myopathy including steroid myopathy, neuromuscular problems, restrictive lung disease, abdominal distension, chest trauma, flail segments.
Metabolic Increased metabolic demand from trauma, burns or sepsis; extremes of nutrition; obesity; cachexia; metabolic acidosis.

Is this patient ready to come out?

Start with the question that is easiest to forget: has the thing you intubated them for actually resolved? Then four letters.

  1. A — airway. Able to protect it.
  2. B — breathing. Spontaneously ventilating on minimal support, or at least supportable once the tube is out.
  3. C — circulation. Haemodynamically stable, or at least not getting worse.
  4. D — disability. Adequate neurological performance.
The thread running through all of it

Almost every question on this page comes back to the same two: what is being controlled, and who is deciding when the breath begins and ends. Get those two straight for the patient in front of you and the mode name stops mattering.
