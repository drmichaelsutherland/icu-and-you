No. 7

The Kids Were Kings

A factory Christmas party at Port Adelaide, and what medicine calls downtime

It is sweet to recall the epic echoes of youth, when a day could last forever, and forever seemed too far away to even think about. Summer days with no clear endpoint, and sunsets that lingered and lingered before suddenly sinking away like a stone in a billabong.

Of course, the recollections get freely modified over time. And they get sweeter with each revision.

My father worked in a factory down near the dockside at Port Adelaide. It wasn’t an easy gig, and probably didn’t even qualify to be called a “gig”.

It was wall-to-wall shift work, often night work, and not at all lucrative. Opportunities to be creative, or even to be an individual, were limited. In his own mind, though, he was wildly creative, and that was his saving grace.

His main ambition was to make things a little easier for his kids, and to make sure their opportunities were larger, brighter and grander. He succeeded. All doors are open to those who try, he used to tell me. I have not forgotten his words.

Each year, in the week before Christmas, his employer, a large multinational in an era before that word became commonplace, shut down for a day and put on a party.

The kids were kings. There were games and treats, fun and freebies. You got to meet wonderful celebrities, like Humphrey B Bear and Bobo the Clown. You got to sing and dance to the nth degree, and then a bit beyond. We were twisting with the Beatles, and shouting just for the joy of it. There were helpers everywhere, making sure it all ran smoothly. The ladies were in the ladies’ lounge, when such a thing still existed, and they liked that a lot. The men were in their designated area, drinking cold beer and talking sport, and that seemed to work as well. I didn’t see aggression, aggravation, angst or acrimony. If there was any, it has since been revised out.

As evening approached, Santa arrived, and there was a gift for every child present. More than half a century later, I still have my Test Match Cricket game, made from plastic and cardboard.

The works Christmas parties signposted the years of my childhood. They were something you looked forward to, and something you remembered.

* * *

I haven’t seen that in my professional life.

The structure of medical life is different. If you are off duty, your colleagues are on, and vice versa. We are firmly entrenched in the concept of “downtime”, the word for the hours when you are not on clinical duties. And as you start to earn more, downtime morphs into being permanently away. The upshot is that too many of us don’t really know our own colleagues, let alone their families or their daily lives.

But the downtime is in fact your lifetime. It is not a furlough after a tour of duty, or parole after a stretch inside. It is the real time, the main game, the time to show yourself and everyone else who you are, and what matters to you outside the medical bubble.

So if your unit has a Christmas get-together this year, go. Bring the kids. Learn the names of everyone else’s. Make them kings for an afternoon.

Somewhere down the track, one of them will be remembering it. And revising it. And making it sweeter every time.

A footnote…

Both celebrities were Adelaide’s own, and both arrived in the same year. Humphrey B. Bear first went to air on NWS-9 on 24 May 1965, and stayed on Australian television until 2008, one of the longest-running children’s programmes anywhere.

Bobo the Clown was Hal Turner, a vaudevillian since the age of six, who developed the character at NWS-9 before taking him to the new rival station: The Bobo Show was the first programme SAS-10 ever broadcast, on 26 July 1965. By the middle of the decade Turner was mobbed whenever he appeared in public as Bobo, and factory visits were part of the job — the National Film and Sound Archive holds footage of him touring the Heinz works in Adelaide, riding a forklift and handing out party bags to the children.

Challenge for you

How many of your colleagues’ children could you name?

Not the colleagues — you know them, after a fashion, from handovers and rosters. Their children. Their partners, if it comes to that.

And if your unit does something at Christmas that brings the families in, I would like to hear what it is, and whether it works. If it does nothing, I would like to hear that too.
