T. E. Oh’s Intensive Care Manual

T. E. Oh, 1979, and eight editions since — the book I slept beside, and complained about for most of those years

Jonathan M. Handy, Bala Venkatesh, Jeremy Cohen and Daniel Martin, editors. Oh’s Intensive Care Manual. Ninth edition. Elsevier, October 2025. About 1,700 pages, 120 chapters in 18 parts. Print and eBook. First published in 1979 by T. E. Oh.

About this series

Books that have made a difference to how we teach, train or practise in intensive care. Not textbooks, or not only textbooks — anything that changed how somebody thinks about the work. No. 1 was not a medical book at all. This one is about as medical as a book can get.

For about three years it lived on the floor beside my bed.

I want to be precise about that, because it sounds like devotion and it was mostly logistics. I was a senior registrar. The thing was too heavy to keep carrying back and forth, and I needed it at hours when I was in no condition to go looking. So it stayed where it landed.

I did not read it in bed. Nobody has ever read Oh’s in bed. It is not bedtime reading and it has never pretended to be.

If you trained in Australia, New Zealand or Singapore at any point in the last forty years, none of this needs explaining. It is the bible. It has been the bible for so long that nobody alive can recall the decision being made. You sat the exams out of it. You settled arguments with it. At some point you stopped saying the title and started saying Oh’s, the way you say the defib.

It opens, before a single patient appears, with the design and organisation of an intensive care unit. The room first. Then who staffs it, how they hand over, how they move a patient down a corridor or across a state. Only then does anybody get sick.

The eighteen parts Structure and process · Decision making · Sepsis and infections · Cardiovascular · Respiratory failure · Gastroenterological emergencies and surgery · Acute renal failure · Neurological disorders · Endocrine disorders · The surgical patient · Severe and multiple trauma · Special populations · The paediatric patient · Environmental injuries · Inflammatory and immune disorders · Haematological management · Transplantation · Pharmacologic considerations

Inside that, the range is what gets you. There is a chapter on the determination of death and a chapter on severity scoring. There is one on tropical infections and one on blast and ballistic trauma, and one on chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear exposure, which I hope you never need and which somebody, somewhere, is reading tonight. There is a chapter on Māori, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations and critical illness. There is a chapter on allied health professionals in the unit, which is new, and overdue.

Two of this week’s pieces on this site are chapters in it. Humidifiers sit in Part Five, which is the whole of Manic Monday in one unglamorous heading. Envenomation sits in Part Fourteen, which is where Maheshani’s talk would go. That is the trick of a book like this. Whatever happened to you last Tuesday, it is in there, filed under something flatter than the way you remember it.

It is called a manual. It is seventeen hundred pages. Somebody should have said something in 1979 and now it is too late.

Reviewing the sixth edition in Anaesthesia and Intensive Care, K. M. Ho of Perth called it a truly outstanding and comprehensive intensive care textbook, and noted drily that it is far more comprehensive than a usual clinical manual — which is the politest possible way of saying the title stopped being accurate several editions ago.

What is wrong with it

Everything that is wrong with any multi-author textbook, and I have never found a way round it.

There is no voice. There cannot be. A hundred and twenty chapters by a hundred-odd people produces a hundred-odd registers, and you feel the join every time you cross one. The flow is not there either. You do not read Oh’s so much as consult it, then look up, then consult it again somewhere else.

Worse, and this is the real cost: there is no unified message. Two chapters that touch the same patient from different directions will not necessarily agree about her, and nobody will tell you they disagree. You find it yourself, usually at three in the morning, usually when you were hoping to be told.

And yet

The same arrangement is the reason the book is any good. Every chapter is written by somebody who actually does that thing, and you can feel it — the energy of a person writing about their own corner, not a generalist being even-handed about somebody else’s. The expertise is real and it is specific. You would not get that from one author, and one author could not possibly have written it. The flaw and the virtue are the same decision.

The problem it cannot solve

Traditional textbooks are in trouble in a fast specialty, and this one has the receipts. The fourth edition, which T. E. Oh edited himself in 1997, ran to just over a thousand pages. The ninth runs to about seventeen hundred. The specialty grew by two thirds of a book in less than thirty years, and that is only the part that could be squeezed in.

Nothing in print can keep up with that. A chapter is written, then edited, then typeset, then printed, then shipped, and somewhere in there a trial reports and makes a paragraph of it wrong. Everyone knows this. Nobody has solved it.

The ninth edition does the sensible things available to it. There is an eBook with every copy, which means the thing is searchable and in your pocket and not on the floor beside the bed. There is online content, some of it arriving after the print date, which is at least an admission that the print date is not the end of the story. Whether that is enough is a fair question. It is more than the fourth edition could do, which was nothing.

One fair criticism, and it is ours

Reviewing the seventh edition in the British Journal of Anaesthesia, MacBrayne and Webster of Aberdeen praised contributors who were undoubtedly experts in their fields and a book designed for global appeal, then observed that the contributions came overwhelmingly from Australasia and the UK, with no North American input at all.

From where we sit that is not obviously a defect — it is precisely why the book fits our practice so well. But it is worth knowing what you are holding. It is not a neutral survey of world intensive care. It is our tradition, written down.

So

I have read it more times than I care to admit, and I have complained about it for most of those times. Both things are true and neither cancels the other. It is a love-hate relationship of the ordinary kind, the sort you have with an institution rather than a book.

It is cherished. It has a place, and it will go on having one, because whatever replaces the printed textbook has not arrived yet and this is the best version of the old thing that anybody has managed. The name on the cover belongs to a man who stopped editing it a quarter of a century ago and whose judgement is still somehow in the architecture.

Mine is not on the floor beside the bed any more. It is on the shelf in the office, at eye level, where I can reach it without standing up.

Which is roughly what promotion amounts to.
