The age it dawns on you

Why mortality turns up in your fifties, and what to do with it besides lie awake

Who this is for

Anyone at all. No medicine is assumed and none is required. This one is for the age group the site is spending a fortnight on — roughly forty-five to sixty — and for anyone who has lately found themselves thinking about dying rather more than they used to.

I am an intensive care specialist in a regional unit. This piece is about a feeling rather than a disease, which is a departure for me, and there is a practical section near the end that is the real reason I wrote it.

The statistics and the telephone numbers here are Australian. If you are reading this somewhere else, the shape of it will be much the same; those will not.

Somewhere in your fifties, often with nothing in particular to explain it, you start thinking about dying. Not constantly, and not morbidly. It is simply there in a way it was not there at thirty-five, and most people assume there is something slightly wrong with them for noticing.

There is not. It is one of the most reliable findings in the psychology of ageing, and almost nobody is told about it.

It is not just you, and it is not random

Death anxiety has been measured for fifty years, and it does not rise steadily across life the way you would expect. It is high in your twenties. It falls through the thirties and forties. And then, somewhere around fifty, it climbs again.

Russac and colleagues found exactly that shape, most markedly in women, and were honest enough to write that they could not explain the second rise. The broader literature puts the peak of death anxiety in middle age rather than in old age — which is the opposite of what most people assume, including most people in middle age.

Why now

There is nothing mystical about the timing. Three things tend to happen in the same decade.

  • Your parents die, or start toUntil they go, there is a generation standing between you and the exit. The phrase people use afterwards is that they feel suddenly exposed, and it is a very exact description of what has changed.
  • Somebody your own age diesNot a grandparent, not a great-aunt. Somebody who was at school with you, or who you worked beside. The first one of those rearranges the furniture permanently.
  • Your body starts sending you mailA knee. A result that needs repeating. A scan that was fine but took eleven days to say so. None of it serious, all of it new, and all of it information you did not have at forty.

Any one of the three would do it. Arriving together, they do not constitute a crisis. They constitute new information, correctly received. The feeling is not a malfunction. It is an accurate reading of a situation that has genuinely changed.

The part nobody tells you

It gets better.

Death anxiety declines in old age, consistently, across study after study. The people who are closest to dying are, as a group, the least frightened of it. That is not bravado and it does not appear to be denial; it is simply what the measurements keep showing.

One study put that side by side in the same families. Elderly parents and their middle-aged children were given the same death anxiety scale, scored out of fifteen. The parents averaged four. Their children averaged about seven for themselves — and when asked to guess, put their parents at around eight.

Read that again, because it is the most useful thing on this page. The middle-aged children were markedly more afraid than their elderly parents, and were certain it was the other way round. If you are fifty-five and quietly dreading what your eighty-five-year-old mother must be going through, there is a reasonable chance she is calmer about it than you are, and that she would say so if anybody asked.

If you remember one thing from this page

You are, in all likelihood, at or near the most frightened of death you will ever be. It is not a preview of the decades ahead. It is a peak, and the slope on the far side goes down.

What I see, and what it has to do with any of this

In my work the fear itself is almost never the problem. People are allowed to be frightened of dying; it is a reasonable position and I hold it myself most days.

The problem is that it does not get converted into anything. People think about this, privately, for ten or fifteen years, and write nothing down and tell nobody. Then one evening somebody is in a bed in my unit and cannot speak for themselves, and I ask the family what he would have wanted, and they look at each other, and one of them says we never talked about it.

That sentence is the single most common thing said to me in the worst conversations I have. And the people saying it are not negligent. They are ordinary families who had every intention of getting to it.

Two conversations, and one of them takes a minute

  • Who speaks for youIf you cannot speak, somebody will be asked. Every state and territory has a legal way to name that person, and the forms differ, and I am not going to pretend the paperwork is the part that matters. The part that matters is that the person knows, and that the rest of your family knows it is them. A family who agree on who speaks is worth more at two in the morning than a document nobody can find.
  • What matters to youNot a form covering forty scenarios. One or two sentences in plain language about what you would want and what you would not. People think this has to be comprehensive. It does not. Almost anything beats nothing, because nothing is what we usually have.

How rare is it? Among Australians over sixty-five — the group most likely to have got to it — around three in ten have any advance care planning document at all in their health record, and only a fraction of those are the legally binding kind. If that is where the over-sixty-fives are, you can guess where the fifty-five-year-olds are.

When the thinking is not useful

There is a difference between a fear that arrives, says its piece and settles, and a preoccupation that will not leave. If it is keeping you awake, or you are checking your body for signs, or it has started making your life smaller rather than sharper, that is worth taking to your general practitioner.

It is common, it is treatable, and it is not a character flaw. Doctors are not surprised by it and you will not be the first one that week.

If someone you love ends up in intensive care

This is the part I would most like you to have read in advance, because nobody takes anything in on the first day.

The room will be worse than you expect and the news will usually be better than the room. There will be more equipment than seems reasonable, and a great deal of it is monitoring rather than treatment. The alarms mostly mean that a number has stepped outside a range somebody set, which is how they are designed to behave. If something were urgent, you would see people walking quickly, and you would not have to work it out from the noise.

A few things that are worth knowing before you are standing there.

  • Sedation is not sleepIt is a drugged state, and people drift up through it without looking any different. Assume you are heard. Say the ordinary things — who is at school, who fed the dog, who sends their love. Familiar voices genuinely help, and the ordinary is more use than the profound.
  • Confusion is expectedA great many patients become muddled, and some are frightened and convinced of things that are not happening. It is common, it is usually temporary, and it is not madness and not a sign of brain damage. Your calm presence and a window with daylight in it do more than you would think.
  • Appoint one personOne family member who rings, and who then tells everyone else. It is kinder to the family and it is much safer for the patient, because the story stops drifting as it is relayed. Ask for a time of day when you can expect a call, and get the name of the person to ask for.
  • Bring the medicine listIf there is one on a phone, that is enough. The family who walk in with a photograph of the boxes change what happens in the first hour.
  • Write your questions downYou will not remember them. You will also not remember the answers, so take notes, or bring somebody whose job is to take them.
  • You may be asked what they would have wantedNot because anybody is giving up, but because in this age group it has usually never been discussed — so when I ask, I am asking you to guess, at the worst possible moment. Knowing the answer is a kindness you can only give in advance.
  • Eat something, and go home and sleepYou are no use to anyone on day nine if you have not slept since day one, and day nine is a real possibility. Nothing will be decided in the hours you are away, and the staff will ring you.

And one thing about afterwards, because it is routinely a shock. People do not leave intensive care and resume. They come out weak, out of breath on a flight of stairs, with a memory that is not working properly and a mood that is not their own, and that can run for months. It is so usual that it has a name: post-intensive care syndrome. It is not a sign that something went wrong, and it is not weakness in somebody who has always been strong. Knowing it is coming helps more than anything else I can tell a family on the way out the door.

One more thing worth saying out loud in this age group, and it is the one most often missed. There are usually children. Nobody asks about them, because it is not on any form, and the ward will not know unless somebody says. Say it. Say who is at home, and who is looking after them, and whether that arrangement will last the week. It is the question whose answer most often turns out to need somebody to act on it.

Take home
  1. Know that it is normal, and that it peaks. You are not becoming morbid. You are at the top of a curve that comes down again.
  2. Name the person who would speak for you — and tell them, out loud, this week. One sentence. It is the highest-value minute in this whole piece.
  3. Say one thing about what matters to you. To the same person, in the same conversation. It does not have to be eloquent.
  4. Write it down if your state has a form, and tell your general practitioner it exists. But do the conversation first; the form without the conversation is the thing we cannot find.
  5. If it has become a preoccupation, see your general practitioner.

And the dawning

Life expectancy at birth in Australia is now about eighty-one for men and eighty-five for women. From fifty-five that is a long way off, and for most people reading this the honest answer is that there is a great deal of time left and this is not the beginning of the end of anything.

But the arithmetic is not really the point, and I do not think it is what is keeping anybody awake. What has actually happened is that you have been handed, unasked and free of charge, a piece of information that most people do not act on until there is no choice left in the acting. It arrives early, which is the generous part. It arrives while every option is still open and while the conversation is still an easy one to have over a cup of tea rather than a hard one to have in a corridor.

So have it, and then put it down. That is what the fear is for. It is not asking you to dwell on it; it is asking you to do one small thing and get on with the rest, which on the evidence is likely to be considerable.

Ask me anything

Really — anything. There is no such thing as a question that is too basic.

This one invites two in particular. If you have ever sat beside someone in an intensive care unit, what did nobody tell you that you wish somebody had? I would like to put the answers into a future page, and the ones that come from families are better than the ones that come from us.

And second, the one I am most curious about. If you have had the conversation — told somebody who should speak for you, and roughly what you would want — how did you start it? That is the part people say they cannot manage, and I suspect the answer is a sentence somebody has already found and the rest of us have not. If you have not had it, I would like to know what stopped you, and I am not asking in order to tell you off.

I cannot give advice about a particular person or a particular illness, and I will say so plainly if that is what is being asked. Everything else is fair game.
