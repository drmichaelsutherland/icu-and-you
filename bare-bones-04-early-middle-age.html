ICU and You  ·  Bare Bones  ·  Number 4

Early middle age

Ten points. Five minutes. Nothing else.

About Bare Bones

Everything else on this site takes its time. This does not. Bare Bones is one topic, stripped to the points that carry the most weight, with no discussion, no argument and no stories.

It is for the shift you are about to start, the term you have just been allocated, or the morning you realise the topic is coming and you have read none of it. Five minutes, ten points, nothing decorative.

It is deliberately not enough. If a point lands, the rest of the fortnight is where it is explained — but a reader who only ever read these would still be better off than one who read nothing.

Thirty to forty-five  ·  ten points

  1. 1 This is the band where the two lines cross. External causes lead every Australian age band up to forty-four; from forty-five, coronary heart disease takes over in men. The same unit will hold both on the same night, and nothing can be read off the age alone — the thirty-two-year-old is probably an event, the forty-four-year-old probably a disease. The fifteen years between are where the weight, the alcohol, the blood pressure and the untreated lipid accumulate without anybody writing them down.
  2. 2 The deliberate overdose here is usually on medicines somebody prescribed, so look for a combination rather than one agent at a toxic level. The one that gets missed is serotonin toxicity: clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation and hyperthermia, worse in the legs than the arms, within hours. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is its mirror image — days rather than hours, rigidity rather than clonus, reflexes down rather than up. Get the full medicine list from the GP or the pharmacy; the hospital record will not have it.
  3. 3 An Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander patient in this band carries the illness burden of someone much older, and the raw numbers hide it. In 330,712 Australian ICU admissions, median age at admission was 51.2 years against 66.5 — fifteen years younger — with the same illness severity on arrival (median APACHE III 45 against 46). Unadjusted twelve-month mortality was identical, 15.8% against 15.7%. Adjusted for age, diagnosis, severity and socio-economic status it was 1.24 (95% CI 1.16–1.33). The unadjusted figure reassures; the adjusted one does not.
  4. 4 Heart failure, not heart attack, fills the cardiac bed at this age. In 29,035 cardiac intensive care admissions, heart failure led in the eighteen to thirty-nines (26% against 20%) while acute coronary syndrome led in the older group (29% against 11%). Of those needing mechanical circulatory support, 29% of the young received ECMO against 10%. Cardiogenic shock here is likelier to need a therapy your hospital may not have, so the conversation about where they should be starts early — in a regional unit, a retrieval conversation.
  5. 5 When the heart of a forty-year-old fails the cause is usually findable, and several reverse. Alcohol accounts for 23 to 47% of what gets called idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy. Atrial fibrillation may be cause rather than consequence — with ablation, ejection fraction normalised in 58% against 9%. Up to 20 to 30% is monogenic, and screening first-degree relatives finds disease in 9 to 22% at the first visit. Chemotherapy, infection, iron, sarcoid and valves fill the rest. The family history has the longest reach of anything on this page.
  6. 6 Chest pain in a woman of thirty-eight is not reassuring, and both obvious reflexes are wrong. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection is an intramural haematoma, not a plaque. Do not thrombolyse it — lysing into a dissection extends the haematoma and can occlude a vessel that would have healed — and do not stent it, because a wire can enter the false lumen and propagate it. Conservative management is the default, 83% in the largest cohort. The patient most at risk of the wrong reflex is the one described: young, female, troponin rising, and a team that has pattern-matched to a STEMI.
  7. 7 The drugs we now give have critical illnesses of their own. Myocarditis from an immune checkpoint inhibitor occurs in about 1%, at a median of 27 to 34 days from the first dose — a first-cycle event, not a late one — and kills 27 to 50%. Troponin is raised in 94%, so it is the test to send in anyone on these drugs who feels unwell, and methylprednisolone is started on suspicion rather than after histology. Clozapine has its own Australian monitoring: weekly troponin and CRP for four weeks, and troponin above twice the upper limit of normal, or CRP above 100 mg/L, means stop and get an echo. Ask what they are on and when they started it — the answer dates the illness.
  8. 8 A normal blood sugar does not exclude ketoacidosis. On an SGLT2 inhibitor — now prescribed for diabetes, heart failure and kidney disease — it arrives with a glucose that looks fine. The Australian definition is blood ketones above 1.0 mmol/L with a base excess below −5; under 14 mmol/L of glucose makes it euglycaemic. The error that does the harm is withholding insulin because the sugar is normal: insulin switches off ketogenesis, and the glucose infusion exists so that you can keep giving it. Measure blood ketones, not urine. Stop the drug three days before a procedure needing an overnight stay.
  9. 9 A first episode of psychosis at thirty with neurological signs is not a first episode of psychosis. Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis runs in sequence: a psychiatric prodrome of weeks, then a movement disorder — orofacial dyskinesia above all — then seizures, autonomic instability and a falling conscious state. About 78% are women and roughly 28% have an ovarian teratoma, whose removal is part of the treatment and works better early. Cerebrospinal fluid antibodies beat serum, so a negative serum does not close the question, and a normal MRI excludes nothing. Antipsychotics are not the treatment and are poorly tolerated — the drug reaction gets blamed for the dyskinesia and the diagnosis recedes.
  10. 10 Be careful with previously fit and well. It delays the diagnosis of the disease that has been accumulating unrecorded through this whole band, and it does the work of a decision nobody has made — he's young, he'll tolerate it sounds like a clinical judgement and is often a substitute for one. Registry data do show young adults getting more of everything and dying less, but those comparisons are unadjusted and the comparator is everybody over forty. And the aftermath is no footnote here: new physical, cognitive or psychological problems are the rule after critical illness, and at forty the patient has dependants, a mortgage, and four decades for whatever does not recover to run.
