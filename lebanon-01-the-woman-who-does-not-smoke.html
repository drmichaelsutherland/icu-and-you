The woman who does not smoke

The arghileh, and the question that goes straight past it

A new column

أهلاً وسهلاً — ahlan wa sahlan is the Arabic welcome, and it is a contraction of something longer: halalta ahlan wa nazalta sahlan, or thereabouts. Ahl is kin. Sahl is the plain — level ground, the easy kind rather than the rough. You have come among your own people, and you have set down on soft going. It may be the most hospitable sentence in any language I have met.

Which is the right place to start, because this first column is about hospitality, and about the part of it that ends up in front of us thirty years later.

A woman of fifty-eight, born in Tripoli, came to Australia at twenty-two. She is on your list for the morning. You ask whether she smokes, and she says no.

She is not minimising, she is not embarrassed, and she is not lying. She has never bought a packet of cigarettes in her life. She has also had an arghileh going three or four evenings a week since she was in her twenties.

The question was about cigarettes

A cigarette is solitary, quick, portable and countable. You can say twenty a day and everybody knows what you mean. The arghileh is none of those things. It comes out in the evening when there are people in the house. It takes an hour, often two. It is passed around. It belongs with the coffee and the plate of cut fruit, which is to say it belongs to the category of things you put in front of a guest.

Nobody counts them, because nobody counts hospitality. So when the form asks do you smoke and offers a box for yes and a box for no, a great many people tick no, accurately, by their own lights.

The word. أرجيلة — arghileh in Lebanon, also written argileh or arkileh; shisha further south and east; narghile in Turkish; hookah in English. Arabic took it from Ottoman Turkish, which took it from Persian. One object, and a different name in every country it passed through on the way here.

What changed in the 1990s

The pipe is old. What goes in it is not, and that distinction is the whole reason this column sits in this block rather than another one.

Ma’assel — tobacco fermented with molasses and glycerol and then flavoured — is an early twentieth-century Egyptian idea, but the sweet, mild, apple-and-mint version became general in the early 1990s. Before that the arghileh largely belonged to older men in cafés. Afterwards it belonged to everybody, and in particular to women and to the young.

Do the arithmetic on that. Somebody who was twenty-five when it happened is fifty-six now. The first generation to smoke flavoured ma’assel straight through adult life is not elderly and is not a future problem. It is arriving in late middle age this year, which is another way of saying it is arriving in our clinics.

What it costs

The World Health Organization’s Eastern Mediterranean office puts a one-hour session at between a hundred and two hundred times the smoke volume of one cigarette, and one head of unflavoured tobacco at about seventy cigarettes’ worth of nicotine.

The water filters nothing that matters. That is the single most durable belief about the device anywhere it is used, and it is simply false. Some mixtures are sold as containing no tar, which is technically true of the tin and irrelevant to the lung, because the tar is made when the tobacco burns. And the charcoal sitting on top contributes carbon monoxide that the tobacco never produced, which is why a waterpipe smoker can arrive with a carboxyhaemoglobin that makes no sense until somebody asks the right question.

On how common it is: in a survey of 2,500 Lebanese adults run between February and April 2024, sixty-six per cent reported using tobacco or nicotine in some form. Read that as reach rather than daily habit — the report’s own footnote concedes the question did not require regular use. The split by sex is the arresting part. Among women who used tobacco, fifty-seven per cent used a waterpipe; among men, twenty-six per cent. More Lebanese men smoke overall. But among women who smoke, the waterpipe is the ordinary form of it.

The question that works

Do not ask whether somebody smokes. Ask what they smoke, and name the thing: cigarettes, arghileh, both?

Then ask how many evenings a week and how long each time, because the unit here is the session and not the stick. An hour is conventional. Two is common. A pack-year column has nowhere to put this, so write it down in sessions and say so.

And ask it of women at least as carefully as of men. On the Lebanese figures she is the one more likely to be at a pipe than a packet, and she is precisely the person a yes-or-no box was built to miss.

Why an Australian unit should care

At the 2021 census 248,430 people claimed Lebanese ancestry and 87,343 were born in Lebanon, nearly 66,000 of those in Greater Sydney. The thirty thousand and more who came out of the civil war between 1975 and 1990 arrived largely as young adults. They are now somewhere between fifty and seventy-five. That is this block, almost exactly.

The best Australian evidence about them pulled 346 Lebanon-born and 302 Australian-born participants of Lebanese ethnicity out of the 45 and Up Study and set them against 36,707 participants of Australian ethnicity. The Lebanon-born group had higher odds of current smoking, of not meeting the physical activity guidelines, and of sleeping outside seven to nine hours. They also had lower odds of sitting more than seven hours a day, of eating processed meat, of drinking more than fourteen standard drinks a week, and of eating less than two serves of fruit. Their unhealthy behaviours clustered less than the reference group’s did, not more.

I should say that the paper publishes its tables as images, so I have the directions and not the effect sizes. The directions are the point. This is not a community doing everything wrong. It is a community doing several things conspicuously better than the Australian average and one thing worse — and the one thing worse happens to be the one our screening question is least able to detect.

Put it beside this block’s piece for medical students, which argues that in late middle age the history is the diagnostic test. Here is that argument with a worked example. The test exists, it takes about nine seconds, and it fails on the wording.

And another thing

What actually moves it. A 2025 study interviewed ten health and community workers in New South Wales — four of them of Lebanese background — about what shifts health behaviour in Arabic-speaking communities. Smoking came up as a priority. What they said does not work is illness, and cost.

What they said does work is family. One worker’s formulation, which I have not managed to improve on: “your family need you, your kids need you and they want you when they get married”.

They also said the message travels on trust rather than on content — a GP, a community figure, somebody speaking the language, somewhere informal, telling a story instead of giving a lecture. And they said some subjects have to be renamed before they can be raised at all, and that cancer and mental health often simply are not discussed. Ten people is a small study and eight of them were women. But that last point is worth carrying into a family meeting rather than discovering halfway through one.
