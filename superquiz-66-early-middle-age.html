Early middle age

Five questions — companion to Mind Map #37, Early middle age

Open book. No time limit, and as many attempts as you like. Look things up — that is the point. Three or more correct earns a certificate you can print or save.

  1. 1A 41-year-old with type 2 diabetes is admitted after two days of vomiting and abdominal pain, having taken bowel preparation for a colonoscopy. Venous gas: pH 7.18, bicarbonate 12 mmol/L, standard base excess −13. Blood glucose 9.8 mmol/L. Blood ketones 4.1 mmol/L. Her regular medicines are metformin and empagliflozin. The most important next step is to:

    This is euglycaemic ketoacidosis on an SGLT2 inhibitor, and the whole danger of it is in the number that looks reassuring. The Australian joint alert — Diabetes Society, Diabetes Educators, ANZCA and the New Zealand society — defines presumed ketoacidosis as blood ketones above 1.0 mmol/L with a standard base excess below −5, and presumed euglycaemic ketoacidosis as the same findings with a blood glucose below 14 mmol/L. This patient meets it three times over. In the alert's own words, a normal or only modestly elevated plasma glucose level does not exclude the diagnosis.

    Why A is the error that does the harm. Insulin is not being given to lower the glucose. It is being given to switch off ketogenesis, and nothing else will. The glucose infusion exists precisely so that you can keep the insulin running — the alert says to add glucose as an infusion once the blood sugar is below 15 mmol/L, with hourly glucose, ketones and gases. Withholding insulin because the sugar is normal is the single commonest mistake in this presentation, and it is made by people who know how to treat ordinary ketoacidosis perfectly well.

    The bowel preparation is not incidental. It is one of the named precipitants, along with prolonged fasting, carbohydrate restriction, dehydration, surgery and intercurrent illness. For a procedure needing an overnight stay, or any bowel preparation with carbohydrate restriction starting the day before, the alert says to stop the SGLT2 inhibitor for at least three days — two days before plus the day itself. For a day procedure without bowel preparation, the day of the procedure is enough. Watch the fixed-dose combinations: stopping Jardiamet or Xigduo stops the metformin too.

    Two practical traps. Urine ketones are unreliable here — measure blood ketones or beta-hydroxybutyrate. And ketones up to about 1.7 mmol/L can turn up after colonoscopy preparation in someone on nothing at all, so it is the acidosis that makes the diagnosis, not the ketones alone.

    Australian Diabetes Society, ADEA, ANZCA and NZSSD, joint alert on ketoacidosis with SGLT2 inhibitor use, July 2022 update.

  2. 2A 38-year-old woman who has never been pregnant presents with central chest pain and a rising troponin. Angiography shows a long, smooth narrowing of the mid left anterior descending artery with no atheroma, and the appearance is accepted as spontaneous coronary artery dissection. TIMI 3 flow is preserved and she is pain-free and haemodynamically stable. Which statement is correct?

    Most spontaneous coronary artery dissection heals on its own, and the instinct that serves you well in atherosclerotic infarction serves you badly here. Conservative management is the default: the figures run from about two thirds to seventy per cent in expert practice, and 83% in the largest published cohort. Intervention is for ongoing ischaemia, left main involvement, or haemodynamic instability.

    Why stenting is not protective. The lesion is an intramural haematoma within the vessel wall, not a plaque. A wire or a stent can enter the false lumen, propagate the haematoma and extend the dissection along the vessel — so the procedure intended to secure it is one of the things that makes it worse. Technical failure rates in SCAD percutaneous intervention are substantially higher than in atherosclerotic disease.

    Why thrombolysis is the one you must not do. There is no thrombus to lyse. Lysing into a dissection risks extending the intramural haematoma and converting a vessel that would have healed into one that occludes. The patient at risk of this is exactly the patient in the stem: young, female, chest pain, troponin up, and a unit that has pattern-matched to a STEMI.

    Option C is on this page because the site got it wrong once. Fibromuscular dysplasia is found in about half of women presenting with SCAD, so it feels as though it ought to predict recurrence — and it does not. In the published analysis the independent predictors of recurrent SCAD were hypertension, which raises the risk, and beta blockade, which lowers it. FMD was not an independent predictor. That correction is recorded on the SCAD podcast page and on the corrections page.

    See the SCAD podcast page on this site, and its verification record, for the sources behind each of these figures.

  3. 3A 44-year-old receiving pembrolizumab for metastatic melanoma presents 24 days after her first dose with fatigue, mild breathlessness and a new conduction abnormality on the ECG. Troponin is raised. Which statement about immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis is correct?

    Checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis is uncommon, early and lethal, and the combination is what makes it dangerous — it is rare enough that you will not think of it and fast enough that you cannot afford not to. Reported incidence is 0.3 to 1.4%. Median onset is 27 to 34 days after the first dose, with most cases inside the first 65 days, so it is a first-cycle event rather than a late one. Mortality is 27 to 50%, which is higher than almost anything else on a general oncology ward.

    Troponin is the test. It is raised in 94% of cases, which makes it the single most useful thing to send in anyone on these drugs who feels unwell — and the reason option C is exactly backwards. The ECG is frequently abnormal and conduction disease is characteristic, because the infiltrate has a particular affinity for the conducting system. Complete heart block and ventricular arrhythmia are how these patients die.

    Do not wait for the biopsy. Treatment is high-dose methylprednisolone, 500 to 1000 mg daily for three to five days, started on suspicion. Up to half are steroid-refractory and need second-line immunosuppression, so the time lost waiting for histology is time the disease uses. Biopsy and cardiac MRI are worth having where they are available and will not delay treatment.

    The general point for this age band: the drugs we now give in oncology, rheumatology and endocrinology have critical illnesses of their own, and they arrive in people whose hearts were normal a month ago. Ask what they are on and when they started it — the answer dates the illness.

    Mauriello A et al, immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis, Journal of Clinical Medicine 2026;15:814. Also covered in Mnemonic #59 on this site.

  4. 4The Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network registry reported 29,035 cardiac intensive care admissions, comparing adults aged 18 to 39 with those aged 40 and over. Which statement is correct?

    The useful finding is the first half: heart failure, not heart attack, is the leading reason a young adult is in a cardiac intensive care unit — 26% against 20% — while acute coronary syndrome leads in the older group, 29% against 11%. If your mental image of a young person in a coronary care unit is a premature infarct, it is the wrong image most of the time.

    The operationally useful number is the second half. Of those who received mechanical circulatory support, intra-aortic balloon pump was used in 52% of the young against 78% of the older, Impella in 24% against 27%, and ECMO in 29% against 10%. That tells you what a young adult in cardiogenic shock is likely to need, and therefore which conversation needs to start early, and with which hospital. In a regional unit that conversation is a retrieval conversation, and it is better had before the lactate doubles.

    Why B is wrong, and why it is the interesting wrong answer. Mortality was lower in the young — 6.5% against 10.5% in intensive care, 9.5% against 14.4% in hospital — despite longer stays and more intervention. Before anyone repeats those figures: they are unadjusted, and a thirty-two-year-old out-surviving a sixty-eight-year-old is close to a tautology. What rescues the observation is that median SOFA and lactate were identical in the two groups, and the young still died at two thirds the rate.

    The authors then raise the uncomfortable reading themselves: young patients received more of everything, and that may be an appropriately aggressive approach in people with more to gain — or it may be a risk–treatment paradox, with older patients who stood to gain more in absolute terms being treated less aggressively. That is the same argument the aphorism for this fortnight is about.

    Isath A and colleagues, Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network registry. Covered in full in Journal Club #102 on this site.

  5. 5A 31-year-old woman is admitted under the mental health team with three weeks of agitation, delusions and disorganised speech, treated as a first episode of psychosis. Over the following week she develops orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability and seizures, and is intubated. Which statement is correct?

    This is the presentation that gets a psychiatric label and keeps it until something happens that a psychiatric illness cannot explain. In a review of 633 patients, 77.8% were female with a mean age at onset of 22.6 years, and an ovarian teratoma was found in 28.1%. Psychiatric symptoms are the commonest presenting feature in adults, at 60 to 80%, and in one series 95% of patients had at least one. Most end up in intensive care.

    The sequence in the stem is the diagnosis. Psychiatric prodrome, then a movement disorder — orofacial dyskinesia above all — then seizures, autonomic instability and reduced consciousness. Any one of those alone is unremarkable. In that order, in a young woman, it is this until proven otherwise.

    Why C is wrong, and worse than wrong. Treatment is immunotherapy — high-dose corticosteroid, intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange — together with removal of the tumour, which is itself therapeutic. Resection within four months of neurological onset is associated with better outcomes, and immunotherapy works better once it is out. Antipsychotics are not the treatment, and these patients are notably intolerant of them: the drug reaction gets blamed for the dyskinesia and the diagnosis recedes further.

    Why D is wrong. MRI is normal or non-specific in a large proportion of cases, so a clean scan is no reassurance at all. The diagnosis rests on NMDA receptor antibodies, and cerebrospinal fluid is more sensitive than serum — a negative serum result does not close the question. The practical instruction is to send CSF antibodies in any young person whose first psychosis acquires neurological signs.

    It is worth knowing the outcome, because it is what justifies the search. Of a cohort of 179 patients with teratoma, about 58% had a modified Rankin score of 0 to 2 within two years, and full recovery was reported in over 80% of women with a mature teratoma. Relapse ran at about 15% with a tumour removed, and 24 to 33% without one.

    Miski H et al, Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis Associated with Ovarian Teratoma: A Comprehensive Review. Biomedicines 2026;14(9):2040.
