ICU and You  ·  Out of Africa  ·  Number 1

Too far to walk

More than a quarter of the world’s maternal deaths happen in one country — and the phrase everybody uses to explain it turns out not to survive being measured

A new column

This site has columns for Sri Lanka, China, India, Russia, Spain, Italy, France, England, Sweden and Australia, and until today not one for a continent that holds nearly a fifth of the people alive. That was an oversight rather than a decision, and this is the start of correcting it.

Out of Africa takes its name from where all of us came from, which is the only honest sense in which any of this is anybody’s. The column will travel — fifty-four countries, and no intention of treating them as one place.

It begins with Nigeria, in a fortnight about young adults, because this is the subject in which the young adult and the arithmetic meet.

In 2023 an estimated 260,000 women died of a maternal cause worldwide. About 75,000 of them died in Nigeria. One country, more than a quarter of the whole.

28.7%

Those are the numbers you will see quoted, and before going any further it is worth being honest about what kind of numbers they are.

First, the denominator

A maternal mortality ratio is deaths over live births. Nigeria’s last completed census was in 2006 — twenty years ago, disputed at the time, and never repeated. Every population figure since is a projection, and federal money and political seats are allocated by state population, which gives every state a standing reason to want a bigger number.

So the 993 carries an uncertainty interval of 718 to 1,540. That is not statistical fussiness. It is an accurate statement of how much is actually known.

And one comparison not to make

Nigeria’s 2018 Demographic and Health Survey reported a maternal mortality ratio of 512. The modelled figure for 2023 is 993. It is tempting to put those side by side and conclude the situation has nearly doubled in badness. Do not. They are different instruments — one a household survey asking women about the deaths of their sisters, the other a Bayesian model drawing on many sources — and they are not a series.

The 2024 survey, for its part, publishes no maternal mortality ratio at all. Nigeria currently has no recent national measurement of maternal death. It has an estimate.

The phrase everybody quotes

In 1994 Sereen Thaddeus and Deborah Maine published a paper in Social Science & Medicine called “Too far to walk: maternal mortality in context”. It is one of the most cited papers in global health, and it gave the field the three delays.

The first is the delay in deciding to seek care — by the woman, her family, or both. The second is the delay in reaching a facility that can actually help. The third is the delay in receiving adequate care once she is inside it.

Almost everyone who quotes the three delays drops the argument the authors built them for, which is that the first is produced by the other two. A woman does not decide late because she is ignorant. She decides late because she has a reasonably accurate idea of what is waiting: whether there is blood, whether there is an anaesthetist, and what she will be asked to pay at the door.

What happened when Nigerians measured it

Aduragbemi Banke-Thomas and colleagues in Lagos did something that sounds obvious and almost nobody had done. They traced the actual journeys of women arriving at public hospitals with obstetric emergencies — four thousand of them — and timed them.

Eighty per cent reached care within an hour. Ninety-six per cent within two.

Then they looked at the women who died. Of 4,181 women with obstetric emergencies, 182 died. Among those 182, sixty per cent had travelled ten kilometres or less, and sixty-two per cent had arrived within half an hour of setting out.

Which is to say: most of the Lagos women who died had not been too far to walk. They were already there.

This is not an argument that distance does not matter. The same group mapped two thousand emergency obstetric facilities across fifteen Nigerian cities and found that in five of them — Lagos, Kaduna, Onitsha, Port Harcourt and Benin City — the poorest fifth of women could reach exactly zero public facilities within an hour, while the wealthiest fifth in Lagos could reach nine. Distance is real, and inside a city it is a wealth gradient rather than a geographical one.

But for the women who died in the hospitals of Lagos, geography was not what killed them. Something else did, and it is worth a clinician’s attention because much of it is recognisable.

What actually fails

Start with the one that stops an intensivist in their tracks. In field surveys of oxytocin collected from Nigerian pharmacies and health facilities, seventy-four per cent of samples failed assay testing. A third of misoprostol samples failed too.

Read that again as a clinical scenario rather than a statistic. The third stage is being actively managed. The drug is drawn up and given, correctly, by somebody who knows what they are doing. And the uterus does not contract, because the ampoule has been sitting at thirty-four degrees in a supply chain with no cold storage and there is very little oxytocin left in it.

Then blood. Nigeria has a national blood service, founded in 2005 and given a statutory footing in 2021, running seventeen centres. By its own account, the overwhelming majority of blood transfused in Nigeria comes not from voluntary unpaid donors but from paid commercial donors and from families required to replace what their relative uses. That is a structural fact with an obvious bedside consequence, and it is why a woman’s relatives are sometimes out looking for donors while she is bleeding.

“Not having a steady supply of safely donated and screened blood available in our blood banks.”

Dr Adaeze Oreh, consultant family physician, Commissioner for Health in Rivers State and formerly of the National Blood Service Commission, naming the main challenge in a state with one of Nigeria’s highest maternal death rates

Then the facilities themselves. A national emergency obstetric care assessment reported in 2026 examined 913 public facilities in three states. Three and a half per cent of the basic units could perform all of the signal functions they are defined by. Among the comprehensive units — the ones that are supposed to operate and transfuse — thirty per cent could. Two per cent of basic units reported a regular oxygen supply.

And the national programme set up to attack all this reports, in its 172 priority local government areas, that ninety-one per cent now have functioning basic emergency obstetric care and twenty-six per cent have functioning comprehensive care. That pair of numbers is the whole problem in miniature. The midwife is there. The theatre and the blood are not.

And the causes are not quite what you would guess

Globally, haemorrhage leads. In the best Nigerian facility data, using the World Health Organization’s own maternal death classification, it does not always. At the University of Abuja Teaching Hospital, across five years of deaths, hypertensive disorders accounted for 36.5% and haemorrhage for 30.8%. In Ogun State’s death reviews the order reversed, haemorrhage 43% against pre-eclampsia and eclampsia at around 36%.

So the honest statement is that the two together account for two-thirds to three-quarters of deaths and which one leads depends on where you look. Two other features of the Nigerian data are worth carrying: ruptured uterus appears as a substantial category in its own right in a way it simply does not in Australian series, and anaesthetic deaths run higher than the pooled figure for comparable countries. Nearly half the women who died at Abuja died within twenty-four hours of arriving.

A word about who gets blamed

In 2026 Roy, Balogun and Banke-Thomas did something unusual: they analysed how maternal death is reported in the West African press. Across fifty-three articles, the third delay — the failure of the facility and the people in it — was invoked in eighty-five per cent of them. The second delay appeared in thirteen. The first, in six.

That is almost exactly the reverse of what Nigeria’s own death reviews find. The authors call it ping-ponging blame, and note drily that it is probably more interesting to report negligence than stock-outs. Patient-facing health workers were quoted in seven articles out of fifty-three.

Anyone writing about this is writing into a genre that has spent a decade making the Nigerian nurse the villain. Your Nigerian-trained colleagues have read that genre. It is worth not adding to it.

What is being done, and what it cost to find out

Nigeria put 2,488 midwives into 652 rural clinics from 2009, four to a clinic, to cover births around the clock. It was evaluated properly, which is more than most such schemes manage, and the evaluation found no effect on skilled birth attendance at all.

The reason is in the evaluation and it is not mysterious. By 2014 only about a third of the clinics still had four midwives. Fifty-seven per cent of local governments owed their midwives salary arrears, a median of five months’ worth. They were being paid around ₦42,000 against the ₦60,000 they had been promised. The scheme did not fail because deploying midwives does not work. It failed because the midwives were not paid, and it was Nigerian health economists who established that.

What is working tends to be specific and unglamorous. Heat-stable carbetocin — which does not need a cold chain, and so answers the oxytocin problem directly — was registered in Nigeria in 2022 and seeded into three states; within months one implementation study had given it to nearly fourteen thousand women. In Kano, 145 primary care facilities across 26 local government areas have been training community health extension workers to recognise and start treating haemorrhage and eclampsia, with senior midwives mentoring them.

“Hafsat stabilised the woman by administering magnesium sulfate before we referred her.”

Yusif Adamu, officer in charge, Jaen primary health care centre, Kano State

That sentence is what task-shifting looks like when it works, and it is worth noticing how ordinary it is.

The other side of the ledger

Nigeria sits on the World Health Organization’s health workforce support and safeguards list — fifty-five countries where the density of doctors, nurses and midwives is low enough that active international recruitment is formally discouraged. Nigeria’s own workforce migration policy records where its departing doctors go: sixty-eight per cent to the United Kingdom, ten to Canada, seven to the United States, and three per cent to Australia.

Three per cent is not nothing. If you work in an Australian hospital you have colleagues who trained in the country this piece is about, and they did not leave a country without doctors so much as a country that could not pay, equip or protect the ones it had.

Which brings me to the thing I most want to leave you with. Nearly every figure above — the Lagos travel times, the cause-of-death breakdown, the media analysis, the midwife evaluation — was produced by Nigerian researchers working in Nigerian institutions on their own country’s problem. Banke-Thomas, Afolabi, Akaba, Okonofua, Sageer, Onwujekwe, Sotunsa. The corrective to the lazy version of this story did not come from outside. It came from the people who stayed.

“We can keep our women alive if we look inward and maximise our opportunities.”

Professor John Sotunsa, Professor of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Babcock University, in his inaugural lecture — Beyond Superficial Success: Holistic Approaches to Maternal Mortality

Too far to walk is a very good title. It was never meant to be the diagnosis, and in the country where it matters most, it mostly is not.

Over to you

That was something of the Nigerian picture. What does this column need to cover next, and from where?

Fifty-four countries and one column. I would rather be directed than guess — so if you trained somewhere on the continent, or have worked there, tell me what an Australian unit consistently misunderstands about it. A disease that is ordinary where you come from. A way of doing something that is done better there than here. A number that gets quoted about your country that is wrong.

And if you are a Nigerian clinician and I have got something in this piece wrong, I would particularly like to hear it, and I will correct it on the page.
