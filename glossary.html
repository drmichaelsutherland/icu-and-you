ICU and You

Glossary

The terms used across the site, in plain words

Dotted underlines elsewhere on the site open these definitions where you are reading, without taking you away from the page. This is the same list, gathered in one place.

4-2-1 rule

Maintenance fluid: 4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg, then 2 mL/kg/h for the next 10, then 1 mL/kg/h beyond that. Resuscitation boluses in children are 10 mL/kg, which is a different calculation.

Absolute risk

The chance of something happening, given as a number out of a whole — nine extra cases per ten thousand women a year. It is the figure a person can actually weigh, and it is the one most often left out.

Advance care directive

A document in which somebody sets out, while they can, what treatment they would and would not want. What it is called and how binding it is varies by state and territory.

AODR

Australian Organ Donor Register

The national register of people who have recorded a decision about organ and tissue donation. Registration is far lower than support for donation, which is most of the gap worth closing.

APACHE

Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation

A score of how sick somebody was in their first day of intensive care, built to compare groups of patients rather than to forecast one. It describes a population, not the person in the bed.

ARDS

acute respiratory distress syndrome

Widespread inflammation of the lungs following another insult — sepsis, aspiration, trauma, pancreatitis — leaving them stiff and leaking fluid. The treatment is to support the lungs gently while whatever caused it is dealt with.

Arterial line

A fine catheter in an artery, usually at the wrist, giving a blood pressure reading with every heartbeat and a place to take blood from. It measures pressure continuously, which a cuff cannot.

Brain death

The irreversible loss of all function of the brain, including the brainstem, determined by clinical testing or, where that is not possible, by imaging of blood flow. It is death in law and in fact, not a severe form of coma.

BRUE

brief resolved unexplained event

An episode in an infant under one of altered breathing, colour, tone or responsiveness, which has fully resolved by the time you see them. The risk lies in what might be missed, not in the event itself.

Capillary refill time

The seconds taken for colour to return after pressing the skin for five seconds. Over two seconds suggests poor perfusion, and in a child it moves before the blood pressure does.

Ceiling of care

A decision, recorded in advance, about how far treatment will go: which interventions are to be offered and which are not. Its absence is not a decision to do everything; it usually means nobody has had the conversation.

Central line

central venous catheter

A catheter threaded into one of the large veins near the heart, for drugs that would damage a small vein and for pressures a small vein cannot measure. The risk is infection in the bloodstream, which rises with every day it stays in.

Comorbidity

Another condition the patient has besides the one that brought them in. It often matters more to the outcome than the admitting diagnosis does.

Confidence interval

The range a result would plausibly fall in if the study were repeated. Its width tells you how much the study actually settled, and a range that crosses no difference has not settled it.

Confounding

When something else, related to both the exposure and the outcome, produces an association that looks causal. The classic example on this site is the non-drinkers who had stopped drinking because they were already ill.

CRP

C-reactive protein

A blood protein that rises with inflammation of almost any cause — infection, injury, autoimmune disease, a tumour. It tells you something is going on. It does not tell you what.

Cruising

Walking while holding onto furniture, usually from around ten to twelve months. It matters because a bruise in a baby not yet cruising has almost certainly been inflicted.

DCD

donation after circulatory death

Organ donation that follows the heart stopping, rather than the determination of brain death. The timing is tighter, because the organs are without circulation from the moment death is declared.

Delirium

An acute disturbance of attention and awareness, fluctuating through the day, caused by illness, drugs or the environment. The quiet form is commoner than the agitated one and is missed far more often.

DKA

diabetic ketoacidosis

Insulin deficiency severe enough that the body burns fat for fuel and the blood turns acid. The glucose coming down is not the same thing as the acidosis clearing, and the potassium falls as the insulin works.

ECMO

extracorporeal membrane oxygenation

A machine that takes blood out of the body, adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, and returns it — doing the work of a heart, a pair of lungs, or both. It buys time for something else to recover; it is a bridge rather than a treatment.

Euglycaemic ketoacidosis

Ketoacidosis with a blood glucose that looks unremarkable, usually in somebody taking an SGLT2 inhibitor. The harm is done by withholding insulin because the sugar is reassuring — insulin is what switches the ketones off.

Extubation

Taking the breathing tube out. The decision is about far more than the lungs — the patient has to be awake enough, strong enough and able to protect their own airway, and roughly one in six has to be reintubated.

FRC

functional residual capacity

The volume of air left in the lungs after a normal breath out — the oxygen reservoir you draw on between breaths. Infants have proportionally less of it, which is why they desaturate so fast.

Grunting

An expiratory noise made by breathing out against a partly closed glottis. The infant is generating their own PEEP, and it is a late and serious sign.

Hazard ratio

A comparison of how fast an outcome is arriving in two groups, used when timing matters as well as whether. A hazard ratio of 0.30 means the event is arriving at under a third of the rate, not that a third as many people have it.

HIE

hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy

Brain injury from a period of inadequate oxygen or blood flow around the time of birth. Therapeutic cooling must begin within six hours, which is why the decision is made clinically.

ICE

Integrated Clinical Examination

A clinical examination assessing history, examination and reasoning together rather than as separate skills — a common format in the clinical years of an Australian medical program.

Incidence

How many new cases appear in a population over a period of time. It answers how often this starts, which is a different question from how many people have it.

Inotrope

A drug that makes the heart contract harder. It is a different job from a vasopressor, which squeezes the vessels, and the two are often confused because several drugs do a little of both.

INR

international normalised ratio

A standardised measure of how long blood takes to clot, used to monitor warfarin and to judge liver function. Standardised so that a result means the same thing in any laboratory.

Intraosseous

Vascular access drilled directly into the marrow cavity of a bone, usually the proximal tibia. Anything that can be given intravenously can be given this way, and it takes under a minute.

Intubation

Placing a tube through the mouth or nose into the windpipe, so that a machine can move air in and out. It takes over the airway, which is why it almost always requires sedation.

ISBAR

Identification, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation

A structure for handing over or escalating a patient. Its point is that the listener gets your concern in the first sentence rather than the last.

Mandatory reporter

A person legally obliged to report a reasonable suspicion of child abuse or neglect. All clinicians in New South Wales are mandatory reporters. Suspicion is the threshold, not proof.

Mechanical ventilation

A machine doing some or all of the work of breathing, through a tube in the windpipe or a tight mask. It buys time; it does not treat the reason the lungs or the muscles failed.

Meta-analysis

Pooling the results of several studies into one estimate. It inherits every weakness of the studies that went in, which is why a tidy pooled figure can be built on untidy foundations.

Modified Rankin Scale

mRS

A 0-to-6 measure of day-to-day dependence after a stroke, where 0 is no symptoms at all and 6 is death. Nought to two is the usual definition of functional independence.

NETS

Newborn and paediatric Emergency Transport Service

The New South Wales retrieval service for newborns and children, reachable on 1300 36 2500. They expect to be called early and uncertain rather than late and sure.

NICU

neonatal intensive care unit

An intensive care unit for newborn babies, including those born very prematurely. Staffed and equipped quite differently from an adult or a paediatric unit.

NIHSS

National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale

An eleven-item score of stroke severity, 0 to 42, in whose units the referral thresholds for clot retrieval are written. It has no item for vertigo, double vision or swallowing, so it reads a stroke at the back of the brain poorly.

Nocebo

The harm half of the placebo effect — symptoms produced by the expectation of a side effect rather than by the drug. The symptoms are real. The attribution is not.

Number needed to treat

NNT

How many people must receive a treatment for one of them to benefit. Forty to prevent one bleed means thirty-nine people take the drug and get nothing from it.

Odds ratio

A comparison of the odds of something happening in two groups. It behaves like a risk ratio when the outcome is rare and exaggerates the difference when it is common, which is a frequent source of overstatement.

OSCE

Objective Structured Clinical Examination

An examination of a series of short timed stations, each testing one skill against a fixed mark sheet. The structure is the point: every candidate meets the same stations in the same order.

Pack-year

One pack of twenty cigarettes a day for one year. Twenty a day for twenty years is twenty pack-years, and so is forty a day for ten. It is a measure of cigarettes, so it has nowhere to put a waterpipe.

Paediatric assessment triangle

Appearance, work of breathing and circulation to skin, judged from the end of the cot before you touch the child. It grades a sick infant in seconds and survives handling, which measurements do not.

PEEP

positive end-expiratory pressure

Pressure held in the airway at the end of expiration to stop alveoli collapsing. It is what keeps the lung open between breaths.

PICU

paediatric intensive care unit

An intensive care unit for children beyond the newborn period. In Australia they are concentrated in a small number of major centres, which is why retrieval services matter so much.

Post-intensive care syndrome

PICS

The physical, cognitive and psychological problems that persist after critical illness — in the patient, and often in the family as well. Surviving intensive care is where this begins rather than where it ends.

Preductal

Measured upstream of the ductus arteriosus — in practice, the right hand. It reflects the blood reaching the brain, which is why the saturation probe goes there in a newborn.

Prevalence

How many people have a condition at one moment. A disease that is common but short-lived has high incidence and low prevalence; a lifelong one can be the other way round.

Pulse pressure

The gap between the systolic and diastolic pressures — 120 over 80 is a pulse pressure of 40. It narrows early in shock, while the systolic is still normal, which is what makes it useful.

Recession

The indrawing of soft tissue between and below the ribs on inspiration — subcostal, intercostal or sternal. It means the child is generating large negative pressures to move air.

Relative risk

How much a risk changes, given as a proportion of what it was: a quarter more, or double. It can be large while the absolute change is tiny, which is how a true number becomes a misleading one.

SCAD

spontaneous coronary artery dissection

A tear in the wall of a coronary artery, with blood collecting inside the wall and squeezing the vessel shut. It is not a plaque, which is why clot-busting drugs and stents can each make it worse.

Sedation

Drugs given to reduce awareness, distress or movement, usually so that a tube or a procedure can be tolerated. It is not the same as pain relief, and it is not sleep.

Sepsis

Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by the body’s own response to an infection. The damage is done by the response as much as by the organism, which is why it can go on worsening after the antibiotics are right.

Septic screen

The set of cultures and samples taken before antibiotics when infection is suspected — typically blood, urine and, in an infant, cerebrospinal fluid.

Septic shock

Sepsis in which the blood pressure cannot be held up without drugs, and the lactate stays high despite fluid. It is a subset of sepsis defined by how much support the circulation needs.

SGLT2 inhibitor

sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor

A drug that makes the kidney pass glucose into the urine, now prescribed for heart failure and kidney disease as well as diabetes. It can cause ketoacidosis while the blood sugar still looks normal.

SOFA

Sequential Organ Failure Assessment

A score of how many organ systems are failing and how badly, from 0 to 4 in each of six. Most of its value is in the direction it moves over days rather than in any single reading.

STEMI

ST-elevation myocardial infarction

A heart attack in which a coronary artery has closed completely and the electrocardiogram shows it. It is the kind that goes straight for reopening the vessel, by catheter or by drug.

Substitute decision-maker

The person who speaks for a patient who cannot speak for themselves. Who that is, and how they come to be it, is set by the law of the state or territory rather than by the hospital.

SUDI

sudden unexpected death in infancy

The sudden death of an infant that was not anticipated. It remains the leading cause of death between one month and one year, and the coroner is involved in every case.

Tracheostomy

A tube placed into the windpipe through the front of the neck, instead of through the mouth. It is usually done when breathing support is going to be needed for weeks rather than days, and it is generally more comfortable.

Triage

Sorting patients by how urgently they need attention rather than by who arrived first. It is a tool for allocating a scarce resource, and the scarce resource is usually time.

Vasopressor

A drug that raises blood pressure by tightening blood vessels — noradrenaline most often. It moves the number on the monitor, which is not always the same as improving the blood supply to an organ.
