The aphorism for this topic
“When they’re young, throw everything at them.”
How to use an aphorism

An aphorism is a compressed argument, not a rule. It earns its shape by leaving things out, which is exactly what makes it memorable and what makes it arguable. Every one in this series is contestable, and chosen because it is.

Each is a mantra, a paradigm, a truism or occasionally a plain myth — something repeated in our units often enough that nobody examines it any more. The piece sets out the case for it, the case against it, and the case for something in between, and then asks which you hold.

There is no right answer waiting at the bottom. The point is to find out what you actually believe and why, because the beliefs that run your practice at three in the morning are mostly ones you have never said out loud.

Preamble

It sounds like generosity, and part of it is arithmetic. Absolute benefit scales with the years a person has left, so the same intervention buys more in a thirty-two-year-old than in anybody else in the unit. Nobody says the sentence meaning anything unkind.

And the behaviour is real. This fortnight’s journal club is a registry of twenty-nine thousand cardiac intensive care admissions in which the 18-to-39s received more of nearly everything — more mechanical ventilation, more invasive haemodynamic assessment, more mechanical circulatory support — and where, among those supported, 29% went on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation against 10% of the older patients. They stayed longer. And they died at roughly two-thirds the rate.

So the aphorism describes something that happens, and the outcome appears to vindicate it. The difficulty is what else could be producing that picture.

The authors of that paper raise it against themselves, which is to their credit. The risk–treatment paradox is the well-described finding that the patients at highest risk — who therefore stand to gain most in absolute terms — are the least likely to receive the treatment that would help them. If that is what is operating here, then part of the survival gap in that registry is not physiology at all. It is us, treating the person we expect to live as though they will, and the person we expect to die as though they must.

Which would make the aphorism self-fulfilling, and the data supporting it circular.

Except that somebody tested the other half

The sentence has a silent converse: and when they are old, do not. That converse has been through a randomised trial, which is more than most aphorisms manage.

ICE-CUB 2 cluster-randomised twenty-four French hospitals to a programme that systematically promoted intensive care admission for critically ill patients aged seventy-five and over — and not the frailest of them either: preserved functional status, no cachexia, no active cancer, median age eighty-five. The programme worked as designed. ICU admission rose from 34% to 61%.

Six-month mortality was 45% in the intervention arm against 39% in the control. Adjusted, the relative risk was 1.05, confidence interval 0.96 to 1.14 — no benefit. In-hospital mortality was 30% against 21%. Nearly doubling the rate at which these patients were admitted to intensive care saved none of them, and the in-hospital figures point the other way.

The trial has its critics, and they are not wrong: the arms differed at baseline in illness severity, recruitment was uneven, and it is one country with one set of admission customs. It should not be read as proof that intensive care harms the elderly. But it is a genuine test of the thing the aphorism quietly assumes, and the thing the aphorism quietly assumes survived it.

What “everything” actually costs

The other half of the sentence deserves the same scrutiny, and gets far less. Everything is spoken as though it were free, or at worst wasted. It is neither.

A thirty-eight-year-old who survives an extracorporeal run and three weeks of ventilation walks out with the longest post-intensive care syndrome in the building, because they have the longest left to carry it. The physical, cognitive and psychological consequences that an eighty-year-old lives with for two years, this patient lives with for forty. Throwing everything at the young is not the costless half of the aphorism. It is the half where the cost is deferred and nobody is measuring it.

The same mistake, caught once already on this site

Podcast 60 turned on a claim that outcomes from extracorporeal support are better in pregnant women. Survival does run higher — but every available comparison is unmatched, and the registry authors themselves put the gap down to youth, absent comorbidity and closer monitoring. Pregnancy is not protective; being thirty and otherwise well is.

Which is the whole of this aphorism, wearing a different costume. The thing doing the work is never the category. It is the reserve underneath it.

And the word it uses is the wrong one

The sentence says young. What it means is has reserve, and has no comorbidity worth naming. Those travel together often enough that the shorthand mostly works, and the places it fails are the places that matter.

It fails at the seventy-year-old who runs, takes nothing, and has better physiology than half the unit — who gets less because the number is wrong. It fails at the thirty-five-year-old with end-stage disease who gets an extracorporeal run that was never going to work, because the number is right. The aphorism gets both of those backwards, and it gets them backwards for the same reason: it has substituted a proxy for the thing itself and then forgotten it did.

One last discomfort, which is the real reason this sentence is said rather than written. It is a rationing rule. It allocates a scarce resource on the basis of a patient characteristic, at the bedside, by a clinician with no mandate to ration and no process behind them. We would not accept it stated that plainly in a policy. We say it in a corridor several times a week.

Do you agree?  Yes / No / Maybe

Yes

Absolute benefit really does scale with years remaining and with reserve, and the young have both. When a trial deliberately pushed more intensive care into the over-seventy-fives it saved nobody and the in-hospital mortality went the wrong way. A clinician who tries hardest for the patient most likely to survive is not being unfair. They are being accurate, and accuracy is the only thing we have to offer in a unit with fewer beds than patients.

No

It is a rationing rule that will not admit to being one, applied at the bedside by somebody with no mandate to ration, using a proxy instead of the variable. The risk–treatment paradox says the patients with most to gain in absolute terms are the ones we try least hard for — which makes the outcome data that appear to support this sentence partly a record of the sentence being obeyed. And “everything” is not a gift. In the young it buys the longest survivorship in the building.

Maybe

Right about the physiology, wrong about the noun. Say reserve, not young, and the sentence stops being a rationing rule and becomes an assessment — one you can be shown to be wrong about, which the age version does not allow. It also gets the two hard cases the right way round: the seventy-year-old who runs, and the thirty-five-year-old with end-stage disease.

Optional reflection

Have you seen this sentence operate in reverse?

The two cases that test it are the ones people remember. The older patient somebody nearly did not admit, who walked out. And the young one where everything was offered, everything was done, and everything turned out to be the wrong answer — for them, or for the family left holding what came after.

Tell me which you have seen, and tell me your verdict. I am particularly interested from anyone who thinks the objection is overdone and that treating the young harder is simply correct triage honestly described. That case deserves making, and I would rather print it than not.
