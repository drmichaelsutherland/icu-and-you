No. 5

The Silent Sounds on the Typewriter

A committee in a bunker working out whether to flatten a hundred German towns, and the people in them never came up

Recently, I visited the Churchill War Rooms in London. I was a reluctant attendee, as I feel I have been museumed out in recent times. As it happened, this museum was a revelation.

It is the site of the secret underground headquarters of the British war leadership in the Second World War. You can see everything there: the War Cabinet room, the top-secret transatlantic comms room (disguised as Churchill’s private toilet), the Map Room, the switchboard room, and all the private dining and sleeping areas. It is a trip back in time, and certainly an illustration of how much “cutting-edge” technology has changed over the past 80 years. I particularly enjoyed the special Remington “noiseless” typewriter, installed because of Churchill’s aversion to all the clicking and clacking of the standard typewriters of the day.

The feature that caught my attention most was an audio file reconstructing a Chiefs of Staff meeting from July 1944. You can listen to it while standing in front of the meeting room itself. In July 1944, the Germans were sending across to London the “flying bombs”, the forerunners of the cruise missile, and causing a lot of damage, deaths and injuries in the process. Churchill had sent the chiefs a memo proposing that a list of 100 German towns and villages be made and that the Germans be told that those targets would be hit and destroyed if the flying bomb raids did not stop.

The discussions were fascinating. The starting position seemed to be yes: the idea had a lot of merit. They could divert current resources (including some working to find and destroy the missile sites) towards the reprisal raids. Then someone said: but would it make any difference? The Germans had no other attacking options and would continue anyway, might take reprisals of their own by killing captured British POWs, or could even read the British raids as a sign that the flying bombs were working. Someone else said it wasn’t the attacks on the villages, but the threat of attacks, that was the key thing. But what are threats if they are not carried out? Incredibly, someone else proposed that the reprisal attacks on the German villages would be better if unannounced, and they should just do it. Eventually, the consensus was that the reprisal attacks on German towns had merit, but it was not the right time.

* * *

It was a classic example of what war is about, and also, to some extent, what human beings are about. What can we do, what will it mean, what can they do in return? The never-ending cycle of us and them. War as a continuation of politics by other means, the diplomacy of violence.

The committee was, in essence, discussing terror bombing, and the audio presentation at the museum is a deterrence seminar without a strong ethics section.

Churchill was told that the proposal had been considered and quietly shelved for the time being. In fact, both sides and all sides were already targeting civilian populations, and would continue to do so for the rest of the war. Brutally.

The ordinary people of the towns and villages under discussion were not mentioned at all. They weren’t participants; they were something else. They were the silent sounds on the typewriter.

A footnote…

What those men were doing in that room has a name, and the name arrived twenty-two years late. The diplomacy of violence is Thomas Schelling’s phrase, from Arms and Influence in 1966 — the book that separated deterrence, the threat that stops somebody acting, from compellence, the threat that makes them stop something they have already started. Schelling was given the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for that work in 2005. The Chiefs of Staff had no vocabulary for any of it on that July morning, and improvised the entire argument anyway, including the two objections that matter most: that a threat not carried out is worth nothing, and that carrying it out tells the enemy his weapon is working.

The flying bombs produced a second proposal that same month, and it went the same way. Churchill pressed the Chiefs of Staff on using poison gas — “We could drench the cities of the Ruhr and many other cities in Germany” — they opposed it, and he backed down. Same committee, same month, same outcome: considered, and quietly shelved.

Challenge for you

Reprisals: appropriate, or over the top?

Every objection raised in that room was a practical one. Would it work, what would it cost us, what would they do back. Nobody argued the other case at all.

So, eighty years on, with the sirens somebody else’s: was the proposal a reasonable answer to a weapon aimed at civilians, or was it over the top? And does it change your answer that the only reason it was shelved was timing?
