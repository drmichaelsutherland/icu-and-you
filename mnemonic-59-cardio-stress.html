ICU and You · Mnemonic Series · Number 59

Cardiomyopathy at forty

Six letters and then a second word: CARDIO — STRESS.

C
Chemo — the old kind and the new kindThe old kind is the anthracycline, and the forty-year-old in front of you was very likely treated at twenty-five: the damage is cumulative, dose-dependent and turns up years later. The new kind behaves nothing like it. Myocarditis from an immune checkpoint inhibitor occurs in about 1% of patients, at a median of 27 to 34 days after the first dose — so it is a first-cycle event, not a late one — and it kills 27 to 50% of the people who get it. Troponin is raised in 94% of cases, which makes it the one test worth doing on anyone on these drugs who feels unwell. It is treated with high-dose methylprednisolone, 500 to 1000 mg daily for three to five days, and up to half are steroid-refractory. Ask what they were given and when they started it, because the answer dates the illness.
A
Alcohol — and the threshold is made upAlcohol accounts for somewhere between 23 and 47% of what gets called idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy in European series, which makes it the largest single reversible cause on this page. The usual diagnostic threshold is more than 80 g a day for at least five years — and the authors who state it also call it arbitrary and not based on robust experimental or epidemiological data, which is worth knowing before you use it to rule someone out. The reason to pursue it is what happens next: after withdrawal, ejection fraction went from 30 ± 12% to 43 ± 16% at nine months. Whether a reduced intake is as good as none has never been settled, so the advice is none.
R
Rate — which way round is it?Atrial fibrillation in a dilated heart is usually written down as a consequence. Sometimes it is the cause, and that is a different illness with a different ending. In CAMERA-MRI, patients with persistent AF and an ejection fraction of 45% or less were randomised to ablation or medical rate control: ejection fraction rose by 18% absolute with ablation against 4.4%, and it normalised in 58% against 9%. So in roughly half of them the arrhythmia had been the disease all along. The same logic covers any incessant tachycardia — atrial flutter, an accessory pathway, frequent ectopy — and recovery of the ejection fraction does not always mean recovery of the ventricle, which can stay dilated for more than a year after the numbers look normal.
D
DrugsMethamphetamine first, and in Australia you will meet it. It rose from 1.8% of all new cardiomyopathy in one Californian region in 2009 to 5.6% by 2014, and an Australian autopsy series of methamphetamine-related deaths found cardiomyopathy in 5.5%. They present late and badly — mean ejection fraction 20 ± 11% in one series — and about a third recover by six weeks if they stop, which is the sentence to say out loud to the patient. Then clozapine, where the Australian monitoring is specific and worth carrying: weekly troponin and CRP for the first four weeks, because that is when almost all of it happens; troponin above twice the upper limit of normal, or CRP above 100 mg/L, means stop the drug and get an echo. And the quiet ones — anabolic steroids, which nobody volunteers, and chronic cocaine.
I
Infection — myocarditis, and the 3 to 9%Most acute myocarditis is not an intensive care problem: about 75% of unselected cases are uncomplicated and settle on their own. Fulminant myocarditis — cardiogenic shock needing haemodynamic support — is 3 to 9% of it, and that fraction is the whole of your experience of the disease, which is why it feels more lethal than it is. Within fulminant cases, sustained ventricular arrhythmia occurs in about 47% and about 26% present with sudden cardiac death. Where biopsy has been done, up to half recover spontaneously, up to a quarter are left with stable dysfunction, and 10 to 25% progress to end-stage disease, transplant or death. Add septic cardiomyopathy, which is the commonest acute ventricular failure on any general unit and almost always recovers, and — in anyone who has lived in Latin America — Chagas disease, which does not.
O
Other — obstetric, sarcoid, valves, inherited, ironObstetric. Peripartum cardiomyopathy is the one cause on this list that belongs to this age band and no other. Incidence runs from about 1 in 100 in Nigeria to 1 in 10,000 in Denmark, with Australia and the United States in between; most present in the first month after delivery and most recover inside six months, but mortality is 8.0% at six months and 9.8% at twelve, and recurrence in a later pregnancy is 30 to 50% — which is why the advice against another pregnancy stands while the ejection fraction remains below baseline. Sarcoid. Cardiac involvement is found in 20 to 30% of systemic sarcoidosis but is symptomatic in only about 5%, and atrioventricular block occurs in 35 to 50% of those with cardiac disease — so the rule is to suspect it in anyone under sixty with unexplained heart block. Valves. A bicuspid aortic valve is the reason a forty-year-old has severe aortic stenosis; it is a structural diagnosis with a surgical answer, and it is missed by not listening. Inherited. Up to 20 to 30% of dilated cardiomyopathy is monogenic — and screening the first-degree relatives finds disease in 9 to 22% at the first visit, while 11% of gene carriers with a normal first echo had developed cardiomyopathy within three years. Every first-degree relative gets an ECG and cardiac imaging. Iron. Cardiac T2* below 10 ms is severe myocardial loading, and in transfusion-dependent thalassaemia the one-year risk of heart failure was 47% below 6 ms against 0.2% above 10. It is a diagnosis made by MRI, not by ferritin, and chelation reverses it.
STRESS
— which is takotsubo, and is mostly not this patientThe word finishes the phrase, and the honest thing to say about it is that takotsubo syndrome is about 90% women, mean age sixty-seven to seventy, roughly 80% of them over fifty — so in a forty-year-old it is uncommon, and in-hospital mortality is nonetheless comparable to that of a STEMI, with recurrence at 1.8% per patient-year. Where it does turn up young is the version we make ourselves: the secondary takotsubo of a catecholamine storm — subarachnoid haemorrhage, status epilepticus, an undiagnosed phaeochromocytoma, and the patient who has been on a high-dose infusion for two days. That one is not grief. It is dose.
etc
… and the list does not closeThyrotoxicosis, which is reversible and is found by one blood test. HIV cardiomyopathy, now largely historical where treatment is available and not historical where it is not. Giant cell myocarditis, which is rarer than sarcoid, behaves worse, and sits beside it in the differential for unexplained block. Amyloid, which is an old person’s disease except for the hereditary variants, which are not. Obesity itself. Radiotherapy, if the mediastinum was in the field. And the honest remainder: a substantial share of these patients are discharged labelled idiopathic, and how large that share is depends almost entirely on whether anybody did the MRI, took the family history, or asked about the drinking.

Challenge for you

What did the family turn out to have?

Of everything above, the item with the longest reach is the family history — one young person’s cardiomyopathy, and then an ECG and an echocardiogram on a brother who felt perfectly well. If you have ever seen that screen come back abnormal, or seen it not be offered, I would like to hear it. The same goes for the reversible ones: the ejection fraction that came back after the drinking stopped, or after the ablation, or after the drug was withdrawn.
