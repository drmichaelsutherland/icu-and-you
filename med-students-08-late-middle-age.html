Late middle age

Forty-five to sixty — the band where the diagnosis is in what nobody wrote down

Find your level: Early years  ·  Clinical years  ·  Final years

The previous page in this series was about the first patients who arrive carrying a history somebody else has already written. This band is the opposite problem. The history exists, all of it, and almost none of it has been written down anywhere.

The tiers map roughly onto the three phases of a six-year medical programme — the science, then the clinical reasoning, then what you will actually be expected to do with your own hands. Read the one that fits, and glance at the one above it.

Forty-five to sixty is the decade and a half in which the illness that will eventually kill most of these patients turns up and introduces itself, usually at night and usually without having been named before. Coronary heart disease leads the causes of death in Australian men of this age and lung cancer follows it; for women it is breast cancer and lung cancer. None of that is a surprise. What surprises people is how often the admission is the first anyone has heard of it.

If this page has one argument, it is this: in this band the history is the diagnostic test, and most of it has never been written down. The scan will be reported as normal, the bloods will look unremarkable, and the thing that explains the admission will turn out to be a job somebody held in 1998, a quantity of alcohol nobody has ever counted, or a symptom the patient decided not to mention. Somebody has to go and get it, and on most wards the person with time to do that is you.

Tier one

Reserve that falls without moving a single number on the chart

Phase 1 — the foundational years, and anything on cardiovascular, respiratory and renal physiology or the biology of ageing

  • A normal observation chart is a record of compensationObservations are the outputs of control systems, not measurements of reserve. A fifty-four-year-old holds a normal rate, pressure and saturation by spending something to do it, and the chart records the result rather than the cost. Everything this band actually loses — diastolic compliance, renal reserve, muscle, chest wall compliance, maximal oxygen uptake — is invisible on the one document the ward looks at most often.
  • Creatinine is a muscle measurement wearing a kidney’s nameCreatinine is produced by skeletal muscle at a rate set by how much muscle there is. From about the fifth decade muscle mass falls, so production falls; if filtration falls at the same time, the concentration can sit still and tell you nothing has happened. A creatinine of 80 in a lean fifty-eight-year-old can represent a substantially lower glomerular filtration rate than the same number in a twenty-five-year-old, and every dose calculated from it is then wrong in the direction that accumulates.
  • Why a stiff ventricle floods on the second bagDiastolic dysfunction moves the ventricle onto the steep part of its pressure–volume relationship, where small changes in volume produce large changes in filling pressure. That is the mechanism behind the patient who looks fluid-responsive, receives a perfectly reasonable amount, and is in pulmonary oedema an hour later with an unremarkable electrocardiogram and a troponin that explains nothing.
  • Varices do not leak, they ruptureBleeding requires a hepatic venous pressure gradient above about 12 mmHg, and below that threshold varices can be present and silent for years. There is no warning haemorrhage, because the physics does not provide for one. It is the cleanest example of the rule that governs this whole band: the first presentation is the worst presentation, and that is a property of the disease rather than a failure of the patient.
  • An aneurysm has no symptomatic phase to catch it inWall tension rises with radius, so enlargement accelerates as the aneurysm enlarges, and the lesion is at its most dangerous when it is least detectable by symptoms. Nothing hurts until the wall fails. Screening programmes exist for this disease precisely because its natural history offers medicine no other opening.
  • Alcohol withdrawal is a timetable, not a surpriseChronic ethanol downregulates inhibitory GABA-A signalling and upregulates excitatory NMDA signalling. Remove the ethanol and the excitatory side is left unopposed, which takes time to declare itself: tremor and autonomic signs within a day, seizures and delirium characteristically at forty-eight to seventy-two hours. That is why the patient admitted on Monday with pneumonia becomes unmanageable on Wednesday — and why the history that would have predicted it was available on Monday.
  • Why sleep apnoea comes looking for you at extubationThe pharynx has no rigid support. It is held open by dilator muscle tone, and that tone is reduced or abolished by anaesthesia, sedation and opioids. Someone with untreated obstructive apnoea has been compensating every night for years through arousals that you will never see in a sedated patient. Obesity hypoventilation is a separate problem and the distinction is examinable: apnoea is an overnight, event-based disorder, hypoventilation is carbon dioxide retention while awake.
  • Muscle is an organ, and this is the band where it starts to goSkeletal muscle mass declines from roughly the fifth decade, and critical illness accelerates that loss enormously. It matters mechanically rather than cosmetically, because the diaphragm is skeletal muscle too: the patient who cannot be weaned is frequently not failing at the lung. Deconditioned is a description of this process which is routinely offered as though it were an explanation of it.

If you take one thing: everything this band has lost sits in a variable nobody charts, and everything it has kept sits on the chart. Reading an observation chart as a measure of reserve is the commonest error of judgement made about these patients, and it is made by experienced people.

Likely to be examined as: a short answer on the determinants of serum creatinine and why it is an imperfect marker of glomerular filtration, or on the physiology of diastolic dysfunction and why it produces pulmonary oedema at modest filling volumes. A fair viva opener is why does a creatinine of 80 worry you more at fifty-eight than at twenty-five?

Tier two

The notes are not the history, and here the gap between them is the diagnosis

Phase 2 — the clinical years, integrated clinical studies, and the ICE

  • No past history is a finding about the health systemIn every earlier band an empty past history is usually true. In this one it is usually an absence of looking. A man of fifty-four with nothing in the record has generally not been examined and found well; he has not attended. Treat the first set of investigations as a baseline being established rather than a result being compared, and write that down, so the next person does not read your normal as a reassuring change from something.
  • The chronic lung disease label is unreliable in both directionsIn the BOLD Australia study, a population sample of more than three thousand adults aged forty and over, roughly half the people who reported having chronic obstructive pulmonary disease had no airflow obstruction on spirometry — and roughly half of those with spirometry-confirmed disease had never been given the diagnosis. So the word in the notes tells you very little, and its absence tells you less. The test is cheap, and almost nobody in this band has had it.
  • Three diseases that routinely arrive under another nameHeart failure presents as a chest infection, because both produce breathlessness, crackles and an abnormal film, and the antibiotics get started first. Cirrhosis presents as confusion, because encephalopathy arrives before anybody notices the stigmata. Sepsis presents as alcohol withdrawal, which is the most dangerous of the three, because the treatment for the wrong diagnosis is sedation. What breaks the tie is almost never a single test. It is whether anybody has gone looking for the second story.
  • The suicide rate in Australian men peaks inside this bandIn 2023 the highest age-specific male suicide rate was in the fifty-five to fifty-nines, at 30.9 per 100,000, well above the overall male rate. It is not the band most people would guess. These deaths are more often planned than impulsive and frequently follow no contact with mental health services at all, which means the opportunity to ask is often a general hospital opportunity — and on a ward round of six it is often the student’s, because you are the one who sat down.
  • The quantity of alcohol is a clinical number, not a social detailAustralians aged forty-five to sixty-four account for about fifty-five per cent of the country’s alcohol-caused deaths. Social drinker is not a history. The course of the admission on day three is predicted by a number, and if nobody writes the number down, nobody downstream can reconstruct it from an adjective.
  • The occupational history is a diagnostic test with a long latencyAsk what they did in their twenties and thirties, by job title and by decade, because the exposures that matter were taken on then and are presenting now. Engineered stone, which can be more than ninety per cent crystalline silica, was banned for processing in Australia from 1 July 2024 and banned from import on 1 January 2025 — which means the exposure is now historical and the disease is still arriving. A fifty-two-year-old benchtop fabricator with breathlessness is a specific question, not a general one.
  • The cancer that gets diagnosed during this admissionA number of these patients will have their malignancy found on a scan ordered for something else, and you may be in the room when the report appears before anybody senior has read it. The reasoning task is not the diagnosis. It is understanding that the sequence — who is told, by whom, with whom present, and after what preparation — is itself clinical care, and that getting it wrong is one of the few harms in medicine you cannot go back and undo.
  • Nobody has written a ceiling of care, and the silence is being read as a decisionA fifty-five-year-old with advanced chronic disease usually has nothing recorded: no ceiling, no substitute decision-maker, no conversation. The error is to treat that silence as a considered judgement that everything is appropriate. It is not a judgement, it is a gap, and in this band it is the expected finding, because fifty-five felt too early to everybody who might otherwise have raised it.

If you take one thing: in this band the distance between what is written and what is true is where the diagnosis lives. Closing that distance is a task somebody has to carry out, not an insight somebody has to have.

Likely to be examined as: a long case where the marks sit in the history rather than the examination, or a data interpretation with normal-looking renal function and an abnormal patient. A fair viva opener is what would you ask a fifty-four-year-old with no past history, and why those questions?

Tier three

The jobs that are actually yours

Phase 3 — the final clinical years, the pre-internship term, and your first nights on call

  • Ring the general practice, and ring before five o’clockThis is the highest-yield job on the page, and it falls to you because nobody more senior has the time. Ask for the problem list, the current medications, the last set of bloods, and whether anybody has ever had a conversation about where the illness is going. Record where each item came from. After hours you still have My Health Record, the community pharmacy dispensing history and whoever came in with the patient, and all three beat the ward list.
  • Take the occupational history by decade, and write the job titles downRetired is not an occupation and labourer is not a job. Stonemason, boilermaker, shipwright, farmer, miner, carpenter, hairdresser, spray painter. It takes thirty seconds and it is a question nobody else on the round will have asked.
  • Ask the partner about the breathing at nightPatients cannot report their own apnoea; it is unobservable from the inside. Somebody who sleeps in the same room can tell you about snoring, witnessed pauses and daytime sleepiness in about a minute, and the answer changes what you should expect at extubation and what you do about the overnight oxygen.
  • Alcohol in standard drinks, with a number, a date and a predictionHow many, how often, and when the last one was. Then write in the notes what you expect to happen and when, because the person who meets the consequence on Wednesday night will not be you and will have no way of knowing that the clock started on Monday morning.
  • Ask about suicide, in plain words, and do not make it conditionalYou will occasionally be told it is not your place, or not what the patient was admitted for. It is a direct question with no evidence of harm from the asking, in the band where the rate is highest, and in many of these lives the contact with health services is this one.
  • Find out what they have already been told, not what they have decidedWhat have the doctors told you about where this illness is going? is a student’s question and a very good one. It is not asking anybody to make a decision, it needs no seniority, and the answer tells the team whether the conversation it is about to have is the first one or the fourth.
  • Find out who would speak for them, and who depends on themBoth directions. Who the substitute decision-maker would be, and who is at home relying on this person — children, a parent, a partner with an illness of their own. In this band the patient is frequently the carer rather than the cared-for, and that single fact changes discharge planning, consent, and the likelihood that they will take their own cannula out on day four.
  • Weigh them, and look at the muscleA real weight, not an estimate, because half the doses on the chart depend on it. And a look at the deltoids and the quadriceps, because it tells you something about reserve that no blood test on the page will. Both are jobs a student can do unaided, and both are routinely left undone.
  • Write down what you could not find outThe practice was closed, the partner flies in tomorrow, the patient would not say. A recorded failed attempt is information: it stops the next three people repeating your phone call, and it marks the gap clearly enough for somebody with more authority to go and close it.

If you take one thing: the two jobs on this list with no glamour attached whatever — the phone call to the general practice, and the collateral history from whoever came in with them — are the two most likely to change the diagnosis.

Likely to be examined as: a history-taking or collateral-history station, or a communication station in which the patient asks you a question you are not the right person to answer. The marks there are for recognising that, saying so plainly without walking away, and knowing who to go and get.

Over to you

Which tier did you read — and what did you end up having to find out for yourself?

Tell me your year, which tier fitted, and what was missing, too basic or too advanced. That part is the same every time, and it is how these get better.

This one has a second question. Tier three is a list of jobs that get handed to students because they take time, and are then almost never taught, watched or marked. Getting past the front desk of a general practice. Asking a stranger of fifty-four about suicide. Asking somebody about their partner’s breathing at night. Which of those has anyone ever actually shown you how to do? I suspect that for most people the honest answer is none of them, and if that is right it is worth saying out loud.
