No. 3

“A Youth Policy”

An administrator explained, with a smile and not a hint of embarrassment, that they were having a youth policy

Some years ago, a friend of mine, a long-time colleague, applied for an additional senior role in his healthcare organisation. He was in his sixties, with decades of experience. Although very well qualified, he missed out on the appointment. It was clear to nearly everyone who looked at it that he was the best-qualified candidate.

He debriefed with me. I asked how the interview went, and he said it was fine, pleasant, and cordial, with no significant issues raised. The panel of three thanked him and said they would let him know within a week. When he hadn’t heard anything for a couple of months, he made a polite enquiry. Shortly after, he received a reply that he had been unsuccessful. An administrator told him, with a smile and not a hint of embarrassment, that they were having “a youth policy”.

He was rather philosophical about it, saying he wasn’t too worried and hoped they had found someone who could do the role well. I told him that I was appalled. Their process was lamentable, or worse, and I felt upset for him and saddened by the sickness in the system.

* * *

We readily understand a “youth policy” and some may even laud it. But how would we feel about “we are having a whites-only policy” or “we have decided it is men-only for this position”? Only people with this religious belief, only people from this social or economic background. Only people with this type of body shape or facial appearance. Or you could go the whole nine yards with “only people who will not disagree with us”.

Discrimination doesn’t discriminate. You can’t pick and choose which form of discrimination is OK to do right now. You either place merit above all, or you will end up placing a mishmash of bigotry above merit.

Ageism has a particular place in this regrettable pantheon. Those who don’t die young will reach a certain age, and at that point they will begin to notice the subtle shunning and the unsubtle shunting off that happens.

Don’t discriminate against the aged. They often have much more to offer than you think. Value that. Value it because it is true, and value it also because one day it may be you.

A footnote, and not part of the story

Discriminating on the ground of age in employment is unlawful in Australia under the Age Discrimination Act 2004 (No. 68 of 2004), and that reaches recruitment and who is offered the job, not merely what happens to people once they are in it. What is curious is how late and how lightly it arrived. It commenced on 23 June 2004, the last of the four federal anti-discrimination Acts — twenty-nine years after race, twenty after sex, twelve after disability.

And as first written it was weaker than all three: a complainant had to show that age was the dominant reason for the treatment rather than simply a reason, a hurdle the other Acts never set. That was removed by amendment in 2009. A dedicated Age Discrimination Commissioner did not exist until 2011, seven years after the Act, when Susan Ryan was appointed the first of them. The Act’s own exception is narrow, and is for the person who cannot meet the inherent requirements of the job.

Australian Human Rights Commission, The Road So Far – the Age Discrimination Act 2004 (Cth), 2011.
