Nutrition in intensive care

Feed early, feed enough, feed protein. Two of those three are now in question — and the argument is live enough that this talk contains both sides of it

Golden rule
A few pieces of evidence are non-negotiable. Most are guidance to support your decision-making — and that decision-making improves with every patient.
One lesson
Critical care is fundamentally evidence-based, held together by clinical judgement and common sense. Which is why its core principles hold across the world, and travel with you.

This is a reference piece rather than an argument. It is the shape of a nutrition round — who needs screening, how much to give, by which route, what goes wrong — set out so you can find a number at three in the morning rather than read it end to end.

With one exception. Somewhere in the middle of assembling this talk, the ground moved, and the talk caught it moving. That belongs at the front.

The argument that is going on right now

For twenty years the teaching was: feed early, work up to the target, and give plenty of protein because the patient is catabolic and protein is what they are losing. The 2016 and 2022 American guidelines say versions of that. Most of this page says versions of that.

Then a run of trials did not show what everybody expected, and a 2026 review in Intensive Care Medicine — which appears in this deck, on slide two — states the position now emerging. It does not agree with the rest of the deck. It does not agree with most of what any of us were taught.

The same two numbers, from two places, four years apart
ASPEN 2022 established Peake and colleagues, Intensive Care Medicine 2026 emerging
Energy, acute phase 12–25 kcal/kg/day through the first 7 to 10 days Avoid overfeeding above 20 kcal/kg/day. In severe illness needing organ support, consider 6–8 kcal/kg/day
Protein, acute phase 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day, to mitigate catabolism and muscle loss Avoid above 1.2 g/kg/day. In severe illness needing organ support, consider 0.2–0.4 g/kg/day. High protein delivery may be deleterious, particularly with organ failure or acute kidney injury
Recovery phase Not separately specified 20–30 kcal/kg/day and 1.0–1.3 g/kg/day, with underfeeding in this phase thought to impair long-term recovery
Read the rest of this page knowing that

Several sections below quote the established targets — 25 to 30 kcal/kg, 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg of protein, 1.5 to 2.0 in burns, 2.0 to 2.5 in obesity. Those are what the guidelines currently say and they are reproduced faithfully. They are also the numbers the 2026 review is arguing against for the acute phase.

This is not a flaw in the talk. It is an accurate photograph of a field mid-argument, and the honest thing is to show you both rather than quietly average them. What is not in dispute: that the metabolic phase matters, that overfeeding is harmful, and that the patient on day ten is not the patient on day one.

Why the phase matters

Stress, surgery and trauma change the metabolic milieu wholesale: substrate utilisation shifts, synthesis rates alter, metabolic rate rises, and lean body mass is consumed — what the talk calls, memorably, auto-cannibalism.

Two phases, two different problems
 Acute critical illnessRecovery
BalanceCatabolism exceeds anabolismAnabolism exceeds catabolism
FuelCarbohydrate is the preferred energy source, because fat mobilisation is impairedNormal substrate handling returns
What protein is forThe hope of blunting the breakdown of muscle protein into amino acids, which then feed gluconeogenesisRestoring lean body mass and function
The riskOverfeeding a patient who is already generating endogenous energyUnderfeeding a patient who can finally use what you give them

What drives malnutrition and negative nitrogen balance in these patients is rarely one thing: poor intake, prolonged bed rest, altered substrate use, the stress response itself, hypermetabolism, exogenous steroids and major surgery all contribute.

Screening: who is actually at risk

Assess every patient on admission. Two instruments are in common use, and the talk presents both.

Interpretation depends on whether you have an interleukin-6 result, which in most Australian units you will not. With IL-6: 6 to 10 is a high score, 0 to 5 low. Without IL-6: 5 to 9 is high, 0 to 4 low. A high score marks the patients with worse outcomes — mortality and ventilation — and those most likely to benefit from aggressive nutrition therapy.

Working out the requirement

Three ways, in descending order of accuracy. Indirect calorimetry measures it. Predictive equations estimate it. 25 to 30 kcal/kg is the bedside shorthand, and it is the figure most units actually run on.

The equations, for when you need them

Harris–Benedict, basal energy expenditure:
Men — 66.5 + (13.75 × weight in kg) + (5.003 × height in cm) − (6.775 × age in years)
Women — 655.1 + (9.563 × weight in kg) + (1.850 × height in cm) − (4.676 × age in years)

Resting energy expenditure: REE = BEE × 1.2, or BEE × a stress factor.

Weir, from indirect calorimetry:
REE in kcal/day = [(3.941 × VO₂) + (1.106 × VCO₂)] × 1440, with VO₂ and VCO₂ in litres per minute.

That is the abbreviated form, and it is the one everybody uses. The full equation then subtracts 2.17 × urinary nitrogen in g/day from the daily total — note from the total, not from inside the bracket. At ordinary nitrogen excretion that correction is worth around 60 kcal a day, which is why it is almost always left out.

Nitrogen balance: (protein intake in g ÷ 6.25) − (urinary nitrogen + 4). Each gram of urinary nitrogen represents 6.25 g of degraded protein, and the goal is a positive balance of 4 to 6 g. A negative balance means insufficient non-protein calories — the body is burning the protein you gave it.

Enteral nutrition, which is the default

If the gut works, use it. Start within 24 to 48 hours in any critically ill patient who cannot maintain their own intake. Early enteral feeding preserves gut immune function, reduces inflammation, and may lower the incidence of infection.

Two things people get wrong

Bowel sounds are not required. Neither is any other evidence of bowel function. Waiting for them delays feeding for no benefit.

But hold feed until the patient is fully resuscitated. Feeding a gut that is not perfused is the one way early enteral nutrition does harm.

Feed into the stomach unless there is a reason not to. Consider a post-pyloric tube when the aspiration risk is high, or when gastric feeding has already failed in this patient.

Tolerance, and the measurement to stop taking

Gastric residual volumes

Do not use gastric residual volumes to assess tolerance or as a marker of aspiration risk. The quality of evidence behind that recommendation is low, but the direction is consistent, and both the 2016 and 2022 guidelines say it.

If your unit still measures them, the practical rule is that feed should not be held for a residual under 500 mL in the absence of any other sign of intolerance.

What to do instead: review daily, and protect the feed from the thing that actually stops it, which is not intolerance but fasting for tests and procedures. Volume-based protocols, which allow the nurse to catch up after an interruption, deliver more of the prescribed feed than rate-based ones.

To reduce aspiration risk — post-pyloric access, continuous rather than bolus feeding, a prokinetic, the head of the bed at 30 to 45 degrees, and chlorhexidine mouthwash. Two things not to do: no colouring agent added to feed as an aspiration marker, and do not stop the feed for diarrhoea.

When the enteral route is closed

The enteral route is always preferred except where it is actually contraindicated, and that list is short: intestinal obstruction or ileus, intestinal ischaemia, a high-output fistula, or severe intestinal haemorrhage.

Parenteral nutrition

Two situations. In a patient at high nutrition risk or already malnourished in whom enteral feeding is impossible, start parenteral nutrition as soon as possible after admission. In everyone else, if enteral feeding has failed to deliver 60% of energy and protein requirements after 7 to 10 days, add supplemental parenteral nutrition.

Not before day seven

In a non-malnourished patient already receiving enteral nutrition, do not start supplemental parenteral nutrition before day 7. This is one of the firmer recommendations in the 2022 guideline and one of the easiest to breach with good intentions.

In the first week, use hypocaloric dosing with adequate protein — under 20 kcal/kg/day, or 80% of estimated energy needs, with protein above 1.2 g/kg/day. Withhold or limit soybean-oil lipid emulsions during that first week. There is no clinical advantage to compounded over standardised formulations. Taper once the patient tolerates 60% of their enteral target.

Blood glucose target on parenteral nutrition: 7.8–10.0 mmol/L (140–180 mg/dL).

For total parenteral nutrition, the infusion needs a large-bore, high-flow vessel to limit vessel irritation. Subclavian puncture and peripherally inserted central catheters are the commonest routes; the internal jugular is less desirable because of its higher infection rate. Confirm the position radiographically. Give it as a continuous infusion, keep the total infused volume constant while the nutritional content is increased, and do not run additional maintenance fluid alongside it.

Stopping it

Discontinue when the patient meets 60 to 75% of their caloric and protein needs by mouth or enterally. Halve the infusion rate for an hour, halve it again for the next hour, then stop — which prevents rebound hypoglycaemia from residual hyperinsulinaemia. If the patient has demonstrated glycaemic stability, tapering is not necessary.

ConditionWhat changes

TraumaEarly enteral feeding, high-protein polymeric formula. In severe trauma, consider an immune-modulating formula with arginine and fish oils
Traumatic brain injuryStart within 24 to 48 hours. Either an immune-modulating formula, or supplement with EPA and DHA

BurnsEnteral feeding unless not feasible. Indirect calorimetry recommended. Protein 1.5–2.0 g/kg/day. “Early” here means within 4 to 6 hours of injury, which is a far tighter window than anywhere else on this page

What to carry away

  1. Screen everyone on admission. The score matters less than the fact of having looked.
  2. If the gut works, use it, and start within 24 to 48 hours. Do not wait for bowel sounds. Do wait for resuscitation.
  3. Stop measuring gastric residual volumes, and stop stopping feed for diarrhoea.
  4. Protect the feed from fasting more than from intolerance — that is where the calories actually go.
  5. Do not add parenteral nutrition before day seven in a well-nourished patient who is already being fed.
  6. And hold the energy and protein targets more loosely than you were taught to. The number that was right in 2022 may not be the number that is right now, and the honest position is that we are in the middle of finding out.
