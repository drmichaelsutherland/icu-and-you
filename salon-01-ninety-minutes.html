Ninety minutes

On being told that once you have given the lytic, you are committed

About this series

The Senior Intensivists' Salon is a room for the arguments we actually have — the ones about practice that is widely done, thinly supported, and difficult to stop doing. No teaching, no answers at the back. A position is put, as honestly as I can put it, and then the floor is open.

I will be wrong in some of these. That is rather the point of a salon.

A registrar said it to me recently, in the corridor, with the easy confidence of somebody who has read the thing properly: if you give a thrombolytic for pulmonary embolism during an arrest, you have to keep going for an hour and a half.

He was not making it up. It is in the guidelines, it is in the courses, and he could have quoted me the number. I asked where it came from. He said the guidelines.

Which is true, and is not the same as an answer.

So let me put the uncomfortable version of the question. If that instruction is followed as written, we are going to perform a great many ninety-minute resuscitations. Not on a selected handful of young, witnessed, obvious cases. On everybody in whom somebody raised the possibility of pulmonary embolism and reached for the drug — which, once the drug is given, is everybody in whom the thought was entertained.

How many of those will be futile? And how many will be worse than futile, in the sense that a death which was going to happen anyway is extended by an hour and a quarter, in front of a family, with a team working hard at something nobody in the room believes in?

What the guideline actually says

It is worth putting the three texts side by side, because the argument is in the wording and nowhere else.

  • European Resuscitation Council, 2021 Consider CPR for 60-90 min after administration of thrombolytic drugs. The sentence as it stood for four years.
  • European Resuscitation Council and Resuscitation Council UK, 2025 In select patients with suspected pulmonary embolism, consider CPR for 60-90 min after administration of thrombolytic drugs. Seven words added at the front. Nothing else changed.
  • ANZCOR Guideline 11.10, approved July 2026 Consider performing CPR for 60 to 90 min before termination of resuscitation attempts. A Good Practice Statement, with no qualifier. This is the one that applies here.

Three things follow from that, and I think only the third is interesting.

The first is that the European default has moved. Under the 2021 wording, giving the lytic committed you: stopping early was the decision that needed justifying. Under the 2025 wording, continuing is the decision that needs justifying. The weight has shifted from the drug to the patient, which is where it belongs.

The second is that ours has not moved. ANZCOR's current guideline was approved in July, and it carries the figure without the qualifier. A colleague here who stops at thirty minutes is working against the letter of the document that governs them, while a colleague in Britain doing exactly the same thing is working within it. Same patient, same evidence, same drug.

The third is the one I keep returning to. The 2025 wording tells us that this is for selected patients, and then does not say who they are. It hands the discretion back without handing back any means of exercising it. We are invited to select, and left to invent the criteria ourselves, and whatever we invent will be unvalidated.

We have done this before

Those of us who have been at this a while will recognise the shape of it, because we spent a decade cooling people.

Two small trials in 2002, both positive: the European HACA study, and Stephen Bernard's seventy-seven patients. Within a few years, thirty-two to thirty-four degrees was close to mandatory, units had bought the machines, and protocols had been written around them. Then TTM in 2013 found that thirty-three was no better than thirty-six. Then TTM2 in 2021 found that hypothermia was no better than simply not letting people get hot — fifty per cent dead at six months against forty-eight. And the language drifted, in small steps that each looked reasonable: cool them, then control the temperature, then prevent fever.

Nobody ever announced that we had been wrong. The practice simply thinned out.

The ninety minutes has the same architecture. A plausible mechanism — the drug needs time to work. Thin evidence hardened into a default. Equipment that made the default feasible, and which we bought: cooling machines then, mechanical compression devices now. And then a softening of the wording, once enough people had become uneasy.

The honest counter-example

It does not always go that way, and I should say so. We proned enormous numbers of patients on physiological grounds long before there was evidence, and the early trials were negative. Then PROSEVA restricted it to a P/F ratio under 150, started early, in sessions of sixteen hours or more, and twenty-eight day mortality went from 32.8% to 16%. Selection did not kill proning. It rescued it, and turned it into one of the few interventions in ARDS that demonstrably saves lives.

So “in selected patients” can be the first step of a retreat, or it can be the refinement that finds the people who actually benefit. The difference is whether anybody does the work of defining the selection. Proning had PROSEVA. The ninety minutes has four words and an empty space.

What is underneath it

The best observational dataset remains Er and colleagues from Cologne: 104 consecutive witnessed in-hospital arrests with confirmed or strongly suspected pulmonary embolism, all given rtPA during CPR. Thirty-eight and a half per cent achieved return of circulation; 18.3% left hospital.

Now look at where the signal actually sits. The patients who achieved return of circulation had been given the drug at 13.6 minutes. Those who did not were given it at 24.6. Those who survived to discharge had it at 11.0 minutes, against 22.5 for everybody else.

That is a study about when you give the drug. It is not a study about how long you continue afterwards, and it has been read as though it were.

Then there is the case. The ERC's own reference base for this recommendation includes a report of good neurological recovery after a hundred minutes of CPR, and it is the sort of case that settles an argument in a tearoom. I went and read it.

The hundred-minute case, in full

A seventy-year-old man, arrest on a neurology ward, pulseless electrical activity. Ventricular fibrillation once, defibrillation unsuccessful. Then narrow-complex PEA for the next hour — with intermittent return of spontaneous circulation throughout. Alteplase was given near the end: five milligrams as a bolus, then forty-five by infusion.

About two minutes later the rhythm converted to sinus and circulation was established. Extensive bilateral emboli were confirmed afterwards. He survived with no major neurological sequelae, after three months of renal replacement therapy and an occipital infarct along the way.

Read that case again with the recommendation in mind. It is used to justify waiting an hour for the drug to work. It is a case in which the drug worked in about two minutes, in a patient who had a circulation for much of the preceding hour.

It is a wonderful case. It is an argument for giving the lytic earlier. It is not an argument for the tail.

And the comparison nobody makes: we do not say this about coronary occlusion. TROICA randomised 827 patients to tenecteplase or placebo during resuscitation and was stopped for futility — thirty-day survival 18.2% against 20.2%. The pharmacokinetics of alteplase during low flow do not know which artery they are in. If the drug needs an hour in the pulmonary circulation, it needs an hour in the coronary one. We decided it did not, and moved on.

And they are not all the same arrest

This is the part that gets lost in the word suspected.

At one end: a witnessed collapse in a post-operative patient with a known deep venous thrombosis, a short no-flow time, compressions within seconds, and a story that was pointing at pulmonary embolism before the arrest happened. At the other: a found-down asystolic arrest of unknown duration, in which somebody put a probe on the chest and saw a big right ventricle.

Both are recorded as a suspected pulmonary embolism arrest. Both attract the same instruction. They are not the same event, and no single number can sensibly govern both.

And the big right ventricle deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is doing more work at the bedside than it can bear. Aagaard and colleagues arrested pigs three ways — pulmonary embolism, hypoxia, primary arrhythmia — and measured the right ventricle during resuscitation. It was dilated in all three. Thirty-two millimetres in the embolism group, against twenty-three and twenty-five. Clinicians with focused echo training separated them with 79% sensitivity and 68% specificity.

A third of the non-embolic arrests looked like embolism. We have all seen the dilated right ventricle on the arrest echo and felt the diagnosis firm up under us. It mostly should not.

The anecdote problem

Which brings me to the form of argument that actually decides these cases at three in the morning, and it is not a guideline.

It goes: we had one like this, we kept going, and he walked out of here. Therefore.

That story has exactly one legitimate use, and it is a good one. It defeats a never. If somebody survived ninety minutes, then nobody-survives-after-thirty is false, and anyone asserting it is corrected. That is a real contribution and I would not want to lose it.

What the story cannot do is set an expectation, for the oldest reason there is: the denominator is missing. Nobody writes up the hundred ninety-minute resuscitations that ended the way everyone in the room expected. The published cases are the survivors, which is what makes the odds feel plausible when you are standing there.

And the story moves the burden of proof. Whoever has the anecdote makes the person who wants to stop into the person who has to justify stopping. That is a rhetorical move, not a clinical one, and it wins almost every time.

The part I like least

In the moment, we are frequently not weighing evidence at all. We are managing the possibility of being wrong in public. We had better keep going is sometimes a clinical judgement and often a defensive one — because the resuscitation that ran ninety minutes will never be criticised, and the one that stopped at twenty-five might be.

I have made that calculation myself and I expect I will again. It is worth naming it rather than dressing it up, because a defensible decision and a defensive one are not the same thing, and only one of them is about the patient.

How these things persist

Once an idea like this is established, we all follow suit. And honestly — how can you not?

The registrar has read it. The nurse has read it. The course taught it, the protocol repeats it, and the person who stops at thirty minutes does so in front of a room full of people who know what the guideline says. The social cost of being the one who stopped early is immediate and personal. The cost of ninety minutes of futile resuscitation is diffuse, shared, and falls mostly on somebody who is already dead.

So we follow suit. Until we don't.

Nobody will publish the trial that overturns this one. Nobody is going to randomise embolism arrests to thirty minutes against ninety. It will go the way cooling went: a qualifier here, a quieter protocol there, extracorporeal CPR taking the argument over in the centres that have it, and in ten years a registrar will say we used to do ninety minutes and somebody will have to explain why.

I would rather we had the argument now, deliberately, than arrive at the same place by attrition and call it progress.

The floor is yours

Thirty minutes and a judgement call — or the full ninety, as a matter of course?

That is the question, and I would genuinely like to know where the room sits. Four more, if you have the time for them:

  1. What would make you stop at thirty? Name the actual criteria you would use, not the ones you would write down afterwards. I suspect we are all using something, and that none of us has said it out loud.
  2. Have you ever continued a resuscitation because of a story — somebody's case from five years ago? I have. I am interested in how common that is.
  3. Does your unit have a local position on this, and does it follow ANZCOR's wording or the European one? If the two are being applied side by side in Australian units, that is worth knowing.
  4. If you have extracorporeal CPR available, does any of this argument survive for you, or does the ninety minutes simply become transport time?

Replies may be published, in whole or in part, in a follow-up page — including the ones that tell me I have this wrong, which are the ones I most want. How you are credited is entirely your call.
