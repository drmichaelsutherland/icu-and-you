The aphorism for this topic
“Assume every woman of childbearing age is pregnant until proven otherwise.”
How to use an aphorism

An aphorism is a compressed argument, not a rule. It earns its shape by leaving things out, which is exactly what makes it memorable and what makes it arguable. Every one in this series is contestable, and chosen because it is.

Each is a mantra, a paradigm, a truism or occasionally a plain myth — something repeated in our units often enough that nobody examines it any more. The piece sets out the case for it, the case against it, and the case for something in between, and then asks which you hold.

There is no right answer waiting at the bottom. The point is to find out what you actually believe and why, because the beliefs that run your practice at three in the morning are mostly ones you have never said out loud.

Preamble

The rule exists because of one disease. A young woman arrives with abdominal pain, or faints at work, and the thing that will kill her before the shift ends is a ruptured ectopic pregnancy. South Australia’s perinatal guideline still calls it the commonest cause of maternal death in the first trimester; in the United Kingdom’s 2024 confidential enquiry every one of the twelve early-pregnancy deaths was an ectopic, and assessors judged better care might have changed the outcome for nine of them. About a third of women who have one carry no risk factor you could have asked about. Australia loses roughly one woman every three years this way, so our case rests on delay and harm rather than mortality — but it is still a good case.

The trouble is the reasoning smuggled in alongside it. Assume she is pregnant means set aside what she tells you about her own body. Two emergency department studies went and looked. In the larger, of the two-thirds of women who said pregnancy was impossible, not one was pregnant; in the other, one of 337. She is almost always right, and so are the clinicians who believe her.

Which is not an argument for testing less. The confidence interval around “not one” still runs to about 1.5 per cent, and routine testing before procedures turns up an unrecognised pregnancy in 0.2 to 2 per cent of women — occasionally an unrecognised ectopic. The rule survives. It survives on asymmetry of consequence rather than on women being unreliable, and that distinction is the whole of this piece.

Nor does assume do any work. An assumption has never diagnosed anything; only the test has power, and the gap between the two is where people come to harm. Katie Cranfield’s question on Podcast #60 is the operational one: on admission, for anyone of childbearing capacity, has a pregnancy test been done?

Meanwhile the same sentence is quoted to withhold things — hold the CT, hold the contrast, wait for the test before the chest film. A chest radiograph delivers under 0.01 mGy to the fetus and a CT head under 0.005; a CT pulmonary angiogram between 0.01 and 0.66. No anomaly, growth restriction or miscarriage has been reported below 50 mGy. So a rule written to protect an unborn child is routinely used to delay the scan that would have saved its mother.

What the rule says nothing about is that a pregnancy test is performed on a person, and its result is information about her body that she may not have, may not want, and may very much not want said aloud in the room she is currently in. The American anaesthetists are explicit: testing is offered rather than mandatory, coercing a patient into one violates her autonomy, and she should be asked who else may learn the result. Britain’s NICE guidance is the best-worded anywhere — sensitively ask, test with her consent, and document the discussion either way. Ours is quieter. The NSW directive requires the proceduralist to confirm pregnancy status and says nothing about how, nothing about what age, and nothing about consent.

And no guidance I could find addresses what actually happens: an unexpected positive, with a partner, a parent or a family member interpreting, standing at the bedside. Where the question is touched at all it is a hospital policy noting the risk of physical danger if the wrong person is told.

One last thing. It says she, and the patient most likely to be missed is the one it does not name. Testosterone is not contraception, a proportion of people taking it still ovulate, and the UK radiographers report the predictable incident — a trans man found to be pregnant after a CT nobody had asked about. Which is of a piece with the rest: almost no college defines “childbearing age” either.

Do you agree?  Yes / No / Maybe

Yes

The arithmetic is not close. A few dollars and five minutes against a ruptured ectopic in a twenty-four-year-old, and a third of them with no risk factor to find. Every other objection here is about how you apply the rule, not whether. Keep the rule, fix the manners.

No

It instructs you to override a competent adult’s account of her own body, and the evidence says she is right essentially every time. It has been used to justify testing people without asking them, and it is quoted at least as often to withhold a scan as to send a specimen. A rule whose stated reasoning is wrong and whose commonest use is harmful has stopped earning its place.

Maybe

The assumption is right and the verb is wrong. Ask, do not assume — then test with her consent, tell her why, and settle who else hears the answer before you have it. That is a longer sentence than the aphorism and it is the whole of the difference between a safe habit and something done to a patient.

Optional reflection

In your hospital, how is pregnancy status actually established — and who hears the answer?

Not the policy. What happens: whether anybody asks before the specimen is sent, whether the question is put with somebody else in the room, and what would occur tomorrow if a test came back positive on a nineteen-year-old with her mother sitting beside her.

Tell me that and tell me your verdict. If you think the objection is overdone and the rule is simply good medicine, that case deserves making and I would rather print it than not.
