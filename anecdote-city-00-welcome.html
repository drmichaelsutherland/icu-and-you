Welcome to Anecdote City

A hundred-odd stories, going up a few at a time — and room for yours

Every unit has a tearoom, and in every tearoom somebody is telling a story.

You will have heard one this week. It begins with that reminds me of the time, and the registrar who already had her bag on her shoulder sits back down, and it goes wherever it goes. Eight minutes later everybody in the room knows something they did not know before. Not one of them could tell you what.

I have been doing that in print for a hundred-odd newsletters. It was never a plan.

* * *

There are respectable arguments for why it works. Narrative medicine has a literature, a journal, and a chair at Columbia. Stories are said to build empathy, to teach us to sit with uncertainty, to carry the tacit knowledge that no guideline has ever managed to hold, to rehearse us quietly for the night it happens for real. Most of that is probably true. I have quoted a fair bit of it myself, usually to committees.

But that is not why I keep them.

I keep them because the guideline I read in 2004 is gone, and the man in bed nine is not. He is still there, sitting up, asking me a question I did not have an answer to. Everything I actually know how to do arrived attached to somebody.

* * *

So: Anecdote City. More than a hundred of mine, written across a dozen years and a good deal more practice than that, going up here a few at a time as I work through the backlog. Some are five paragraphs. Some are one. They were written for people in medicine, but most of them do not need you to be one, and I will say so where it matters.

There is no running order. A city is not a corridor. You arrive where you arrive.

And there is room for yours — which is the part I am actually interested in. I have forty years of these and that is precisely the problem: they are all from one angle, one country, mostly one specialty, and told by somebody who was in the room and therefore cannot see all of it. The nurse who was also in that room saw a different thing. So did the wardsman. So did the daughter.

The one rule

Nobody in these stories is identifiable, and that is not negotiable. Change the age, the decade, the hospital, the sex and the diagnosis if the diagnosis is not the point. If a detail is so unusual that it identifies the person, it goes — and if the story only works because of that detail, the story stays in the tearoom.

Write it as though the family will read it, because one day somebody’s family will. I check every one before it goes up, and I will ask you about anything that gives me pause.

What makes a good one

Not the save. The ones worth reading are almost never the ones where it all went well — they are the ones where somebody said something unexpected, or where the thing you were certain of turned out to be the thing you were wrong about, or where the technical problem was solved and the actual problem was somewhere else entirely.

Short is good. One page is plenty. If you find yourself explaining the physiology, you are writing a different piece and I would like that one too, but somewhere else on the site.

It need not be dramatic. Some of the best things I have are about a corridor, or a cup of tea, or somebody’s shoes.

* * *

The tearoom holds eleven people and the story stops when the door swings shut.

This place does not have a door.

Send me one

You have one. You have told it at least twice.

One page. Write it the way you say it — I would rather have it rough and in your own voice than smooth and in nobody’s. If writing it down is the barrier, tell it to your phone and send me the recording; I have done that myself more than once.

You decide how you are credited, and you see it before anybody else does.

Send me your anecdote

How you are credited is entirely your call — by name, by initials, or not at all — whether you write the piece or I do, and whether I quote you or simply say who sent something in; nothing of yours goes up with your name on it unless you have said yes. And the story stays yours: sending it gives me permission to publish it here, with the credit you choose. If it were ever to appear anywhere else — a collection, or print — I would come back and ask you separately first.
