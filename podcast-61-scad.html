ICU and You  ·  Podcast Sessions  ·  Number 61

The opposite of hard arteries

Jason Kovacic on the heart attack that runs the other way — and the thirty-year-old woman nobody troponins

Show  Healthed Guest  Professor Jason Kovacic, cardiologist, St Vincent’s Sydney; Executive Director and CEO, Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute Host  Dr David Lim, GP and medical educator Released  7 November 2025 Length  43 minutes
Listen to the episode

Free, Australian, and three quarters of an hour. Lim asks the questions a GP would actually ask, which keeps it concrete — and Kovacic has spent a career on this particular disease, so the answers come with the workings attached.

How to use a podcast

Listen first. It is not a test. Do not take notes, do not try to hold the numbers, and do not stop to look anything up. Let it wash over you on a drive or a walk and accept that most of it will not stick. That is not a failure of attention; it is how listening works.

What a podcast does that no paper can is let you hear somebody think. The hesitations, the qualifications, the places where an expert says plainly that we do not know — none of that survives into print, and it is most of what you are actually there for. You are picking up how somebody holds a problem, not a set of facts.

Then talk about it. Bring one thing to a ward round, argue with somebody about it over coffee, disagree with the guest out loud. An episode discussed once is worth three listened to alone.

And if something matters enough to act on, look it up properly afterwards. A podcast is a way in, not a source.

A thirty-year-old woman comes in with chest pain. In Kovacic’s own description of what used to happen, and often still does, “she gets worked up for, you know, pneumonia, maybe pulmonary embolism. But no one’s thinking of a myocardial infarction in a 30-year-old woman.”

She may be having one. And the reason is a disease that is, in the most literal sense, the opposite of the one you were taught to look for.

Why it is worth your time

Jason Kovacic is a practising cardiologist at St Vincent’s in Sydney, a physician-scientist with a laboratory still running at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai after eleven years there, and Executive Director and CEO of the Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute — about twenty-five labs and some two hundred and fifty staff. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection is not a side interest. It is, in his words, “sort of my career”.

That matters for how you listen to him, in both directions. He knows more about this than almost anybody. He is also, in this episode, visibly enthusiastic — and twice he states a bold version of a claim and a careful version of the same claim within a few sentences of each other. Both times the careful version is the one that holds. That is not a criticism. It is the single most useful thing on this page, and the reason to listen to experts talking rather than only to read them.

The idea the episode turns on

Here is the analogy he gives his patients, and it is the best thing in forty-three minutes.

We actually call atherosclerosis hardening of the arteries. And what’s the opposite of hard arteries? It’s soft arteries. And what happens if your arteries are soft? Well, they probably dissect and tear.

He calls it simplistic and probably overly so. It is not. It is very close to what the genetics say.

SCAD and coronary artery disease are not merely different; at the level of the genome they pull in opposite directions. The largest genome-wide study of SCAD found that several of the associated variants have diametrically opposite associations with coronary artery disease, that at six loci where a shared causal variant could be pinned down the risk allele ran the opposite way in each one, and that the genome-wide genetic correlation between the two diseases is negative. The clearest single example is collagen IV: higher expression raises the risk of SCAD, lower expression raises the risk of atherosclerosis. Soft and hard, in one gene.

So Kovacic tells his patients there is a silver lining — having had a SCAD, you are genetically somewhat less likely to go on and develop atherosclerosis. That is a real and lovely thing to be able to say to a frightened thirty-five-year-old.

Where the bold version overreaches

He puts it two ways in consecutive sentences. First, carefully: “the two diseases aren’t mutually exclusive.” Then, less carefully: “I’m yet to see a SCAD patient with significant atherosclerotic disease. They just don’t co-exist together.”

The published correlation is −0.12. That is real, it is statistically solid, and it is weak. An inverse tendency of that size does not make the two diseases mutually exclusive, and a woman of fifty-five who has had a SCAD can perfectly well also have plaque. Take the first sentence, not the second — and notice that he gave you both.

One more thing worth carrying out of that paper, because it reframes the whole risk conversation: genetically elevated blood pressure was the only risk factor shared by SCAD and coronary disease. Body mass index, lipids, type 2 diabetes and smoking showed no effect on SCAD risk at all. Every piece of cardiovascular furniture you own, except the blood pressure cuff, is furniture for the other disease.

The number that does not survive the next sentence

Twice he says SCAD is “the most common cause of heart attack in women under 50”. He is in good company — Sharonne Hayes, who founded the Mayo SCAD registry, is quoted saying the same thing in the American College of Cardiology’s own magazine. And then he gives the number:

So we’re not really sure of the two incidences, but it is the most common cause of heart attack in women under 50. Probably accounts for around 4–5% of MIs in that group.

Those two sentences cannot both be true. Four to five per cent is not most common, and nothing turns on how you read it.

The literature does not settle it either, because the literature does not agree with itself. The same quantity, from four places, one of them his own institute:

SourceShare of heart attacks in women under 50
Kovacic, in this episode4–5%
Victor Chang Cardiac Research Instituteabout 25%
JACC State-of-the-Art Reviewup to 35%
Population-based, Californian discharge dataabout 1%

A thirty-five-fold spread. The 35% is the one everybody quotes, and the review that publishes it flags in the same breath that it is a single-centre figure and that it is for acute coronary syndrome rather than infarction. The 1% is a whole-population count and will be an undercount, for exactly the reason Kovacic gives: a great deal of SCAD was missed, and some still is.

Set against that, the best contemporary account of what actually causes heart attacks in younger people — fourteen hundred events in Olmsted County, Minnesota — puts atherothrombosis at 47% of infarcts in women up to sixty-five. Nothing published puts SCAD above it.

There is one figure here that does not wobble, and he gives it himself in his closing summary: SCAD is 1 to 4% of all acute coronary syndrome. Everyone agrees on that one, because the denominator is unambiguous. It is only when the denominator narrows to young women that the numbers scatter — which tells you the disagreement is about how many are being missed, not about how many there are.

The version of the claim that is solid

Later in the same episode he states it precisely, and this one stands up: SCAD is “way, way the most common cause of heart attack at the time of childbirth” — between 15 and 43% of pregnancy-associated infarcts, and the single commonest cause. His institute says the same.

And he has a mechanism for it that is the soft-artery analogy again, doing real work: the hormones of late pregnancy soften the cervix and the birth canal so the baby can pass, and the same hormones probably soften the coronary arteries. The qualifier — at the time of childbirth — is the whole claim. Without it, it is a different and much shakier statement.

The female artery is not the male artery

This is the thread that ties the episode to everything else in this fortnight, and he reaches it from his own laboratory bench rather than from epidemiology.

Even in atherosclerosis, there are fundamental differences in the arterial wall between males and females… the genes expressed in, for example, smooth muscle cells in the arterial wall, they are not the same between males and females.

In mice with the first SCAD gene ever identified deleted — PHACTR1, which his laboratory has worked on for years and on which they submitted a manuscript the day before this recording — the arteries relax and contract differently, and he attributes that to intrinsic sex differences in the vessel wall rather than to hormones. He is careful to say he does not yet have depth there. He believes it anyway.

Hold that beside the Olmsted numbers and one idea explains both. Atherothrombosis causes 75% of heart attacks in men under sixty-five and 47% in women. In women aged forty-five and under, the non-atherothrombotic causes are collectively as common as atherothrombosis itself. The female coronary artery fails differently because it is, at the level of what its cells are transcribing, a different artery.

A note on our own Top Ten

This month’s Top Ten says that in someone young, atherosclerosis is still two thirds of it. That remains true — for the whole under-sixty-five population, both sexes together. It is three quarters of the men and under half the women, and that distinction did not make it onto the page. Consider it made here. Being young exempts nobody from atherosclerosis; being female changes the odds considerably.

What actually sets it off

Genetics and environment, like most things. On the genetic side, upwards of twenty genes now, most of them pointing at collagen and matrix — the machinery of tissue integrity, elasticity and recoil. COL3A1, the gene behind vascular Ehlers–Danlos, turns up in SCAD too. About 10% of SCAD patients have an identifiable syndromic connective tissue condition underneath.

On the environmental side, stress — “a common, not a universal, but a common feature” — working through adrenergic surges, with extreme physical exertion the commoner trigger in men. He notes the overlap with Takotsubo, which shares the adrenergic surge and the vasospasm, and suspects the two conditions overlap more than has been appreciated.

Then he undercuts his own list, which is the right instinct: he has a patient who dissected while unwrapping Christmas presents.

Two companions to know about. Hypertension is present in a third to half of women with SCAD, less often in the youngest, and labile hypertension in particular — 180 or 200 systolic and then the emergency department — he regards as a genuine trigger. Given that blood pressure is the one risk factor the genetics say these two diseases share, that is more than an association of convenience. And fibromuscular dysplasia is found in about half of women presenting with SCAD; some argue they are one condition showing itself in different arterial beds.

Catching it is one test

There is no clever diagnostic pathway here. The ECG is often unhelpful, because SCAD frequently affects distal vessels and the changes can be subtle. What catches it is the thing you decided not to order.

It doesn’t really matter the age, maybe even the gender. But if you got a suspicious chest pain, just make sure you put a troponin with the blood.

That is Lim summarising, and Kovacic agreeing. He describes being telephoned at six in the evening by a GP with a thirty-year-old woman and a troponin of a hundred, or a thousand — and being grateful for it. A raised troponin in a young woman is not specific; it may be pericarditis, Takotsubo, spasm. It is still a reason to send her in.

The one piece of advice here I would not take wholesale

He offers a tip: “if you’re ordering a troponin, it’s helpful to also order a D-dimer at the same time”, because acute coronary syndrome and pulmonary embolism are confused for each other in both directions, and he will not let a patient leave his emergency department without both.

In his setting that is defensible — he is being phoned about an undifferentiated chest pain already serious enough to be under cardiology review, so the pre-test probability is not low. Generalised to general practice, which is who this episode is for, it runs against settled advice. The American College of Physicians is explicit: establish pre-test probability first, and do not order a D-dimer in a patient who is low-risk and PERC-negative, or in a patient who is high-risk and needs imaging whatever the result. A D-dimer sent without that step buys false positives, and a false positive buys a CT pulmonary angiogram, with its contrast, its radiation and its incidental findings.

Order the troponin on the thirty-year-old. Order the D-dimer when you have asked yourself about pulmonary embolism and the answer came back maybe.

And then he says the thing nobody says

Asked how SCAD is treated, he opens with a disclaimer that is worth more than most of the answers that follow it.

There are actually no randomized clinical trial data whatsoever on how to manage SCAD, absolutely nothing. So everything I’m going to tell you now is anecdotal, expert consensus, you know, retrospective case study kind of data.

Not a hedge dropped in at the end. The front door. Everything below is one experienced man’s practice, and he has told you so.

In the acute phase, mostly leave it alone

The reasoning starts from the pathology. The artery tears, exposing the subintima, and that clots — or the vessel is squeezed shut by the intramural haematoma and clots off that way. Hence aspirin, and often clopidogrel.

But the primary event may be a bleed into the arterial wall, and that is why the escalation stops there: no ticagrelor, no prasugrel. The more potent agents treat the clot and feed the haematoma.

The same logic governs the cath lab. Dissected coronaries tend to heal if left alone, so the interventionalist will generally not stent a stable patient with flow in the vessel. Stenting is for ongoing major ischaemia, heart failure, ventricular tachycardia. By his count two thirds to seventy per cent are managed conservatively now; the largest published cohort puts it higher still, at 83%.

The long game, and what she ends up taking

His sequence, stated plainly:

  1. Clopidogrel stopped early — about a month.
  2. Aspirin stopped at about a year. She ends up on no antiplatelet therapy at all, which will surprise anyone whose instincts were formed on atherosclerosis.
  3. A beta blocker, lifelong if she will tolerate one.
  4. An ARB if the blood pressure needs it — borrowed, like the beta blocker, from the Marfan literature, where both reduce aortic dissection.
  5. No statin unless the LDL is raised on its own merits. “For most women with SCAD, we don’t actually use statins.”

That last one is the tell. This is not a coronary patient as you were taught to build one. The genetics said lipids do not drive this disease, and the prescribing has caught up.

Beta blockers are the hard sell. Young women dislike them — tired, lethargic, and nightmares, which he notes bother the young far more than the old. He pushes anyway, because of what predicts recurrence.

Recurrence — and one of his three is not supported

He names three things retrospectively linked to recurrent SCAD: lack of a beta blocker, presence of fibromuscular dysplasia, and untreated hypertension. The study this comes from is Saw and colleagues, 327 patients followed a median of three years, and it found two of them.

Hypertension raised the risk — hazard ratio 2.46. A beta blocker lowered it — hazard ratio 0.36. Fibromuscular dysplasia was not a predictor of recurrence on multivariable analysis.

That distinction matters at the bedside, and it cuts in the patient’s favour. Screen her for FMD, by all means, and treat the blood pressure that goes with it. But finding FMD is not a reason to tell a frightened thirty-eight-year-old she is more likely to dissect again. The two things that change her odds are the tablet she finds annoying and the blood pressure you are already measuring.

His headline recurrence figure of 20 to 30% is also the top half of the range: that cohort recorded 10.4% over three years, and the field reports anywhere from 10 to 30% depending on how you count. By his closing summary he has settled on “roughly 20%” — and adds the part that matters most to the person being told: most recurrences are less severe than the first event and can be managed. If you only ever repeat one sentence from this episode to a patient, repeat that one.

The best practical tip in the episode

SCAD and fibromuscular dysplasia both travel with migraine, and he knows why: the same genes. The first SCAD gene, PHACTR1, is also a migraine gene. So —

If in my SCAD patients that also get migraine, I try them on propranolol if they don’t have asthma… they often love propranolol.

One tablet, two problems: the beta blocker she does not want for a disease she cannot feel, prescribed instead for the headaches she has every month and can. The compliance problem solves itself. He calls it one of his little tips. It is the sort of thing you only learn from somebody who runs the clinic.

And when that fails he simply keeps going — metoprolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol, “I try to find a combination that will work for them.” Not a protocol. Persistence, bought by that hazard ratio of 0.36.

The genetics are better than he has time to explain

PHACTR1 is not loosely associated with these things. The same risk allele raises the risk of fibromuscular dysplasia, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, cervical artery dissection, migraine and hypertension — and the opposite allele raises the risk of coronary artery disease. One locus, one direction for the soft-artery family and the other for the hard.

Note what is in that list. Cervical artery dissection. Remember it for the next paragraph, because it turns a piece of advice that sounds like prejudice into straightforward anatomy.

The prescribing point, which is the one to actually remember

If you take one thing from forty-three minutes, take this. Fluoroquinolones. Ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, norfloxacin.

I basically tell all my SCAD and FMD patients to just consider themselves — they’re not technically allergic, but they’re strongly contraindicated in those groups. And as you know, occasionally we do use something like ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin for UTIs and other reasons in young women.

That is the collision. A thirty-five-year-old woman with a urinary tract infection is exactly the patient who gets a quinolone, and exactly the patient in whom he does not want one. The same drugs rupture Achilles tendons, and for the same reason — Lim gets there himself in the episode, and Kovacic’s answer is the whole thesis in one line: “a very simplistic way to generalise things, but softening of the tissues, right?”

What the warning actually says, and how far he is extending it

The FDA safety communication of December 2018 applies to the fluoroquinolone class and concerns aortic aneurysm and dissection. It says the drugs should not be used in people with the genetic conditions associated with aortic aneurysm and dissection — naming Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos and Loeys–Dietz syndromes — unless no other treatment is available. The TGA has issued its own Australian advisory on the same question.

SCAD and FMD are not named. Kovacic is extending an aortic warning to a coronary and extracoronary arteriopathy, by analogy. The analogy is a fair one — FMD is an arteriopathy, and about one SCAD patient in ten carries one of the very syndromes the FDA lists — but it is an extension, and worth knowing as one.

It is also the cheapest advice on this page to follow. There is nearly always another antibiotic for a urinary tract infection. Where the evidence is absent and the alternative is free, take the precaution.

The list he gives every patient, and where it outruns the evidence

So the chiropractors out, I just say full stop, chiropractors done, because there are some tragic, well-reported stories of neck manipulations in these women with softer arteries causing carotid vertebral artery dissection, causing stroke and even death.

That one is well founded, and the genetics above are why. A woman who has dissected a coronary artery carries alleles that also predispose to dissecting a carotid or a vertebral. She is not the general population and the general population’s risk does not apply to her. Yoga with aggressive neck manoeuvres goes the same way, and the published expert advice agrees about extreme head and neck positions.

Then the list keeps going. Anything with sudden acceleration or deceleration — bungee jumping, parachuting, motocross, car racing; gentle roller coasters probably fine, “crazy roller coasters not”. Heavy lifting limited to about 8 kg in a weights class. Scuba out, snorkelling fine. High-altitude mountaineering out. Ice baths, after a couple of cases following “these extreme physiological changes”. And, a couple of years on, when she is confident again and wants to travel: not too far off grid, “the Tasmanian wilderness or wherever it may be”.

He is honest about the standing of all of it. “There’s really no data.” “It just doesn’t seem intuitively right to me.” He knows what he is offering.

And he gets the pushback you would expect. “Most women just look at me like, come on, you’re kidding me — but occasionally I’ve had women say, what, I can’t race Rallycross anymore? That’s my life.” He stopped her. One of his patients, written up in The Australian, went on to have a neck dissection.

The published advice is less restrictive than his

Tweet, Olin, Bonikowske, Adlam and Hayes — between them the Mayo registry, the European cohorts and the genetics — set out expert guidance on exercise in SCAD and FMD in the European Heart Journal in 2021. On several of these points it does not go where he goes.

It states that there are no data to support restricting scuba diving, and none to support restricting roller coasters or skydiving. It sets no weight limit in kilograms, asking instead for low-resistance training with proper technique and no Valsalva or straining. And it is more positive than prohibitive: moderate aerobic exercise 30 to 40 minutes, five to seven days a week, and cardiac rehabilitation for every patient, on the grounds that exercise is good for vascular function and should not be avoided out of fear of dissection.

Neither he nor they have a trial. The difference is what each does with that absence. Note which way the cost falls: the quinolone advice costs a woman nothing, and being told she may not go to the Tasmanian wilderness, dive, or race the car that is her life costs her a great deal. Caution is not free when it is spent on somebody else’s life.

What they agree on: extreme or unusual exertion is reported as a trigger in up to 29% of cases, heavy muscle-building deserves caution, and the neck is genuinely different.

The counterweight is his too, and it should be said as loudly as the prohibitions: light to moderate aerobic activity is always in. “I strongly encourage them to do that” — for the heart, and for the mental health, the stress and the anxiety that are half of this illness.

The part he says belongs to general practice

He is clear about the division of labour, and generous with it. The antiplatelet balancing and the beta blocker titration, he says, are probably better left to a specialist. What is not:

That ongoing care is, you know, and the mental care is so critically a part of SCAD… here you’ve got a woman in the prime of her life, and the last thing she expects to be doing is having a heart attack.

He refers to support groups, to psychologists and psychiatrists, and — “I’m obviously no psychiatrist” — will occasionally start an antidepressant himself for the anxiety and the anguish about recurrence. Roughly one SCAD survivor in three carries clinically significant post-traumatic symptoms years afterwards, so this is not a soft add-on to the cardiology. It is a large part of the illness.

His argument for treating it is blunt to the point of discomfort: a woman who is petrified is not living, and she has young children. You can hear him choosing not to soften it.

His closing list of what she should be connected to is accordingly broader than a cardiology follow-up: mental health support, physiotherapy, and cardiac rehabilitation — which the published exercise guidance says plainly should be offered to every SCAD patient, and which is the one intervention on this page that is both free of harm and backed by something.

Screening, and the thing that does not work

Asked whether you can screen for SCAD, his answer is a clean no, with a useful substitution.

We don’t screen for SCAD as such. What we screen for is arteriopathy changes that can manifest as fibromuscular dysplasia… There is no hallmark of people at risk of SCAD in the coronary arteries.

Which means the two investigations a worried patient will ask for are the two that will not help: a CT coronary angiogram and a stress test are both useless for this. What you look at instead is elsewhere — renal, carotid and other arteries, for the changes of fibromuscular dysplasia. He defers the detail to a separate episode.

And then he does something unusual

Lim asks what to tell a woman who has had a SCAD and wants a baby. Kovacic answers with his own practice, and labels it as such: “this is no guidelines, there’s very little clinical data, but nevertheless, these are my pretty hard rules on this.”

He will agree to see a woman through a pregnancy after SCAD only if:

  1. The previous SCAD was not peripartum. If it happened around childbirth, he will not sanction a further pregnancy at all.
  2. She is at least a year out from the event.
  3. She is free of chest pain — some have ongoing microvascular angina afterwards, and he wants none of it.
  4. Genetic testing is negative, to exclude the roughly one in ten with vascular Ehlers–Danlos, Marfan or another arteriopathy underneath.

If all four are met, she is seen in his clinic and a high-risk obstetric team is assembled around her. Then the blood pressure is watched very closely through the pregnancy, with a low threshold to treat — and the agent he reaches for is labetalol, which is both a standard antihypertensive in pregnancy and the beta blockade he wants her on anyway. The international experience, he says, is that it can be done safely, but it needs very specialist hands.

He is careful about the word sanction, and the care is worth noticing: “the ultimate decision of getting pregnant or not is obviously not my decision, it’s their decision. But my decision here is whether I’ll sanction it and whether I think it’s a reasonable and safe thing.” Then, without being asked, he tells you how it turns out when it works — a Christmas card a few days earlier from a family he saw through exactly this, a healthy baby safely delivered, “one of the highlights of my week”.

You may or may not agree with the rules; the first is a considerable thing to say to a woman who wants a second child, and it rests, like everything else here, on no trial at all. What is worth copying is the labelling. He tells you there is no evidence, tells you he has made a decision anyway, and tells you exactly what the decision is. Most of medicine is conducted in that gap. Most of it is not announced.

Which is, in the end, what this episode is for. Not the disease — you will see three to eight SCADs a year in a big city unit, by his own count at St Vincent’s, and far fewer than that anywhere smaller. It is the habit of hearing somebody say the striking thing and then, unprompted, say the true one.

Challenge for you

When did you last put a troponin on a thirty-year-old?

Not when it was obvious. The one where you thought about it, decided the story was musculoskeletal or anxiety or reflux, and let it go — or the one where you ordered it feeling slightly foolish and were right.

Tell me what made the difference, either way. I am also interested in the other half of this: whether anyone reading has had a SCAD come through their department, and what it was first called. That is a question dozens of people can each answer in five minutes and nobody can answer alone.
