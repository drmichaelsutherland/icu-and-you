Early middle age

Thirty to forty-five — the first patients who arrive carrying a history

Find your level: Early years  ·  Clinical years  ·  Final years

Everything earlier in this series was about a patient whose story was short. This is the first band where they arrive with one already written — a GP, a diagnosis, a medication list, and usually people who depend on them.

The tiers map roughly onto the three phases of a six-year medical programme — the science, then the clinical reasoning, then what you will actually be expected to do with your own hands. Read the one that fits, and glance at the one above it.

What is difficult here is not any single disease. It is that two different kinds of patient arrive through the same door. External causes lead every Australian age band up to forty-four, and from forty-five coronary heart disease takes over as the leading cause of death in men. You are working the fifteen years in which those lines cross, and the same unit will hold both on the same night.

If this page has one argument, it is this: in this band the drug chart is a diagnostic document. More of the illness you will meet here is caused by treatment than in any earlier age group, and the medication list is where that is written down. Read it before you read the notes.

Tier one

A body that has started to change, and a pharmacology that changes with it

Phase 1 — the foundational years, and anything on body composition, drug handling and the physiology of ageing

  • Where the two lines crossThis band is defined by a crossover rather than by a disease. Up to forty-four, what kills people is something that happened; from forty-five it is something that grew. Nothing about a thirty-two-year-old and a forty-four-year-old is the same clinical problem, and the age on the front of the notes will not tell you which one you have.
  • Reserve does not disappear, it narrowsAnd it narrows in the variables nobody measures: lean muscle mass, chest wall compliance, arterial stiffness, maximal oxygen uptake. None of these appears on an observation chart, which is exactly why a patient in this band can look physiologically identical to a twenty-five-year-old and behave differently under load.
  • Body composition changes what a dose meansVolume of distribution is a property of the body, not of the number on the scale. A lipophilic drug distributes into fat, so the loading dose rises and the offset is prolonged; a hydrophilic drug does not, so giving it on total body weight overdoses it. This is the mechanism behind every “which weight?” question you will ever be asked.
  • And the honest part of thatThe published guidance is not as settled as it is taught. Pharmacokinetic data in obesity are missing for most drugs, and sources disagree with each other — including, in one widely used exam resource, the model answer disagreeing with its own discussion about which weight to use for lipophilic drugs. The useful habit is not a memorised table. It is to know which weight your unit’s protocol means, say it out loud when you prescribe, and ask for levels where the therapeutic index is narrow.
  • Why the obese patient desaturates so fastFunctional residual capacity falls as the abdomen loads the diaphragm, and it falls towards closing capacity — so small airways begin shutting during ordinary tidal breathing, shunt rises, and the oxygen store that buys you time during apnoea is much smaller. Meanwhile oxygen consumption is higher. Less in the tank, and a faster drain.
  • What chronic alcohol does to a myocardiumNot an acute poisoning but a slow cardiomyopathy, and it accounts for 23 to 47% of what gets labelled idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy in European series. It is also one of the few cardiomyopathies that substantially recovers if the exposure stops, which is why the quantity belongs in the history and not in the social section.
  • Thiamine runs out in weeks, not yearsBody stores are small, the vitamin is water-soluble and not stored in fat, and absorption is impaired by alcohol. That is the whole mechanism behind a rule you will be given in tier three and rarely told the reason for.
  • Why an SGLT2 inhibitor uncouples glucose from ketonesThe drug makes the kidney spill glucose, so the blood sugar stays unremarkable. Ketogenesis is driven by the insulin to glucagon ratio, not by the glucose, and fasting or illness drives it anyway. So the two things you were taught to see together — a high sugar and ketoacidosis — come apart. The physiology is the entire reason the diagnosis gets missed.
  • The autoimmune windowMany autoimmune diseases have their onset in exactly this band and most of them fall disproportionately on women. For intensive care that matters in two directions: the disease itself can present as organ failure, and the immunosuppression used to treat it changes what infection looks like when it arrives.

If you take one thing: nothing in this band has changed enough to show up on an observation chart, and almost everything has changed enough to alter how a drug behaves.

Likely to be examined as: a short answer on the pharmacokinetic consequences of obesity, or on the respiratory physiology of obesity and why it shortens safe apnoea time. A fair viva opener is why can a patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor be in ketoacidosis with a blood glucose of nine?

Tier two

The drug chart is a diagnostic document

Phase 2 — the clinical years, integrated clinical studies, and the ICE

  • Read it before you read the notesIn every earlier band the medication list is short and mostly irrelevant. Here it is the single most information-dense page in the file, and several of the diagnoses below cannot be reached without it. If you take one habit from this page into the clinical years, take that one.
  • A normal blood sugar does not exclude ketoacidosisOn an SGLT2 inhibitor — prescribed now for diabetes, heart failure and kidney disease, so you will meet them constantly — the Australian definition is blood ketones above 1.0 mmol/L with a standard base excess below −5, and a glucose below 14 mmol/L makes it euglycaemic. Fasting, bowel preparation, surgery and intercurrent illness are the precipitants. Measure blood ketones, not urine.
  • Immunotherapy has a cardiac complication and it is earlyMyocarditis from an immune checkpoint inhibitor occurs in about 1%, at a median of 27 to 34 days after the first dose — so it is a first-cycle event, not a late one — and it kills 27 to 50% of those who get it. Troponin is raised in 94%. The clinical lesson is small and specific: anyone on these drugs who feels unwell gets a troponin.
  • Clozapine, and an Australian monitoring protocol worth knowingWeekly troponin and CRP for the first four weeks, because that is when almost all of it happens. Troponin above twice the upper limit of normal, or CRP above 100 mg/L, means stop the drug and get an echocardiogram. You will be the person who notices the result.
  • Serotonin toxicity, and its mirror imageA combination rather than one agent at a toxic level, which is why the drug chart finds it and a single level will not. Clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation and hyperthermia, worse in the legs than the arms, within hours. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is the other way round in every respect: days rather than hours, rigidity rather than clonus, reflexes down rather than up.
  • Distrust the words previously fit and wellThey do two jobs at once. They delay the diagnosis of whatever has been accumulating unrecorded through this whole band, and they stand in for a decision nobody has actually made. He’s young, he’ll tolerate it sounds like a clinical judgement and is frequently a substitute for one.
  • Chest pain in a woman of thirty-eightNot reassuring, and both obvious reflexes are wrong. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection is an intramural haematoma, not a plaque, so thrombolysis extends it and a wire can enter the false lumen. Conservative management is the default — 83% in the largest published cohort. The patient most at risk is exactly the one described: young, female, troponin rising, and a team that has pattern- matched to a STEMI.
  • Heart failure, not heart attackIn a registry of 29,035 cardiac intensive care admissions, heart failure led the diagnoses in the eighteen to thirty-nines (26% against 20%) while acute coronary syndrome led in the older group (29% against 11%). And of those who went on to mechanical circulatory support, 29% of the young received ECMO against 10%. A young adult in cardiogenic shock is likelier to need a therapy the hospital you are standing in may not have.
  • A first psychosis at thirty with neurological signs is not a first psychosisAnti-NMDA receptor encephalitis runs in a sequence — psychiatric prodrome, then a movement disorder with orofacial dyskinesia, then seizures and autonomic instability. About 78% are women and roughly 28% have an ovarian teratoma. Two things to remember: cerebrospinal fluid antibodies are more sensitive than serum, and a normal MRI excludes nothing.
  • Who is in front of you, and what the raw numbers hideIn 330,712 Australian ICU admissions, the median age at admission was 51.2 years for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients against 66.5 — fifteen years younger — with the same illness severity on arrival. Unadjusted twelve-month mortality was identical, 15.8% against 15.7%. Adjusted for age, diagnosis, severity and socio-economic status it was 1.24 (95% CI 1.16–1.33). The unadjusted figure reassures; the adjusted one does not.
  • The history here is partly about other peopleAsk a thirty-eight-year-old what they are worried about and the answer is usually not their own prognosis. It is work, money, and who is collecting the children. That is clinically relevant information, not small talk: it predicts self-discharge, it shapes what they will consent to, and it is the thing nobody else will have written down.

If you take one thing: more of the illness in this band is caused by treatment than in any band before it. The chart that lists the treatment is therefore a list of differential diagnoses.

Likely to be examined as: a case with an unremarkable glucose and a metabolic acidosis, or a young woman with chest pain and a troponin rise. In a long case, the marks for “relevant medication history” are rarely for reciting the list — they are for saying what each drug makes possible.

Tier three

What you will be expected to do

Phase 3 — the final clinical years, the pre-internship term, and your first nights on call

  • Take the medication history properly, and name your sourceThis is a job that will fall to you, and it is the one on this page with the highest yield. The ward list is not the source. The GP, the community pharmacy, My Health Record and the person who came in with them are. Write down where each item came from, because the next person needs to know how much to trust it.
  • Thiamine before glucoseIn anyone with chronic alcohol use or poor intake, give thiamine before or with a glucose load, not after. The mechanism is in tier one: the stores are small and glucose metabolism consumes what is left.
  • Do not withhold insulin because the sugar is normalIn euglycaemic ketoacidosis the insulin is what switches off ketogenesis, and the glucose infusion exists so that you can keep giving it. This is the error that does the harm, and it is made by people who treat ordinary ketoacidosis perfectly well.
  • Send the troponinOn anyone on an immune checkpoint inhibitor who feels unwell, and on anyone in the first month of clozapine with a fever, tachycardia or breathlessness. Both are cheap tests on an unglamorous suspicion, and both have a mortality attached to missing them.
  • Recognise serotonin toxicity at the bedsideClonus is the sign to go looking for, and the ankles are where to look. Stop the serotonergic drugs, cool actively, and treat agitation and rigidity with benzodiazepines. Do not add another serotonergic agent, which is the commonest way it is made worse.
  • Know when the conversation is a retrieval conversationIf a patient in this band is in cardiogenic shock in a hospital without extracorporeal support, the referral is not something to raise after the next blood gas. Your job as a student or intern is not to make that call — it is to recognise that it is the call, and to say so to somebody senior early.
  • Find out who is looking after the children tonightIt is a real clinical task, it is nearly always delegated to the most junior person present, and it is almost never written in anybody’s curriculum. Ask it early, because the answer sometimes turns out to be “nobody”.
  • Ask about alcohol in units, and write the number down“Social drinker” is not a history. The thresholds that matter clinically are quantitative, the withdrawal you will be managing on night three is predicted by the number, and nobody downstream can reconstruct it from an adjective.
  • Do not let “he’s young” do the work of a decisionRegistry data do show young adults receiving more of everything and dying less — but those comparisons are unadjusted, and the comparator is everybody over forty. Escalation in this band is usually right. It should still be a decision somebody has made out loud, with a reason you could write in the notes.

If you take one thing: the two jobs on this list that nobody will praise you for — the real medication history and finding out who is at home — are the two that change what happens next.

Likely to be examined as: a prescribing or medication-reconciliation station, or a communication station with a patient refusing admission because of work. In an OSCE, the marks for the alcohol history are for the quantity and the pattern, not for having raised the subject.

Over to you

Which tier did you read — and what were you taught that this page contradicts?

Tell me your year, which tier fitted, and what was missing, too basic or too advanced. That part is the same every time, and it is how these get better.

This one has a second question, because three things on this page are still taught the other way round. That a normal blood glucose rules out ketoacidosis. That chest pain with a troponin rise in a young woman is a STEMI until proved otherwise, and should be treated as one. That a first episode of psychosis at thirty is a psychiatric diagnosis. Which of those three were you taught, and which way round? No names and no universities. A few dozen replies would tell me something I cannot find out any other way.
