Eight scenarios and a town

The ACRRM Advanced Specialised Training StAMPS in Emergency Medicine — what it is, how it is marked, and the 2027 dates that are closer than they look

A second examination joins this section. Until now Exam focus has been the CICM First Part, which is one college’s entrance examination and not everybody’s. This one is for the ACRRM pathway, and it exists because a registrar sitting it asked what was on this site for her, and the honest answer was nothing.

StAMPS is not an OSCE and it is not a viva, and candidates who prepare for one or the other tend to be surprised by it. It is eight unfolding scenarios, three hours, conducted online, set in a town the College invents and hands you in advance.

The name unpacks to Structured Assessment using Multiple Patient Scenarios, and the College itself calls it a blend of the objective structured clinical assessment and the traditional viva voce. The version in question here is the Advanced Specialised Training StAMPS in Emergency Medicine — AST EM StAMPS — one of five AST disciplines that use the format, alongside Adult Internal Medicine, Mental Health, Paediatrics and Surgery.

What it actually is

  • Eight scenarios, three questions eachTwenty-four questions in all. Each scenario runs ten minutes, and the material unfolds as you go rather than arriving all at once.
  • Ten minutes between each oneFive to read the next scenario and five held back for technical problems. You read all eight at the start as well.
  • Three hours in totalRun as rotations over one or two days depending on how many people are sitting. You rotate between examiners rather than between rooms.
  • OnlineDelivered through the College’s assessment system over Zoom. You find an approved venue and nominate an invigilator, and it can be sat from overseas if the venue and the information technology hold up.
  • You talk to a personThis is the part that catches people. You are not performing a task in front of a silent observer. You are explaining your reasoning to an examiner who will push on it, and who will ask what you would do when the thing you just proposed is not available.
The town you are given beforehand

Every StAMPS comes with a community profile: the place the scenarios happen in, its services, its distances, who else is on and what you can and cannot get. It is published on the College website before the exam, you may keep it during reading time, and you may keep it in front of you throughout.

Emergency Medicine has a profile of its own — the other four AST disciplines share one. The EM profile describes a regional hospital serving a vast district, which is a different problem from the small-town general practice the Core Generalist profile describes.

Learn it the way you would learn a new job before the first shift. Candidates are explicitly expected to make decisions that are realistic for that hospital, and an answer that would be right in a tertiary centre and impossible in the profile is a wrong answer.

What it is actually testing

The College says StAMPS assesses higher-order functions in a contextualised setting, which is administrative language for something quite specific: you are not being asked what the guideline says. You are being asked what you would do, why, and then what you would do instead.

That last move is the whole examination. The scenarios are written so that management has to begin before the diagnosis is settled, and the examiner will introduce a system or patient factor that removes your first answer. The retrieval aircraft is three hours away. The scanner is down. The patient will not stay. What you do then is the thing being marked, and it cannot be revised for in the ordinary way.

How it is marked

Each scenario earns five separate scores, each on a scale of nought to seven, using a rubric with written behavioural anchors:

  1. Management in part one, including the medical and the rural contextual factors.
  2. Management in part two, the same.
  3. Management in part three, the same.
  4. Problem definition and systematic approach.
  5. Communication and professionalism.

Five scores, eight scenarios, seven marks each: a theoretical maximum of 280. Examiners also record, scenario by scenario, whether they judge you to be at the standard. The pass mark is set by a Delphi panel and verified statistically afterwards, so it moves between sittings — it was 171 at the October 2026 sitting. Candidates within ten marks below it were formally reviewed before results were released, by an examiner who had not seen the original performance.

Two College documents disagree, and it is worth knowing which to believe

The online curriculum pages describe six scored areas per scenario and a maximum of 336, the extra domain being flexibility to a changing context. The Fellowship Assessment Handbook and the most recent public report for this exam both describe five areas and a maximum of 280.

Two sources agree, including the one published after the last sitting, so five and 280 is what I have used here. But this is exactly the sort of thing to confirm with the College rather than with me, and it is a reminder that the public report for your own discipline is the most current document the College produces.

What happened at the last sitting

At the October 2026 AST Emergency Medicine StAMPS, 49 candidates sat and 32 passed — a pass rate of 65.3 per cent. That is a real examination with a real failure rate, and it is worth knowing before you decide how much preparation is enough.

More instructive is the spread across scenarios. The station on paracetamol overdose and mental health was passed by 96 per cent of candidates. The station on burns, mass casualty and airway was passed by 38 per cent, and a gastroenteritis outbreak paired with a shoulder dislocation by 46.

Look at what the hard ones have in common. They are not harder medicine. They are scenarios with two problems running at once, where the second one is a system problem — many patients, a public health response, a resource that will not stretch. The single-patient clinical stations are where candidates do well. The stations about running the department are where they do not.

If you take one thing from this page

Prepare for the scenarios in which the medicine is easy and the situation is not. On the published figures that is where the marks are lost, by a wide margin, and it is the part that reading a textbook does not touch.

What the examiners keep asking for

  • Clinical leadershipSetting priorities early, coordinating a team, anticipating deterioration, escalating in time, planning retrieval, and thinking past the immediate task to disposition and ongoing care. The College says candidates are encouraged to demonstrate this throughout, which is a polite way of saying that many do not.
  • A structured approach, out loudUse a consistent framework, hold a broad differential before narrowing it, and say explicitly how each new piece of information changes the plan.
  • Time-critical care firstImmediate threats to life before secondary problems, and say that you are doing it.
  • The rural context, usedNot mentioned. Used. Decisions that fit the services the community profile actually describes.
  • Public healthNotification, infection prevention, outbreak management, which agencies you involve and what the community impact is. This is the content area that separates this exam from a metropolitan emergency examination, and it is in the stations with the lowest pass rates.
  • Procedures, in fullPreparation, consent, risks, how you confirm it worked, documentation, complications and aftercare — not just the technique.
  • Communication under time pressureClear and concise, culturally safe, and practised against a clock. Ten minutes is shorter than it sounds.
The 2027 dates, and one deadline that has nearly gone

AST StAMPS Emergency Medicine sits twice in 2027. Saturday 1 May, with enrolment open 12 January to 21 February and results on 27 May. And 9–10 October, with enrolment open 7 June to 18 July and results on 4 November.

The mock exams are the thing with the near deadline. Mock StAMPS runs 6–7 March 2027, and enrolment for it closes on 6 December 2026 — which, as this page goes up, is about a week away. The second mock is 11–12 September 2027, enrolling 7 June to 29 July.

Fees, excluding GST: StAMPS $3,970, the mock $1,545, a study group $1,165. They are GST free for registrars sitting on an ACRRM pathway. All dates and prices are the College’s and are subject to change, so check them rather than taking them from here.

And the rest of AST Emergency Medicine

The examination is one requirement among several, and the others take a year. Twelve months full-time, or part-time at no less than half, at an ACRRM-accredited hospital or hospital network. A specialist principal supervisor who holds ACEM Fellowship, or FACRRM with AST Emergency Medicine. An average of at least four hours of protected teaching a week, recorded.

Three courses, one from each group: trauma, meaning EMST or ETM; adult, meaning REST, ALS2, ELS, ACME or EMAC; and paediatrics, meaning APLS or APEM. Emergency obstetrics is encouraged rather than required. Then four supervisor reports, plan and progress reports every three months, at least five mini-CEXs covering a range of presentations and ages, five case-based discussions, and the logbook.

The limits of this page

Challenge for you

If you have sat StAMPS: which scenario did you walk out of knowing you had lost marks, and what was it about?

I am asking specifically because of the published pass rates. The stations people fail are not the ones with difficult medicine in them; they are the ones with two problems running at once and a system that will not stretch. I would like to know whether that matches what it felt like from the inside.

And if you are preparing for 2027, tell me what you cannot find anywhere. This series will be written to that rather than to what I assume you need, and there is a great deal more ACRRM material to write than CICM material.
