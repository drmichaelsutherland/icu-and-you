ICU and You  ·  The Top Ten

October 2026

A month of material, reduced to the ten things worth carrying

Seventeen new pieces went up this month — the young adult fortnight, two new columns and two entirely new groups. This is what survived the boiling down.

The Top Ten is the absolute distillation — the ten takeaways from everything published as new material in the month. Short, blunt, and chosen for one criterion only: would knowing this change what somebody does? Each links back to the piece it came from if you want the argument behind it.

If you read nothing else from October, read this page. It will take you four minutes.

Subscribers get it first. The Top Ten goes out with the newsletter a few days before it appears here — come and join us if you would rather not wait.

  1. There is no four-minute rule, and do not tilt the bed.

    ANZCOR Guideline 11.10 sets no time threshold for resuscitative hysterotomy. Displace the uterus manually and compress on a flat surface — tilting degrades your compressions. The four-minute rule you were probably taught is not Australian guidance and was never a rule.

    From Bare Bones No. 3, Medical Students’ Place No. 6 and Superquiz #65

  2. About half the women who die are not dying of anything obstetric.

    They are dying of the things you do every day — sepsis, cardiac disease, neurology, overdose. If the pregnancy makes her somebody else’s patient in your mind, you have handed away the half you are actually best at.

    From Podcast #60, Katie Cranfield

  3. A creatinine of 105 two days postpartum will not make you blink, and it should.

    A normal creatinine in pregnancy is never really more than 75. You are so used to seeing numbers well into the hundreds that a woman whose renal function has substantially fallen looks unremarkable on the screen — unless you are specifically looking for it.

    From Podcast #60 and Superquiz #65

  4. The reserve that hides deterioration is the same reserve that runs out all at once.

    They hold a normal blood pressure through a loss that would have dropped anybody else in the unit, and then lose it in one step. The number in front of you describes the vasoconstriction, not the blood volume. The trend matters more than the value, and a normal set of observations under thirty is less reassuring than the same set at twice the age.

    From Bare Bones No. 3 and Mind Map #36

  5. In someone young, atherosclerosis is still two thirds of it.

    Being young exempts nobody: familial hypercholesterolaemia accounts for 9% of infarcts under fifty, and 43% of those patients were not on a statin beforehand. The remaining third is where the misclassification lives — spontaneous coronary dissection causes 11% of infarcts in women under sixty-five, and over half of those were first called something else.

    From Mnemonic #58, LIPIDS

  6. A child gets the adult dose of antivenom.

    There is no weight-based calculation. The dose neutralises the venom the snake injected, and the snake did not adjust for the size of the patient. One vial covers one snake; extra vials do not hasten recovery, and there is no role for redosing.

    From Your Presentations No. 1, Maheshani Wijenatha

  7. The venom detection kit has left the pathway.

    No longer recommended for the management of snake bite in Australia. The false-positive rate in patients who turn out not to be envenomed has been reported around 36%, and the result almost never changed what was given. Geography, syndrome and the bloods choose the antivenom now.

    From Your Presentations No. 1

  8. Go and read your own unit’s insulin infusion protocol. It may name a product that no longer exists.

    In Australia a staged withdrawal takes three more presentations on 31 December 2026, including Levemir in both forms, which leaves no insulin detemir at all from 2027. Wherever you are, the general point holds: protocols name brands, brands get discontinued, and nobody tells the protocol. Concentrations are not interchangeable either — U-100 and U-300 glargine are not bioequivalent.

    From Drugstore No. 3

  9. Dry gases in a dry airway make brick-like mucus that will obstruct the tube.

    A circuit without humidification can produce an airway you cannot ventilate through — lungs that feel like rock, a tidal volume down at anatomical dead space, and a patient who looks exactly like severe asthma and has none. In biological terms, you need a patent tube and plenty of lube.

    From Anecdote City No. 1

  10. “Assume she is pregnant” survives on asymmetry of consequence, not on women being unreliable.

    The two best studies say she is right essentially every time. The rule is worth keeping because the cost of the rare miss is so lopsided — which is a reason to test anyway, and not a reason to disbelieve her to her face. And assume does no work: only the test has power, and the gap between the two is where people come to harm.

    From Aphorism #40

And one to keep

The phrase everybody uses to explain Nigeria’s maternal deaths is a title, not a diagnosis — and the people who showed that are Nigerian.

In Lagos, most of the women who died had travelled under ten kilometres and arrived within half an hour. What failed was oxytocin that had stopped working, blood the family had to go and find, and theatres that could not open. From Out of Africa No. 1.
