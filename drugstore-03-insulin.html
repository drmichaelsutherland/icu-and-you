ICU and You  ·  Drugstore  ·  Number 3

Insulin

One hormone, a shrinking list of Australian products, and an infusion that behaves like none of them

About this series

Drugstore is for drug profiles. Each one runs the same way: the few things that matter most, then the practical business of doses, routes and the errors that recur, and then the pharmacology proper — kinetics, dynamics, indications and adverse effects.

The intention is to serve two readers at once. The clinician who needs the drug at three in the morning, and the First Part candidate who has to explain its metabolism. Those are different needs, and most references choose one.

This one is insulin, with the weight on the preparations — which is where the practical difficulty actually lives. It sits in a fortnight about young adults because ketoacidosis from omitted insulin is one of the most predictable admissions in that age group, and because a twenty-four-year-old with type 1 diabetes will outlive every protocol you write for them.

There is one insulin. There are about twenty ways to give it, they are not interchangeable, and the Australian list is shorter this year than it was last year and will be shorter again in ten weeks.

The ten that matter

  1. Prescribe in units, written in full. Never u, never U, never a trailing zero. The NSW Clinical Excellence Commission names this as a documented source of tenfold error, and if a dose is ambiguous the instruction is to contact the prescriber and have the order amended before administering — not to interpret it.
  2. Clear is not short-acting. It used to be a safe shorthand. Glargine and degludec are clear solutions and last more than a day. The only thing clarity now tells you is that the preparation is a solution rather than a suspension.
  3. Only a solution can go intravenously. Every suspension — Protaphane, the premixes, NovoMix, Humalog Mix, Ryzodeg — and every long-acting analogue, including Optisulin, Toujeo, Tresiba and the weekly Awiqli, is prohibited by its own Product Information from intravenous use. In practice the infusion is neutral human insulin.
  4. Insulin sticks to the giving set. The Actrapid Product Information says plainly that on mixing with infusion fluid “an unpredictable amount of insulin will be adsorbed to the infusion material”. This is why protocols tell you to prime and waste, and the volumes they specify differ.
  5. Basal insulin is never omitted in type 1 diabetes. Not for fasting, not for a procedure, not because the glucose is normal. A person with no endogenous insulin becomes ketoacidotic without it whatever the glucose is doing, and this is among the most common serious inpatient insulin errors.
  6. In ketoacidosis the endpoint is the ketones, not the glucose. Queensland’s protocol is the cleanest statement of it: stop when ketones are below 0.6 mmol/L and the anion gap has closed, explicitly disregarding a residual hyperchloraemic acidosis.
  7. Give the long-acting insulin at least two hours before the infusion stops. Subcutaneous basal insulin takes that long to establish. Stopping an infusion and writing up subcutaneous insulin at the same moment is how people go back into ketoacidosis on the ward.
  8. The cartridge belongs to its own pen. As the disposable pens disappear, cartridges are replacing them — and Novo cartridges fit only NovoPen devices, Humulin cartridges only HumaPen. NSW Health has issued a safety notice specifically about this.
  9. Never draw insulin out of a pen with a syringe. The pen's internal markings do not correspond to a syringe scale. For the U-200 products the error is a doubling.
  10. Tight glycaemic control is settled, and the answer is no. NICE-SUGAR found it killed people; TGC-Fast found that even with the hypoglycaemia nearly engineered out, it bought nothing.

One practical warning that belongs with that table. The concentrations are not interchangeable unit for unit. The Toujeo Product Information says so in terms: glargine 100 units/mL and glargine 300 units/mL “are not bioequivalent and are not directly interchangeable”. Moving an adult from U-100 to U-300 needs roughly 10 to 18 per cent more units, and about 8 per cent more in a child. Treating a concentration change as arithmetic is one of the ways people get hurt.

What disappears on 31 December 2026

Australia is part-way through a staged withdrawal of its human and older insulins. On 31 December 2026 three more presentations go: the Actrapid Penfill cartridge, the Protaphane Penfill cartridge, and Levemir in both the FlexPen and the Penfill — which means no insulin detemir in Australia at all from 2027, with no like-for-like substitute. Actrapid and Protaphane continue as 10 mL vials only.

Already gone, if your protocol still names them: Mixtard (2024), Fiasp vials and FlexTouch, Ryzodeg FlexTouch, Protaphane InnoLet, Humulin R vials (February 2026), Humulin 30/70 vials, Humalog vials, and Humulin NPH vials (August 2026). And Lantus has not been available in Australia since July 2020 — it was replaced by Optisulin and Semglee. Written material across the country still says Lantus out of habit.

A consequence worth noticing

Humulin R vials went in February 2026, and the Humulin Product Information prohibits intravenous use of the cartridge. Essentially every Australian intravenous insulin protocol specifies “Actrapid or Humulin R” and is made up from a vial.

Which leaves the Actrapid 10 mL vial as the only human neutral insulin vial in the country — and from January, Actrapid’s only presentation. Every intravenous insulin infusion in Australia now depends on a single product line. That is an inference from the supply position rather than a published warning, but it is worth your unit knowing, and it is worth checking what your own protocol still says.

The infusion

  • ConcentrationOne unit per millilitre is the Australian standard — 50 units in 50 mL or 100 units in 100 mL of sodium chloride 0.9%. The NSW Clinical Excellence Commission also permits 2 units/mL (100 units in 50 mL) in critical care.
  • Priming, and the number nobody agrees onNSW says prime with 10 mL. The Western Australian paediatric monographs say 20 mL, and the neonatal guideline goes further: prime, wait twenty minutes, then discard the line contents and the priming syringe. The underlying evidence is Goldberg and colleagues in 2006, who measured it: with no priming the delivered concentration was 15.8% low, at 10 mL still 6.6% low, and at 20 mL 3.4% low and no longer significantly different from maximum. The Australian 10 mL instruction leaves a measurable shortfall.
  • CompatibilityCompatible with glucose 5% and 10%, sodium chloride 0.9%, Hartmann's and Ringer's. Y-site compatible with potassium chloride, esmolol, milrinone and sodium nitroprusside. Incompatible with noradrenaline, and with dopamine, ketamine, labetalol, rocuronium, piperacillin-tazobactam, aminophylline and protamine. The noradrenaline incompatibility is the one that catches people, because the two run together so often.
  • Separate pumpsInsulin and glucose infusions belong on separate pumps. If they share one, a pump failure removes the glucose and the insulin together, or worse, only one of them.

Ketoacidosis, in adults

There is no current national Australian adult protocol. What exists is state-based, follows the United Kingdom Joint British Diabetes Societies, and varies between jurisdictions in ways worth knowing if you work across borders or read somebody else’s guideline.

Three things generalise. No bolus. Potassium before insulin, because insulin drives it intracellularly and the total body deficit is already large. And glucose is added to permit the insulin to continue, rather than the insulin being reduced to permit the glucose to rise — because it is the insulin that is switching off ketogenesis, which is the thing you are actually treating.

Euglycaemic ketoacidosis

An SGLT2 inhibitor can produce ketoacidosis at a glucose that looks reassuring. The joint Australian and New Zealand alert defines presumed ketoacidosis as ketones above 1.0 mmol/L with a base excess below −5, and presumed euglycaemic ketoacidosis if the glucose is also below 14. Glucose is added to the fluids below 15 mmol/L, or straight away. Check ketones in any unwell patient on one of these drugs, whatever the glucose says.

Hyperkalaemia

  • WhenConsider insulin and glucose at a potassium of 6 mmol/L or above.
  • WhatTen units of short-acting insulin intravenously with 25 g of glucose — 50 mL of 50%. Omit the glucose if the pre-treatment reading is above 14 mmol/L.
  • And if they start lowIf the pre-treatment glucose is below 7 mmol/L, follow the bolus with a further 25 g as an infusion — 250 mL of 10% over five hours. A pre-treatment glucose below 7 is the single strongest predictor of hypoglycaemia afterwards.
  • How much it buysA fall of 0.65 to 1.4 mmol/L, and it is a shift rather than a removal. Rebound is common, so potassium at one to two hours, again at two to four, and at twenty-four.
  • The hypoglycaemiaUp to 30% of patients, typically about two hours later, and rarely before thirty minutes or after five. Monitor glucose half-hourly for two hours and then hourly for at least six — and the clock restarts if a further dose is given.
  • The documented trapMonitoring is most often inadequate in the patients without diabetes, who are the ones at highest risk. Australian guidance also suggests considering a reduced dose of five units in severe kidney dysfunction, while noting the evidence for that is not settled.

How tightly to control it

NICE-SUGAR settled the question and is largely an Australian and New Zealand trial — 6,104 patients across 42 centres, of whom seven in eight were in this part of the world. Intensive control to 4.5–6.0 mmol/L against a conventional target of 10 or less produced 90-day mortality of 27.5% against 24.9%, odds ratio 1.14, p = 0.02 — against the tight arm. Severe hypoglycaemia ran at 6.8% against 0.5%.

TGC-Fast in 2023 asked whether the harm had been the hypoglycaemia rather than the target. It randomised 9,230 patients, used a computerised algorithm to titrate, and got severe hypoglycaemia down to 1.0% in the tight arm. There was still no benefit: no difference in time to discharge readiness and 90-day mortality of 10.5% against 10.1%. Two caveats keep it honest — its liberal arm withheld insulin until the glucose exceeded 11.9 mmol/L, which is looser than any guideline, so it does not tell you that 10 is no better than 12; and a 1% hypoglycaemia rate reflects an algorithm rather than a bedside protocol.

So the working Australian position remains the conventional arm of NICE-SUGAR, a glucose of 10 mmol/L or less with a floor set to keep clear of hypoglycaemia. International guidance has drifted slightly looser rather than tighter. Worth noting that the Australian Diabetes Society's inpatient guidance is from 2012 and explicitly excludes intensive care, so there is no current Australian ICU target to cite — which is itself a small scandal.

The errors that recur

  1. The pen and the syringe. Withdrawing insulin from a pen device with a needle and syringe is prohibited, and with a U-200 product it delivers double.
  2. The wrong cartridge in the pen. Now the live risk, as cartridges replace disposable pens. Novo cartridges fit NovoPen devices, Humulin cartridges fit HumaPen. Check the name is visible through the window before each dose.
  3. Two vials that look alike. Actrapid and Protaphane are both reverting to 10 mL vials from the same sponsor, one clear and short-acting, one cloudy and intermediate. They already share a consumer information document.
  4. Basal omitted in type 1 diabetes. Covered above, and worth two mentions.
  5. The pump disconnected first. Start the intravenous or subcutaneous insulin before the pump comes off, never after. Document the brand, the insulin, the set and the site on admission, and never override the pump's own dose-error software.
  6. The protocol nobody updated. Any local document still naming Humulin R vials, Lantus, Mixtard or Fiasp FlexTouch is describing products that no longer exist.
For the First Part

Structure. A 51-amino-acid polypeptide: an A chain of 21 and a B chain of 30, joined by two interchain disulfide bonds with a third intrachain bond within the A chain. Synthesised as preproinsulin, cleaved to proinsulin, then to insulin and C-peptide in equimolar amounts by the prohormone convertases. Be ready for the follow-up: equimolar secretion does not mean equal plasma concentrations, because insulin undergoes substantial first-pass hepatic extraction and C-peptide does not — which is precisely why C-peptide is the marker of endogenous secretion.

Dynamics. A receptor tyrosine kinase, an α2β2 heterotetramer. Autophosphorylation, then insulin receptor substrate proteins, then phosphoinositide 3-kinase, then Akt, then translocation of GLUT4 to the membrane in muscle and fat. Mention the MAPK arm in one clause so the answer is not purely PI3K. Effects: glucose uptake, glycogenesis, lipogenesis and protein synthesis; inhibition of gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, lipolysis and ketogenesis; and an intracellular potassium shift through the sodium-potassium ATPase, which is the basis of its use in hyperkalaemia.

Kinetics. Intravenous half-life four to six minutes — and the examiner's point is that the clinical effect long outlasts it, which is why an infusion is titrated and why stopping one does not restore euglycaemia immediately. Volume of distribution around 0.1–0.4 L/kg, approximating extracellular fluid. Clearance: liver 50–60%, kidney about 30%, by receptor-mediated endocytosis and then insulin-degrading enzyme; around 1% appears unchanged in urine. The first-pass figure of roughly half applies to endogenous insulin arriving by the portal vein — exogenous insulin, subcutaneous or intravenous, bypasses the portal circulation entirely, which is a favourite distinction.

And the renal question. Requirements fall in renal failure for three reasons: reduced renal clearance of insulin, loss of renal gluconeogenesis, and reduced intake. The mark is in the counterweight — uraemia also causes insulin resistance, so the net effect is that requirements become unpredictable and hypoglycaemia more likely, rather than simply lower.

One caution for candidates. The Australian Product Information for these products states that pharmacokinetic data were not assessed at registration, so it gives no half-life and no volume of distribution. The figures above come from the pharmacology literature, not from a regulatory document, and if you are asked where they come from, that is the honest answer.

Challenge for you

Go and look at your unit's insulin infusion protocol. Which insulin does it name?

If it says Humulin R, it is naming a vial that stopped being available in February. If it says Actrapid, check whether it specifies the vial, because the cartridge goes at the end of December. Tell me what yours says, and whether anybody has looked at it this year.

And a second one, for anyone who teaches: what is the insulin error you have actually seen? Not the famous ones — the specific thing that happened on your unit, and what was changed afterwards.
