ICU and You  ·  Drugstore  ·  Number 5

Heparin

Two drugs with one name, a platelet count nobody looks at twice, and the interval before a needle in the back

About this series

Drugstore is for drug profiles. Each one runs the same way: the few things that matter most, then the practical business of doses, routes and the errors that recur, and then the pharmacology proper — kinetics, dynamics, indications and adverse effects.

The intention is to serve two readers at once. The clinician who needs the drug at three in the morning, and the First Part candidate who has to explain its metabolism. Those are different needs, and most references choose one.

This one is heparin, which is not one drug. Unfractionated heparin and enoxaparin differ in how they are given, how they are monitored, how they leave the body, how well they can be reversed and how often they turn on the patient — and almost every heparin error begins by treating them as one thing with two brand names. It sits in a fortnight about late middle age because this is the band in which the first clot arrives, usually during an admission for something else.

A man of fifty-six is admitted on Monday with pneumonia and charted enoxaparin on the drug round, as everyone is. On Saturday his platelets are half what they were. Nobody has looked at them since Tuesday, and the thing that is about to happen to him is not bleeding.

The ten that matter

  1. It is two drugs with one name. Unfractionated heparin is a heterogeneous mixture of chains that inhibits thrombin and factor Xa about equally; the low molecular weight heparins are fragments of it, weighted towards factor Xa by somewhere between two and four to one depending on the agent. One is cleared by the reticuloendothelial system and the kidney together, the other essentially by the kidney. That single difference drives most of what follows.
  2. The bolus is not the one you were taught. The classic nomogram is 80 units per kilogram. The current NSW guideline caps it: 80 units/kg only if the patient is under 60 kg, and a flat 5,000 units for everybody at or above it, with the infusion starting at 18 units/kg/h and capped at 2,000 units/h. A 110 kilogram patient does not get an 8,800-unit bolus.
  3. There is a second, quieter nomogram. For acute coronary syndromes and anyone in whom bleeding must be minimised, the bolus is 60 units/kg or a flat 4,000, and the rate is 12 units/kg/h capped at 1,000 units/h — and the bolus may be omitted altogether after recent surgery or in acute stroke. Choosing the wrong table is a silent error, because both look right.
  4. The target is in seconds. NSW writes the therapeutic range as an APTT of 50 to 90 seconds, which corresponds to an anti-Xa of 0.3 to 0.7 units/mL. Ratios are a different language and the two are not interchangeable — monitor one or the other, never both.
  5. Enoxaparin is a renal drug and heparin is not. Queensland’s statewide guideline does not recommend enoxaparin at all below a creatinine clearance of 30 and sends you to an unfractionated infusion instead; between 30 and 50 it is 1 mg/kg twice daily, with caution. Older protocols and the product information instead halve the frequency to 1 mg/kg daily, so check which your hospital follows before you follow it. Either way, in the patient whose kidneys are failing and whose weight you are guessing, the infusion you can turn off in an hour is the safer drug.
  6. The platelet count is part of the prescription. At least every two days while the infusion runs. A fall of 30 to 50 per cent from baseline, five to ten days in, is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia until somebody proves otherwise.
  7. HIT is a clotting disease that happens to lower the platelet count. The danger is thrombosis, not haemorrhage, and the instinct the number provokes is the wrong one. Platelets are not transfused for HIT in the absence of bleeding.
  8. Stopping the heparin is not the treatment. Stopping it is half a treatment. The patient needs a non-heparin anticoagulant at a therapeutic dose, started at once.
  9. Protamine reverses one of them properly. One milligram per 100 units of unfractionated heparin given in the last hour, to a maximum of 50 mg, slowly. For enoxaparin it is partial at best — roughly 1 mg of protamine per 1 mg of enoxaparin, and you should expect to get some of the effect back, not all of it.
  10. It raises the potassium. Heparin suppresses adrenal aldosterone synthesis, and mild hyperkalaemia follows in something like seven per cent of exposed patients. It is dose-independent, it happens with the low molecular weight drugs too, and almost nobody goes looking for it.

Two drugs with one name

Both work the same way at the start. Neither is an anticoagulant in its own right: each binds antithrombin through a specific pentasaccharide sequence and converts it from a sluggish inhibitor into a very fast one. What differs is what the resulting complex can reach.

To inactivate thrombin, the heparin chain has to be long enough to bridge antithrombin and thrombin at the same time — about eighteen saccharide units. To inactivate factor Xa it does not; the conformational change in antithrombin is enough on its own. So the long chains in unfractionated heparin do both, in a ratio near one to one, and the shorter fragments that make up enoxaparin are weighted towards Xa. Fondaparinux is the logical end of that line — the pentasaccharide alone, pure anti-Xa activity, a half-life of seventeen to twenty-one hours and no business in anyone with a creatinine clearance below 30.

The practical consequences are entirely predictable from that paragraph, which is why it is worth knowing. Unfractionated heparin binds promiscuously to plasma proteins, endothelium and macrophages, so its dose–response is erratic and it needs monitoring. Enoxaparin binds much less, so it does not. Unfractionated heparin is short-acting and can be switched off; enoxaparin cannot. Unfractionated heparin is fully reversible; enoxaparin is not.

The infusion

  • Weigh the patientThe NSW guideline wants an actual measured weight, in kilograms, recorded. An estimate is for the unconscious intubated patient who cannot be weighed now, and they are to be weighed at the first opportunity. A dosing error here is multiplied by every hour the infusion runs.
  • Get the baseline bloods firstFull blood count, APTT, prothrombin time, electrolytes, urea, creatinine and liver function. The APTT has to be normal — 25 to 37 seconds — for APTT monitoring to mean anything; if it is not, that is a haematology conversation before it is a dosing one. In a STEMI going to the laboratory, start and chase the results.
  • Four to six hours, then dailyCheck the APTT every four to six hours until two consecutive results are in range, then once a day. Any rate change puts you back on four to six. Do not sample from the limb the infusion is running into, which is the commonest reason for a result that makes no sense.
  • Above 120 seconds is a stop, not a slow-downStop the infusion, look for bleeding, check the weight, the pump and the sampling, and tell the medical officer. Repeat at two hours. If it has come down and there is no bleeding, restart 300 units/h lower — and do not re-bolus.
  • Not therapeutic by twenty-four hours is a diagnosisNot a reason to keep climbing. The guideline calls it heparin resistance when the APTT barely moves on 35,000 units a day or more, and the move is to measure an anti-Xa rather than to keep turning the dial.
Why the two tests disagree

The APTT is a clotting time, so anything that alters the intrinsic pathway moves it independently of how much heparin is present. A lupus anticoagulant prolongs it with no anticoagulant effect at all; a high factor VIII, which is an acute phase reactant and so is high in exactly the patients who get heparin infusions, shortens it. Either can make a correctly dosed patient look wrong.

NSW names the situations in which to use anti-Xa instead: lupus anticoagulant, antiphospholipid syndrome, inherited or acquired deficiency of factors VIII, IX, XI or XII, and the patient who simply does not respond as the nomogram expects. Choose one test and stay with it, and write unfractionated heparin on the request form, because the assay is calibrated differently for the low molecular weight drugs.

Enoxaparin, and the kidney

  • Prophylaxis20 to 40 mg once daily depending on the procedure and the patient’s risk; 20 mg once daily if the creatinine clearance is under 30.
  • Treatment1 mg/kg twice daily above a clearance of 50. The 1.5 mg/kg once-daily regimen exists but Queensland does not recommend it for inpatients or for high-risk patients, cancer among them. Between 30 and 50, 1 mg/kg twice daily with caution and a repeated look at the kidneys. Below 30, not recommended — use an infusion. Round to the nearest 10 mg.
  • The large patientStart at 1 mg/kg twice daily on actual body weight. Once-daily dosing is not recommended here at all, and above a body mass index of 40, or a weight over 120 kg, dose reduction or anti-Xa monitoring may be needed — a consultant decision rather than a resident one.
  • STEMI, and the line at seventy-fiveUnder 75 with a clearance above 30: a 30 mg intravenous bolus immediately before thrombolysis, then 1 mg/kg subcutaneously twice daily, capped at 100 mg for each of the first two doses. At 75 and over: no bolus, and 0.75 mg/kg twice daily, capped at 75. The age cut-off exists because the bolus is where the intracranial haemorrhages were.
  • Use Cockcroft–Gault, not the reported eGFRThe thresholds were derived with Cockcroft–Gault, and the MDRD figure your laboratory prints beside the creatinine is a different quantity. In the lean patient of this age band the two can differ enough to change the dose.
  • Monitoring, when it is worth itNot routine. Anti-Xa is for the extremes — renal impairment, pregnancy, the very small and the very large — sampled four hours after a dose.

When the platelets fall

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is an antibody against complexes of platelet factor 4 and heparin. The antibody activates platelets, and activated platelets make thrombin. The thrombocytopenia is the by-product of a prothrombotic state, which is why the patient’s limb or lung is at more risk than their gut. It is commoner with unfractionated heparin than with the low molecular weight drugs, and only a small minority of those who form antibodies get the disease.

Score it before you phone anybody. The 4Ts runs to eight points over four domains: the depth of the fall, its timing, the presence of thrombosis, and whether anything else could explain it. Three or under is low probability, at well under one per cent; four to five is intermediate, around ten; six to eight is high, around fifty.

  • The timing is the most specific partDays five to ten, or within a day if there has been heparin in the previous thirty. A fall within the first four days in somebody with no recent exposure scores nothing, and usually is not this.
  • Take the sample at least four hours after the last dose
  • Immunoassay first, then a functional assayA positive immunoassay should be confirmed functionally — serotonin release is the reference, and multiple electrode aggregometry is the faster substitute. Treat as HIT while you wait. Ask the laboratory for the optical density and the cut-off, not a yes or a no.
  • Stop every heparinThe infusion, the prophylactic dose, the line flushes, the coated catheter. Enoxaparin is not an alternative; the antibody cross-reacts.
  • Anticoagulate, at treatment doseDanaparoid, argatroban, fondaparinux or bivalirudin. Argatroban is preferred below a clearance of 30, danaparoid avoided there. None of them is registered in Australia for this indication and argatroban comes through the Special Access Scheme, so the pharmacy conversation starts early. Queensland Health now puts rivaroxaban first for the stable patient — 15 mg twice daily — which is a larger change in practice than it looks.
Three things not to do

Do not transfuse platelets for the count alone. The guidance is explicit: not in the absence of clinical bleeding.

Do not start warfarin until the platelet count is back above 150 × 109/L. Early warfarin in HIT causes venous limb gangrene and skin necrosis, by dropping protein C before it drops anything useful. If the patient was already taking it, reverse it with vitamin K.

Do not stop at stopping. A patient taken off heparin and left on nothing has had their risk of thrombosis managed by hope.

Getting it back

Protamine is a strongly cationic protein, originally from salmon sperm, which binds the strongly anionic heparin molecule and neutralises it. The dose is not a single number, which is the part most often got wrong: it depends on how long ago the heparin was given, because you are neutralising what is left rather than what was administered.

For unfractionated heparin, against the units given in the hour before it stopped: within thirty minutes, 1 to 1.5 mg of protamine per 100 units; at thirty to sixty minutes, 0.5 to 0.75; at one to two hours, 0.375 to 0.5; and beyond two hours protamine is generally not recommended at all. Maximum 50 mg, by slow injection over at least ten minutes, then check the APTT.

For enoxaparin it is a different proposition, because protamine neutralises the anti-IIa activity well and the anti-Xa activity only partly — and the anti-Xa activity is most of what enoxaparin does. Within eight hours of the dose, 1 mg of protamine per 1 mg of enoxaparin; at eight to twelve hours, 0.5 mg per milligram; beyond twelve hours, take advice and send an anti-Xa, because poor kidneys may still be holding the drug. Expect partial reversal and plan for it.

Protamine is not a benign drug. Given quickly it causes hypotension and bradycardia, and it can produce genuine anaphylaxis — most of all in patients sensitised by protamine-containing insulin, by a previous dose, or by vasectomy. Slowly, and with the trolley in the room.

The needle in the back

The intervals are not negotiable and they are the thing most often got wrong on a Friday afternoon. For a prophylactic dose of a low molecular weight heparin: no block and no catheter removal within twelve hours of a dose. For a treatment dose: twenty-four, and a treatment dose should not be given at all while the catheter is in. The next dose comes no sooner than four hours after the block goes in or the catheter comes out. For unfractionated heparin the figures are different again: four to six hours, or a normal APTT, before the needle, and one hour afterwards with no bolus. Longer if the tap was bloody, or the patient is also on something else, and all of it assumes normal kidneys and liver.

The errors that recur

  1. The 80-units-per-kilogram bolus in a big patient. The number is capped in the current NSW guideline and the cap is the part people have not read.
  2. Treatment-dose enoxaparin at a clearance of 20. It accumulates, and the first sign is a bleed rather than a number.
  3. The APTT drawn from the infusion arm. A spuriously enormous result, an infusion stopped for no reason, and an hour of subtherapeutic anticoagulation.
  4. The platelet count that stopped being checked on day three. HIT declares between days five and ten. The admission bloods will not find it.
  5. Platelets transfused for a HIT count. Treating the number instead of the disease, in a patient whose problem is clotting.
  6. Warfarin started while the platelets are still down. This is the one that takes a limb.
  7. The epidural catheter pulled four hours after the Clexane. Twelve for a prophylactic dose, twenty-four for treatment.
For the First Part

Class and mechanism. A sulphated glycosaminoglycan, and an indirect anticoagulant — it does nothing to clotting factors by itself. It binds antithrombin through a unique pentasaccharide sequence, producing a conformational change that accelerates antithrombin’s inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa by around a thousandfold. State that first: the examiner is listening for indirect.

Why chain length decides everything. Inhibition of thrombin requires the heparin chain to bind antithrombin and thrombin simultaneously — a ternary complex, needing roughly eighteen saccharide units. Inhibition of factor Xa does not. Unfractionated heparin, with a mean molecular weight around 15,000, mostly has chains long enough for both, giving an anti-Xa to anti-IIa ratio near 1:1. Enoxaparin, mean molecular weight about 4,500, mostly does not, giving a ratio of roughly two to four to one. Fondaparinux is the pentasaccharide alone, so the ratio is infinite and the APTT is unaffected.

Kinetics, and the bit they ask. Unfractionated heparin has no oral bioavailability and is roughly 30% bioavailable subcutaneously, against about 90% for enoxaparin. It is cleared by two mechanisms: a rapid, saturable, receptor-mediated uptake into endothelium and macrophages, and a slower first-order renal route. Because the fast route saturates, the apparent half-life is dose-dependent — around 30 minutes after 25 units/kg and some 150 minutes after 400 — which is the single most examinable fact about the drug, and the reason a bolus and an infusion behave so differently. Enoxaparin is cleared renally with a half-life near four to five hours, which is why the renal threshold exists for one drug and not the other.

Heparin resistance. Worth having a structured answer. Antithrombin deficiency, inherited or acquired — consumed in sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation, lost in nephrotic syndrome, diluted on bypass. Raised factor VIII and fibrinogen, both acute phase reactants, which shorten the APTT without reducing the anticoagulant effect: a false resistance, and the reason to check an anti-Xa before escalating. Raised heparin-binding proteins. And, rarely, the patient who is simply not getting the drug.

Adverse effects, in order of how often they are forgotten. Bleeding. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, immune and prothrombotic, days five to ten. Hyperkalaemia from suppression of aldosterone synthesis, in about seven per cent, dose-independent, and present with the low molecular weight drugs too. Osteoporosis with prolonged unfractionated heparin, which is why pregnancy is a low molecular weight indication. Raised transaminases, common, benign and a reliable distractor. And skin necrosis at injection sites, which is HIT until proven otherwise.

Compare and contrast. Against warfarin: indirect but immediate, parenteral, monitored by APTT rather than INR, reversed by protamine rather than vitamin K, and safe in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta. Against the direct oral anticoagulants: no antithrombin requirement, no monitoring, no protamine. Against the direct thrombin inhibitors: bivalirudin and argatroban inhibit thrombin without antithrombin, which is exactly why they are the drugs you reach for when heparin is the problem.

Challenge for you

Go and look at the platelet counts on your unit, and find the last one that was taken.

Every patient on a heparin infusion should have had one in the last two days. My guess is that the infusions are fine and the prophylactic doses are not, because the infusion has a protocol attached to it and the prophylactic dose is just a line on the chart. Tell me whether that guess is right where you work.

And a second one, for anyone who has seen it: the HIT that was nearly missed, or the one that was called and turned out to be something else. Both are instructive, and the second is the one nobody writes up.
