The young adult

Eighteen to thirty — the most reserve you will ever treat, and the narrowest margin once it is spent

A young adult will hold a normal blood pressure through a loss that would have dropped anybody else in the unit, and then lose it all at once. Most of what goes wrong in this age group happens in that gap.

The physiology is no longer the puzzle. Airway, circulation and doses have been adult for years, and capacity is presumed rather than negotiated. What is different is the reserve, and what it hides. A normal blood pressure here describes the physiology rather than the blood volume; tachycardia is often the only thing that has moved. This is not a margin you can watch being spent. It holds, and then it does not.

The epidemiology turns outward with it. Most of what brings them in is something that happened rather than something that grew — injury, which leads every age band up to forty-four, what they took, a sepsis with no comorbidity to blame it on, a heart that stops for a reason nobody ever finds in two cases out of five. But a growing number are not new patients at all. Cystic fibrosis, muscular dystrophy, congenital heart disease and childhood cancer now deliver people to this age in numbers they never used to, carrying a paediatric history that somebody else is holding.

Four stations: what makes them different, what brings them in, the patient who is or has recently been pregnant — which an adult unit forgets — and what outlasts an admission they will carry for another fifty years.

How to use a mind map

Do not read it like a chapter. Scan it. Let your eye go where it wants, read three boxes that catch it, and stop. You are not trying to finish it and you are certainly not trying to memorise it.

Then come back. Before a shift on which this subject might come up, or after a case that went a particular way, or while waiting for a retrieval team. Thirty seconds at a time, often, beats an hour once.

It works the way a painting in a corridor works. Nobody sits down and studies the painting they walk past every morning, and yet after a year they could tell you what is in it, where the light falls, and exactly what is on the left-hand side — and they would notice at once if something had changed. That noticing is the point. These maps are not built to be recalled on demand; they are built so that when the thing in front of you does not fit, something quietly objects.

On a phone: turn it sideways, or scroll the map across. Pinch to zoom on any section.

Challenge for you

Name a situation where somebody compensated beautifully — and then suddenly did not.

It does not have to be a young patient; it is the pattern I am after. The ruptured ectopic holding a systolic of 110. The slow gastrointestinal bleed with a pulse of 95. The asthmatic whose carbon dioxide had climbed all the way back to normal. The patient who looked entirely well from the end of the bed, an hour before they did not.

Tell me the situation, and the thing that turned out to be the clue — the number you stopped trusting, the observation you now make earlier, or the moment you realised the chart had been saying so for an hour. I would like to collect these. The list of ways a person can look well while they are losing is not written down anywhere, and most experienced clinicians are carrying two or three of them.
