No. 6

Across a Sleepy Footbridge

Eighteen, a new motorbike, and two hours that have never come back

I was just 18 years old and absolutely loving the Adelaide University lifestyle. I had recently bought a small motorbike, and suddenly I was ultra-mobile, footloose and fancy-free. I didn’t have a girlfriend yet, but the prospects were looking very good. I had just received a near-perfect score in my favourite subject: human genetics. It was a fascinating topic, in an era when it was starting to look like genetics was basically the answer to everything.

Leaving the campus one Saturday afternoon, walking towards my parked motorbike, I came to the picturesque footbridge over the local river. It was a lovely bridge, right next to the University and leading into a beautiful part of the city. The bridge’s wonderful reputation had taken a hit in the recent past when a prominent lecturer had been bashed up and thrown into the sleepy Torrens, where he drowned, apparently because of his sexual orientation.

But I wasn’t thinking about that then. I was thinking about the usual things, the parties, the girls, football, Led Zeppelin and the Beatles. It was springtime, and this was essentially my springtime. My motorbike was parked on the other side of the bridge.

The next thing I knew, I was in the ward of the hospital. I had half a dozen broken bones in the face, and no memory of anything. The hour or two in between was then and remains now a total blank. That blank has been a source of great annoyance to me ever since (was there a preliminary discussion or confrontation, or was it a bolt out of the blue?). I was told and surmised that I had been punched and then kicked multiple times in the face by an assailant and/or assailants, but I simply don’t know any of the details.

I lay low at home for several weeks. The police identified an accused perpetrator. He pleaded not guilty. Outside the courtroom, my lawyer said I needed to point to the accused as the one involved. I wasn’t at all sure, as my memory files had really been wiped. At the last moment, after the accused’s lawyer saw my father and me waiting outside the courtroom, the accused changed his plea to guilty.

When I first reappeared at one of the University gatherings, the group’s unofficial leader greeted me and then said stridently to the group, “I would have fought.” It was an interesting mix of victim-shaming and self-aggrandisement, and I didn’t appreciate it at all.

Eventually I recovered from the bruises and broken bones. But the episode, of course, stayed with me throughout my life. I haven’t talked much about it. In fact, I haven’t said anything about it at all. Until now. Clearly, that episode could have been the end of my life, and I am glad it wasn’t. Undoubtedly, my assailant had taken something from me. The reasons were not clear, and possibly don’t matter.

I don’t want to play the victim or live in that role. Not long before my time, teenagers were sent overseas to receive much worse injuries, and also to die, also by persons unseen, for reasons unknown.

* * *

Violence is a part of all lives in one way or another. People say they hate it and want it totally eliminated, and yet it exists in our stories, in our past, in our consciousness. Like a lot of people, I’ve become a little more conservative as I have got older, and I do think perpetrators and potential perpetrators need to be aware of the consequences to dissuade them, and there must be punishments to follow. But more than all that, and apropos of everything we live by in our world of Intensive Care, we must search for understanding. We don’t need to beat up, whip, flog or execute those who inflict violence on others; we need to work out what is going on inside their sad and wayward heads.

Genetics taught me that this guy’s DNA was basically the same as mine, and it is true that the potential for violence may be in some way written into the genetic code. However, over time I’ve realised that it’s not all about the DNA.

If this is close to home

If reading it has brought something nearer the surface than you expected, these lines are open around the clock: Lifeline 13 11 14; 1800RESPECT 1800 737 732, for sexual, domestic and family violence; 13YARN 13 92 76, for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander readers; and MensLine 1300 789 978. A footnote…

The lecturer was Dr George Duncan. He arrived in Australia on 25 March 1972 to teach law at the University of Adelaide, and he was dead by 10 May — forty-six days in the country. He was thrown into the Torrens near that footbridge and drowned, and the rumour, then and since, was that the men who did it were vice squad officers.

Three constables refused to answer questions at the inquest. They were suspended, and they resigned. Detectives were brought out from Scotland Yard, and the crown solicitor decided not to prosecute anybody. The case was reopened in 1985; in 1986 three former officers were charged with manslaughter, two were tried, and both were acquitted. A police task force in 1990 found there was not enough evidence to charge anyone else. Nobody has ever been convicted of killing him.

Within two months of his death a private member’s bill went into the Legislative Council, and in 1975 South Australia became the first state or territory in the country to decriminalise homosexual acts. A man who had been in Australia for forty-six days changed the law of the state by being killed in it.

Challenge for you

Is there an hour of your life you cannot account for?

Mine is gone for good — an hour or two on a Saturday afternoon in Adelaide, and no amount of wanting it back has ever produced a single frame of it.

Anaesthetic, concussion, a bad fall, a seizure, a long night of grief. A great many people are missing a piece of time somewhere. I would like to know whether yours ever stopped bothering you, or whether it simply took its place among the things you have learned to carry.
